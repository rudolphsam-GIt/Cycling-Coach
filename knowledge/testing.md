# FTP and Threshold Testing (20 Minute Test, Ramp Test, Critical Power, MLSS, LTHR, Zones)

## Summary

Functional threshold power (FTP) is meant to be the highest power a rider can hold in a roughly steady state for about an hour. Most riders estimate it as 95% of 20 minute all out power, or as 75% of the best 1 minute power from a ramp test. Across groups of trained cyclists, 20 minute FTP correlates strongly with lab thresholds and the test is very repeatable (about 3% variation). For an individual, though, the error is often 5 to 10% or more, the 0.95 factor runs high for some riders (small studies disagree, with best fits from 0.88 to 0.95), and most riders cannot hold FTP for 60 minutes (typically about 35 to 50 minutes). The ramp test 75% factor has no published validation we could find.

Critical power (CP) is the best supported boundary between sustainable (heavy) and unsustainable (severe) exercise. It is estimated from 2 to 5 maximal efforts (for example 3, 7 and 12 minutes) and comes with W', the work available above CP. CP sits a little above FTP and MLSS on average and can be held for about 20 to 30 minutes. For an app, FTP is a practical zone anchor, but treat it as an estimate of about plus or minus 5%, retest under the same conditions, and adjust from the rider's response (RPE, heart rate drift, finishing sessions).

## Size of the evidence

The FTP evidence is small. No FTP meta-analysis exists. Summed by us, about 9 studies with about 220 riders and triathletes (mostly trained men, none above 49 people) compare the 20 minute test with lab thresholds. The 0.95 factor rests on 5 small, conflicting studies (about 75 riders), and how long FTP can be held on 3 small studies (at least 36 riders). The 75% ramp factor has no published validation.

Bigger pools exist next door: a reliability meta-analytic review of 101 studies, three CP vs MLSS syntheses (9, 10 and 36 studies), a test of fixed percentage zones in 100 adults, and threshold physiology in 888 athletes (393 cyclists).

The largest power datasets are real world files: 144 male professionals (129,262 files), 44 female professionals (32,028 files), a GoldenCheetah OpenData analysis of about 2,500 amateur athlete seasons (blog, not peer reviewed) and a 2025 analysis of a large power curve dataset (N not reported in abstract). They are observational and none tested FTP against lab thresholds or how long FTP can be held. We found no large peer reviewed analysis of Strava, TrainingPeaks, Zwift or intervals.icu power data.

## Key findings

### The 20 minute test with a 0.95 factor matches lab thresholds on average, but not for every individual
**Evidence: Moderate (strong correlation and wide individual error), Limited (which lab marker it matches on average)**
**Pool: 9 studies, about 220 athletes (trained male cyclists and amateur triathletes; no meta-analysis, summed by us)**
In 23 trained men, FTP20, 60 minute time trial power and the individual anaerobic threshold did not differ on average (bias 1 to 5 W), but limits of agreement reached about plus or minus 35 to 60 W (Borszcz et al., 2018). In 15 cyclists, FTP was within 1.4% of MLSS on average but with limits of plus or minus 9.2%, better in well trained than trained riders (Klitzke Borszcz et al., 2019). In 20 men, FTP matched lactate threshold in trained riders but was 6.5% lower in recreational riders (Valenzuela et al., 2018). In 46 road cyclists, FTP correlated with Dmax, modified Dmax and 4 mmol/L markers but differed from 2 mmol/L markers (Sitko et al., 2022a). In 20 competitive men, FTP matched 4 mmol/L power on average with limits of minus 45 to plus 51 W and sat above all other lactate markers (Jeffries et al., 2021). In 49 amateur triathletes, FTP correlated with the respiratory compensation point (r = 0.92) in both sexes, again with wide limits (Engelke et al., 2026). The consistent result is a strong group correlation with individual error too wide to swap FTP for a lab threshold; which marker FTP equals on average differs by study.

### The 0.95 factor can overestimate MLSS and 60 minute power
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 5 studies, about 75 athletes (trained cyclists and triathletes; results conflict)**
In 11 trained riders, 95% of 20 minute power overestimated MLSS by about 12 W and the best factor was 0.91 (Lillo-Beviá et al., 2022). In 8 trained men, 60 minute power was about 0.90 of 20 minute power (calculated from MacInnis et al., 2019). In 18 well trained men and women, MLSS was 88.5% of 20 minute power, and after 7 months of training MLSS rose 12 W while FTP did not change (Inglis et al., 2020). Against this, FTP matched 60 minute power in 23 riders (Borszcz et al., 2018) and MLSS within 1.4% in 15 (Klitzke Borszcz et al., 2019). So 3 studies (37 riders) favour about 0.88 to 0.91 and 2 studies (38 riders) support 0.95, and no large dataset settles it. We keep 0.95 as the default because the zone system is built on it, but treat it as the top of the likely range.

### Most riders cannot hold FTP for an hour
**Evidence: Moderate (that most riders fall short of 60 minutes), Limited (exact durations)**
**Pool: 3 studies, at least 36 athletes (trained to professional male cyclists; one study's N not reported in abstract)**
Time to exhaustion at FTP20 averaged 50.9 minutes (plus or minus 15.7) in 23 riders (Borszcz et al., 2018) and 33.7 minutes in 13 cyclists, with lactate rising to 6.7 mmol/L (Wong et al., 2022). It rises with fitness: median 35 minutes in recreational, 42 in trained, 47 in well trained and 51 in professional cyclists (Sitko et al., 2022b). FTP was 57 to 60 W below the respiratory compensation point in 15 cyclists (Barranco-Gil et al., 2020). Time to exhaustion at MLSS was about 38 minutes in 15 men (Fontana et al., 2009) and about 50 minutes in 21 men (Faude et al., 2017). Three independent groups agree in direction, but no large dataset on holding FTP exists; professional 60 minute bests (for example 4.2 W/kg in 44 women; Mateo-March et al., 2022) are not tests at FTP.

### The 20 minute test is reliable when the protocol is held constant
**Evidence: Strong (power tests in general are reliable), Moderate (the 20 minute FTP test specifically)**
**Pool: 101 studies (N not reported in abstract; healthy adults, athletes and non athletes); 20 minute test itself 4 studies, about 65 athletes (trained cyclists)**
With a familiarization trial, the standard protocol (about 45 minute warm up including a 5 minute effort, then 20 minutes all out) varied 2.9% test to test (intraclass correlation 0.97) in 25 trained cyclists (Klitzke Borszcz et al., 2020). Repeat tests had a smallest detectable change of about 9 W in 11 riders (Lillo-Beviá et al., 2022) and a typical error of 4.6 W in 8 (MacInnis et al., 2019). In 21 riders, 20 minute power was about 5% higher after a short or self selected warm up than after the standard one, mainly through pacing (Tramontin et al., 2022). A meta-analytic review of 101 studies found 0.9 to 2.0% variation for constant power tests of 1 minute to 3 hours in athletes, more in non athletes, and about 1.2% learning gain from the first to second trial (Hopkins et al., 2001).

### Ramp tests and the 75% factor
**Evidence: Limited (the 75% factor is convention)**
**Pool: none, coaching convention (for the 75% factor); related lab work 3 studies, about 210 athletes (mixed trained and untrained adults, triathletes)**
App ramp tests estimate FTP as 75% of the best 1 minute power in a stepwise ramp (TrainerRoad, n.d.). We found no peer reviewed validation of this factor. Ramp peak power with VO2max predicted FTP well (r = 0.93) in 40 adults (Denham et al., 2020), and peak power, body mass and 4 mmol/L power predicted FTP in 70 triathletes (McGrath et al., 2022). Peak incremental power is very reliable (about 0.9% variation; Hopkins et al., 2001), but in 100 men and women MLSS fell anywhere between 44% and 71% of ramp peak power (Iannetta et al., 2020). That ramp differs from app ramps, so the numbers do not map onto the 75% factor, but the spread shows one factor will misplace many riders. Riders with high anaerobic capacity likely get a ramp FTP that is too high and very aerobic riders one that is too low; this is reasoning, not a tested finding.

### Critical power and W' describe the power duration relationship
**Evidence: Strong (concept), Moderate (field estimation)**
**Pool: 36 studies (N not reported in abstract; cyclical exercise, CP vs MLSS); field CP 5 studies, about 57 athletes plus one study with N not reported in abstract (active adults to professional cyclists)**
**Real world data: 27 athletes, 3,336 training and race files from male professional cyclists, observational**
**Real world data: 188 athletes, record power profiles of 144 male (129,262 files) and 44 female (32,028 files) professional cyclists, observational**
For efforts of about 2 to 30 minutes, time to exhaustion falls on a hyperbolic curve described by CP (the highest power sustainable without steadily using up a limited reserve) and W' (the size of that reserve, in kJ); time to exhaustion = W' / (power minus CP) (Jones et al., 2010). CP separates the heavy domain, where oxygen uptake and lactate can stabilize, from the severe domain, where they cannot and VO2max is reached (Poole et al., 2016). Jones et al. (2019) argue CP, not MLSS, is the true maximal metabolic steady state; MLSS as normally measured falls somewhat below CP, and exercise at CP is sustainable for only about 20 to 30 minutes.

A 3 minute all out test gave end power within about 5 W of CP in 8 of 10 active people (Vanhatalo et al., 2007), but overestimated CP by about 50 W in 6 elite track cyclists (Bartram et al., 2017). Field CP from 3, 7 and 12 minute efforts agreed with lab CP (Karsten et al., 2014) and was reliable (variation 2.4 to 6.5%); CP from best training and racing powers had a 5.2% standard error (Karsten et al., 2015). In 27 professionals, CP and best 5 and 20 minute powers from training plus racing files matched formal time trials in the competitive period (error below 5%), but training files alone ran low (Pallarés et al., 2022). Large professional databases show power curves differ by rider type, with sprinters highest at 5 to 30 seconds and time trialists or climbers highest at 10 to 60 minutes (Valenzuela et al., 2022; Mateo-March et al., 2022). A functional analysis of a large power curve dataset found 3 shape components (overall level, sprint versus endurance bias, a W' like component) explain 97% of differences between riders (Puchowicz & Skiba, 2025). The hyperbola fits best from about 2 to 15 minutes; a power law may fit shorter and longer efforts better (Drake et al., 2024). Leo et al. (2022) recommend multi effort CP testing with set rules rather than single effort predictions such as FTP.

### How FTP, MLSS and CP relate
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 9 studies, about 104 athletes (CP vs thresholds, mixed trained adults); also 10 and 36 study syntheses (N not reported in abstract)**
On average MLSS sits at or slightly below FTP and CP a little above it. CP exceeded FTP by 7 W in 17 trained cyclists, with individual limits of minus 19 to plus 33 W (Karsten et al., 2020). MLSS is the highest constant power where lactate rises no more than 1 mmol/L between minutes 10 and 30 (Beneke, 2003); time to exhaustion there is about 38 to 50 minutes and poorly repeatable (variation 24.6%) (Fontana et al., 2009; Faude et al., 2017). Three syntheses agree CP sits above MLSS: by about 11% in 9 studies with 104 participants (Galán-Rioja et al., 2020), by about 12 W with limits of minus 19 to plus 44 W in 10 studies (Micheli et al., 2025), and by an amount that depends on test design in 36 studies (Borszcz et al., 2024). None can be swapped for another in an individual; all describe a zone near the top of steady state.

### Zone systems
**Evidence: Moderate (3 zone physiology), Limited (7 zone percentages are convention)**
**Pool: 3 studies, about 1,040 athletes (runners, cyclists and active adults, recreational to world class), plus descriptive elite training data**
Descriptive studies of elite endurance athletes show about 80% of sessions below the first threshold and about 20% at high intensity, which underlies the 3 zone model (Seiler, 2010). Fixed percentages of maximal values do not put different people in the same domain; the first lactate or ventilatory threshold marks the moderate to heavy boundary and CP the heavy to severe boundary (Jamnick et al., 2020). In 100 adults, fixed percentages of VO2max, peak power or maximal heart rate placed people in different domains (Iannetta et al., 2020), and in 50 cyclists zone 2 markers varied 6 to 29% between riders (Meixner et al., 2025). The Coggan 7 zone system (zone 1 below 55% FTP, zone 2 56 to 75%, zone 3 76 to 90%, zone 4 91 to 105%, zone 5 106 to 120%, zone 6 121 to 150%, zone 7 maximal sprint) comes from coaching practice, not validation. In 393 cyclists (part of 888 athletes), average power was 190 W at the first lactate threshold and 240 W at the lactate turnpoint (Mougin et al., 2026). If FTP sits near the turnpoint, as several FTP studies suggest, the first threshold is about 79% of FTP on average; this is our inference.

### Lactate threshold heart rate (LTHR)
**Evidence: Limited (convention)**
**Pool: 1 small study, 15 athletes (trained cyclists) for heart rate at FTP vs MLSS; the 30 minute method itself has none, coaching convention**
A common field method from coaching books takes the average heart rate of the last 20 minutes of a solo 30 minute all out effort as LTHR. Heart rate at FTP matched heart rate at MLSS within about 1.4% on average, but with individual limits of agreement of plus or minus 8.2% (Klitzke Borszcz et al., 2019). Heart rate at a fixed power rises with heat, dehydration, fatigue and duration, so LTHR is a useful cross check rather than a precise anchor.

### How often to retest
**Evidence: Limited (convention)**
**Pool: none, coaching convention**
Retesting every 4 to 8 weeks, or at the start and end of each training block, is coaching convention. The reliability data give a useful filter: a change smaller than about 3% (or about 9 W for a 300 W rider) between two 20 minute tests is within normal test to test noise (Klitzke Borszcz et al., 2020; Lillo-Beviá et al., 2022).

## Rules for the coach

- Treat any FTP as an estimate with about plus or minus 5% uncertainty. Show it as a single number for zones, but describe threshold work as a range (95 to 105% FTP).
- 20 minute test protocol: use the same warm up every time (either the standard one with a 5 minute hard effort or a short one, never switching). Then 20 minutes all out, evenly paced, on the same trainer or same road. FTP = 0.95 x average 20 minute power. Treat 0.95 as the top of the likely range (0.88 to 0.95); for long steady efforts near threshold, plan around 90 to 95% of 20 minute power.
- For riders who are new to testing, count the first test as familiarization; expect about 1 to 2% improvement on the second test from learning alone.
- If a rider's FTP came from a 20 minute test and they fail to complete 2 x 20 minutes at 95 to 100% FTP at RPE 7 to 8 out of 10, or blow up during threshold sessions repeatedly, lower FTP by 3 to 5% (factor 0.90 to 0.92 instead of 0.95).
- Recreational riders (roughly below 4.5 W/kg at ramp peak) may have an FTP from the 20 minute test that is slightly low relative to lab threshold; if threshold sessions feel easy (RPE 5 or less out of 10 at 100% FTP), raise FTP by 2 to 3%.
- Ramp test: FTP = 0.75 x best 1 minute power, as a convention. Flag riders with a strong sprint or short power profile (ramp FTP may be too high) and very aerobic, time trial riders (ramp FTP may be too low). For these riders, confirm with a 20 minute test or a CP test within 2 weeks.
- Do not compare FTP from a ramp test with FTP from a 20 minute test as if they were the same measurement. Track each test type separately.
- Do not assume FTP can be held for 60 minutes. Expect 35 to 50 minutes at 100% FTP, longer in more trained riders. For 40 km time trials or hour efforts, target about 95 to 100% FTP depending on fitness and duration.
- Critical power option: ride maximal efforts of 3, 7 and 12 minutes (30 minutes easy recovery between them on one day, or on separate days), then fit CP and W'. Use this when a rider wants W' for pacing or when FTP seems off.
- Do not use the 3 minute all out test for well trained or elite riders; it can overestimate CP by a large amount.
- Estimating from ride data: use true maximal efforts from the last 4 to 6 weeks, preferring files that include races or hard group rides (training files alone ran low in professionals). Use best 3, 7 and 12 minute powers for CP, or best 20 minute power x 0.95 for FTP, only for efforts the rider confirms were all out, and flag lower confidence.
- Retest every 4 to 8 weeks, at the end of a build block, after an easy (recovery) week, and after any break of 2 weeks or more. Test when rested (no hard session in the previous 48 hours).
- Only change zones when a new test differs from the old one by 3% or more. Smaller changes are within test noise unless supported by session data.
- If session data show the rider repeatedly completing threshold intervals at RPE 6 or less with stable heart rate, suggest a retest early rather than guessing a new FTP.
- LTHR: set from a solo 30 minute all out effort (average heart rate of the final 20 minutes). Use it as a cross check and for riders without a power meter. Heart rate in hot conditions, when dehydrated or late in long rides runs higher at the same power, so do not lower power targets just because heart rate is above zone in those conditions.
- Default zone system for the app: Coggan 7 zones by % FTP (zone 1 below 55%, zone 2 56 to 75%, zone 3 76 to 90%, zone 4 91 to 105%, zone 5 106 to 120%, zone 6 121 to 150%, zone 7 maximal). For intensity distribution planning, map to 3 zones: low (below about 75% FTP, the approximate first threshold), moderate (about 76 to 100% FTP), high (above 100% FTP). The 75% boundary is approximate; if lab or lactate data give the first threshold, use that instead.
- Always label zones as based on FTP from a specific test type and date, so old or mixed values are visible.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed

- The 0.95 factor is an average. Three small studies (37 riders) found 0.88 to 0.91 fits better, two (38 riders) support 0.95. Individual ratios were not reported in the abstracts reviewed, and no large dataset exists.
- Whether FTP is a true physiological threshold is disputed. Some researchers argue it sits below the heavy to severe boundary and should not be treated as one (Wong et al., 2022; Leo et al., 2022); others find it a practical stand in for MLSS in trained riders (Klitzke Borszcz et al., 2019).
- The ramp test 75% factor has no published validation that we could find; it comes from app documentation.
- Whether CP or MLSS is the true maximal metabolic steady state is an ongoing debate (Jones et al., 2019).
- Most validation samples are small (8 to 49 people, about 220 in total across validation studies), mostly male, and mostly trained rather than recreational or elite. Data in women and masters riders are scarce.
- The 4 to 8 week retest interval and the 30 minute LTHR method are convention without direct testing.
- The 7 zone % FTP boundaries are not validated; they do not guarantee the same physiological domain for every rider.
- Large real world power datasets (professional teams, GoldenCheetah OpenData) describe power curves but have not been used to test FTP, the 0.95 factor or time to exhaustion. They are observational.
- Field CP estimates depend on efforts being truly maximal, on recovery between efforts, and on terrain; W' estimates are much less precise than CP.
