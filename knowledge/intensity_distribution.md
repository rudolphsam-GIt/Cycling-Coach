# Training Intensity Distribution (Polarized, Pyramidal, Threshold, Sweet Spot)

## Summary

Training intensity distribution (TID) means how a rider's weekly training time is split across easy, moderate and hard efforts. Research usually uses three zones. Zone 1 is easy riding below the first lactate or ventilatory threshold (you can talk in full sentences). Zone 2 is the band between the first and second thresholds (steady, "tempo" to "threshold" riding, where sweet spot sits). Zone 3 is above the second threshold (hard intervals). A **polarized** plan puts most time in zone 1, a meaningful slice in zone 3, and very little in zone 2. A **pyramidal** plan also puts most time in zone 1, then less in zone 2, then the least in zone 3. A **threshold** plan puts a large share of time in zone 2.

The short version of the evidence is this. Elite endurance athletes almost always do the large majority of their training easy, and most of them, including WorldTour cyclists, train in a pyramidal pattern. Early small trials suggested polarized was clearly best and that threshold heavy plans were worse. Larger and newer pooled analyses (2024 to 2026) find polarized and pyramidal give about the same time trial gains, and no model, threshold included, is clearly better than another. Polarized may be a bit better for VO2max in competitive athletes and pyramidal a bit better for recreational athletes. Training logs from more than 100,000 marathon runners show faster runners mainly do more easy volume, and long training breaks are linked to slower races. These are associations, not proof of cause. For an amateur with limited hours, the most important things are riding consistently, keeping most riding genuinely easy, and doing about two well executed hard sessions per week. The exact split matters much less than total volume and consistency.

## Size of the evidence

The trial evidence is modest. The largest pooled analyses combine 13 to 17 small studies with about 350 to 440 athletes, mostly young men, mostly runners and mixed endurance athletes, mostly lasting 6 to 16 weeks. Only about 220 athletes contribute to the time trial result, and few trials test threshold heavy plans.

The biggest datasets are observational. Training logs from 119,452 marathon runners (2025) and wearable data from about 14,000 runners (2020) link more easy volume to faster racing. A 2022 analysis of about 300,000 recreational marathon runners links training breaks of a week or more to 5 to 8% slower times. A 2020 meta-regression of 85 studies links higher volume to faster marathons. All of these are runners, and all show associations, not cause and effect: faster athletes may train more because they are more talented or motivated.

For cyclists the evidence is small. Professional cycling data come from groups of about 30 riders, the only cycling systematic review of periodization and intensity distribution found 7 studies and no clear winner over 8 to 12 weeks (Galán-Rioja et al., 2023), and no peer reviewed big data analysis of amateur cyclists' intensity distribution against performance (from platforms such as Strava, TrainingPeaks, Garmin, Zwift, intervals.icu or Golden Cheetah) was found in this review. Women, masters riders and riders under 5 hours per week are poorly studied.

## Key findings

### Elite endurance athletes do most of their training at low intensity
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: about 43 studies in 4 systematic or scoping reviews, mostly descriptive, plus 2 professional cycling datasets, at least about 250 athletes where N is reported (elite runners, rowers, skiers, cyclists and other endurance athletes)**
**Real world data: 119,452 athletes, marathon runners' training logs (Muniz-Pumares et al., 2025), observational**
Observational work in well trained athletes shows a consistent pattern. In 11 junior cross country skiers, about 75% of endurance sessions were in zone 1, 8% in zone 2 and 17% in zone 3, and the split was similar whether measured by heart rate or session RPE (Seiler & Kjerland, 2006). A review of descriptive studies in athletes training 10 to 13 times per week found roughly 80% of sessions at low intensity and about 20% containing high intensity work (Seiler, 2010). Later reviews found that most retrospective studies actually show a pyramidal pattern, with some world class athletes shifting to polarized in certain phases of the season (Stöggl & Sperlich, 2015). Systematic reviews of elite distance runners (10 studies) and elite rowers (9 studies, 82 rowers) found pyramidal was the most common pattern, often shifting toward polarized in the competition phase (Casado et al., 2022; Zhong et al., 2025). More than 90% of 32 WorldTour cyclists trained pyramidal before their first race (Mateo-March et al., 2026), and 4 years of data from 30 professional riders showed most time in low zones, with women doing less volume at higher relative intensity (van Erp et al., 2020). In 119,452 marathon runners, faster runners did more than three times the volume of slower runners, nearly all of the extra time in zone 1, and pyramidal was the pattern of more than 80% of the fastest group (Muniz-Pumares et al., 2025). Long term data are thin: a scoping review found only 17 studies with 109 athletes (Staff et al., 2023). This is descriptive data. It shows what successful athletes do, not that it is optimal for everyone, so the grade stays Moderate despite the large running dataset.

### Easy base plus hard work versus a threshold heavy plan
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 3 studies, N not reported in abstract (endurance trained athletes; the only polarized versus threshold meta-analysis)**
**Real world data: 119,452 athletes, marathon runners' training logs (Muniz-Pumares et al., 2025), observational**
In 12 trained male cyclists in a crossover design, 6 weeks of polarized training (about 6.4 h per week; 80/0/20) gave larger gains than threshold training (about 7.5 h per week; 57/43/0) in peak power (8% vs 3%) and lactate threshold (9% vs 2%), with less weekly time. Both improved 40 km time trial performance (Neal et al., 2013). In 48 well trained runners, cyclists, triathletes and skiers over 9 weeks, polarized training produced the largest gains in VO2peak (+11.7%), ramp time to exhaustion and peak power, while threshold and high volume training produced little further change (Stöggl & Sperlich, 2014). In subelite runners, more zone 1 (about 80%) beat more zone 2 for a 10.4 km race when zone 3 was held equal (Esteve-Lanao et al., 2007). A 2019 meta-analysis of three RCTs favored polarized over threshold for time trial performance, but the trials were few and of modest quality (PEDro 4 to 5 out of 10) (Rosenblat et al., 2019). Reviews of runners concluded that pyramidal and polarized plans, or plans with at least 70% low intensity, gave better results than threshold plans (Kenneally et al., 2018; Campos et al., 2022).

The larger pooled analyses do not confirm a clear threshold penalty. The 2025 individual participant analysis found no significant difference between polarized and any other model (Rosenblat et al., 2025), and a 2026 Bayesian network meta-analysis of RCTs found no model definitely better than polarized; it even ranked threshold training as the most likely best option for VO2max, although all intervals crossed zero (Li et al., 2026). In the marathon dataset, a higher share of zone 2 and 3 went with slower times, but absolute minutes in zones 2 and 3 were similar across levels; faster runners simply added far more zone 1 (Muniz-Pumares et al., 2025). That association is tangled up with volume. **Grade change: Moderate to Limited.** The early advantage over threshold heavy plans rests on a few small trials and is not confirmed by larger pooled analyses. Easy base training stays the default because successful athletes do it and it lets volume grow, not because threshold work is proven harmful.

### Polarized and pyramidal give similar time trial gains
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 17 studies, about 437 athletes (trained endurance athletes, mixed sports; 221 contribute to the time trial result)**
**Real world data: 119,452 athletes, marathon runners' training logs (Muniz-Pumares et al., 2025), observational (pyramidal most common)**
A 2024 meta-analysis of 17 studies (437 athletes) found polarized was slightly better for VO2peak (SMD 0.24), but only in interventions shorter than 12 weeks and in highly trained athletes. For time trial performance, time to exhaustion and power or speed at the second threshold, polarized was no better than other distributions (time trial SMD -0.01) (Silva Oliveira et al., 2024). A 2025 network meta-analysis using individual participant data from 13 studies (348 athletes, 296 men and 52 women) found no difference between polarized and pyramidal for VO2max or time trial performance, and no significant difference between polarized and any other model (Rosenblat et al., 2025). A 2026 Bayesian network meta-analysis of RCTs also found no model clearly better than polarized (Li et al., 2026; number of studies and athletes not reported in abstract). In national elite rowers, polarized was not better than pyramidal (Treff et al., 2017). In 60 well trained runners over 16 weeks, 8 weeks of pyramidal then 8 weeks of polarized gave the best 5 km result (about 1.5% faster) (Filipas et al., 2022). The 2022 published debate between leading researchers shows the field has not reached agreement on whether polarized is "optimal" (Foster et al., 2022; Burnley et al., 2022). The pool is several hundred athletes with consistent results, but the trials are small and short, so "no difference" partly reflects limited power. That keeps the grade at Moderate, not Strong.

### Competitive athletes may respond better to polarized, recreational athletes to pyramidal
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 13 studies, about 348 athletes (198 competitive and 150 recreational endurance athletes, one subgroup analysis)**
In the 2025 individual participant network meta-analysis, a subgroup analysis found competitive athletes tended to improve VO2max more with polarized, while recreational athletes tended to improve more with pyramidal (Rosenblat et al., 2025). This is one subgroup finding, not tested in a trial designed for it. The 2024 meta-analysis similarly found the polarized advantage for VO2peak only in highly trained athletes (125 athletes in that subgroup) (Silva Oliveira et al., 2024), and the 2026 network meta-analysis listed training status as a possible moderator (Li et al., 2026).

### In recreational athletes most models work, and moderate intensity can save time
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 3 studies, about 83 athletes (recreational runners and moderately trained triathletes)**
In 30 recreational runners over 10 weeks, polarized (about 77/3/20) and a between thresholds plan (about 46/35/19) improved 10 km time by 5.0% and 3.6%, not significantly different (Muñoz et al., 2014). In 38 recreational runners over 8 weeks with load matched by TRIMP, a polarized plan (77/3/20) and a moderate focused plan (40/50/10) gave similar gains, but the focused plan took about 17% less total time (29.9 h vs 24.8 h) (Festa et al., 2019). In 15 moderately trained triathletes over 6 weeks, polarized and moderate distributions gave similar results (Röhrken et al., 2020). Samples are small, but when time is the main limit, some zone 2 work looks a reasonable way to get load in.

### Total volume and consistency matter at least as much as the split
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 85 studies (137 groups of runners), N not reported in abstract (elite and recreational marathon runners, observational and interventional)**
**Real world data: about 300,000 athletes, recreational marathon runners' training logs (Feely et al., 2022), observational; also 119,452 runners (Muniz-Pumares et al., 2025) and about 14,000 runners from wearables (Emig & Peltonen, 2020)**
Several of the trials above held total load or time roughly constant, which means TID was tested on top of a fixed volume. When load is matched, differences between models are small (Festa et al., 2019; Rosenblat et al., 2025). Elite athletes reach their 80/20 type split at very high volumes, 10 to 13 sessions per week (Seiler, 2010). Large datasets point the same way. A meta-regression of 85 studies found weekly distance, number of runs, long runs and hours per week were all linked to faster marathon times (Doherty et al., 2020). In 119,452 runners, markers of volume and the share of time in zone 1 were strongly linked to faster times (Muniz-Pumares et al., 2025). In about 14,000 runners, faster runners trained at lower relative intensity (Emig & Peltonen, 2020). In about 300,000 runners, comparing the same runners across races, a break of 7 days or more in the 16 weeks before a marathon was linked to a 5 to 8% slower finish than a race with only short breaks (Feely et al., 2022). In 28 cyclists, weekend only riders had lower VO2max than those riding 3 or more days per week (36.7 vs 48.9 mL/kg/min), a small cross sectional comparison (González et al., 2026). **Grade change: Limited to Moderate.** The reason is consistency across several independent large datasets, a meta-regression and one within-runner comparison, not the size of any single dataset. It is not Strong because it is all observational, the large data are all runners, and talented athletes may simply train more. There is still no direct trial testing what split is best at 4 to 6 hours per week in cyclists, so applying elite ratios to low volume riders is an extrapolation.

### How you count the zones changes the answer
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 2 studies, about 26 athletes (15 trained road cyclists, 11 junior cross country skiers)**
Different quantification methods (heart rate, session RPE, lactate, pace, power) can classify the same training differently (Campos et al., 2022). In 11 junior skiers heart rate and session RPE gave very similar splits (Seiler & Kjerland, 2006). In 15 road cyclists over 10 weeks, however, the same training came out as 45/30/25 by session RPE, 87/9/4 by heart rate and 80/9/12 by power (Sanders et al., 2017). So in cyclists, session RPE classified far more time as moderate or hard than heart rate or power did. Studies also count differently; Treff et al. (2017) counted zone 3 as interval duration but zones 1 and 2 as time in heart rate zone. In a survey of 778 practitioners, cyclists set the lowest heart rate limits for zones 1 to 3 (Seiler-Viken et al., 2025). A simple polarization index (log10 of zone 1 times zone 3 times 100 divided by zone 2, with zone values as percentages) above 2.0 identifies a polarized week (Treff et al., 2019). **Grade change: Moderate to Limited.** The direct comparisons are two small studies that disagree on whether RPE matches heart rate.

### Sweet spot training
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 0 studies of sweet spot by name; indirect evidence from 3 studies, about 98 athletes (12 trained cyclists, 48 mixed endurance athletes, 38 recreational runners)**
"Sweet spot" (roughly 88 to 94% FTP) is a popular coaching term for the upper part of zone 2. A search of Europe PMC for peer reviewed trials on "sweet spot" cycling training found none. The closest evidence is threshold and between thresholds groups in the trials above, which did not beat easy base plus hard work models in trained athletes (Neal et al., 2013; Stöggl & Sperlich, 2014), but performed about as well in recreational runners while saving time (Festa et al., 2019). Pooled analyses did not find threshold models clearly worse than polarized (Rosenblat et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). Treat sweet spot as a reasonable, time efficient zone 2 tool, not a proven superior method.

## Rules for the coach

Zone mapping used below is a common practical approximation, not a value taken from the cited studies. Research zones were set from lab thresholds, and FTP only approximates the second threshold. Zone 1 is below about 75% FTP (RPE 1 to 4 out of 10). Zone 2 is about 76 to 100% FTP (RPE 5 to 7), with sweet spot at 88 to 94% FTP (RPE 6 to 7). Zone 3 is above 100% FTP (RPE 8 to 10). Anchor zones to a measured threshold such as FTP rather than a fixed % of max heart rate; in pooled data from 1,544 people, threshold based prescription gave larger VO2max gains (Meyler et al., 2025, mixed populations).

- Suggestion, not a rule (the rider decides): a low intensity base of about 70% or more of weekly riding time in zone 1, and 75 to 85% when hours allow, is a common pattern in successful athletes. Trials do not show a lower share is worse, so offer it with the trade off and accept the rider's choice. Sources for the pattern (Campos et al., 2022; Seiler, 2010; Muniz-Pumares et al., 2025).
- Count intensity by session, not just minutes. A week of 3 to 5 rides should usually hold no more than 2 hard sessions (zone 2 or zone 3 intervals). The rest are easy.
- Suggestion: easy rides work best kept under about 75% FTP and RPE 3 to 4, so the hard days stay strong. If the rider prefers endurance rides near the top of zone 1 or into tempo, respect it and watch for fading quality on hard days.
- Do not force a choice between polarized and pyramidal. Either is acceptable (Rosenblat et al., 2025; Silva Oliveira et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026). Use these defaults.
  - Recreational rider, under 8 h per week, or less than 2 years of structured training. Use pyramidal. Example week is 1 sweet spot or threshold session plus 1 VO2max session plus easy rides.
  - Competitive rider, 8 or more h per week, VO2max likely above about 55 mL/kg/min. Polarized is reasonable, especially in a short block of 6 to 12 weeks targeting VO2max. Example is 2 zone 3 sessions plus easy volume, minimal zone 2. Pyramidal is equally fine; most professionals use it (Mateo-March et al., 2026).
- Do not call threshold or sweet spot work harmful or inferior; pooled evidence does not show that. Limit it to keep easy days easy and leave room for volume.
- Periodize when there is a goal event. Use pyramidal in the base and build phase, then shift toward polarized for the final 6 to 8 weeks before the event (Filipas et al., 2022, runners; Casado et al., 2022 and Zhong et al., 2025, descriptive data in elite runners and rowers).
- Adjustments by hours available.
  - 3 to 5 h per week. 2 quality sessions of 45 to 75 min plus 1 to 2 easy rides. A higher zone 2 share (up to about 30 to 40% of time) is acceptable because it adds load in less time (Festa et al., 2019; Muñoz et al., 2014). Still keep at least 1 genuinely easy ride.
  - 6 to 10 h per week. 2 quality sessions plus 3 to 4 easy rides, including 1 long ride of 2 to 4 h. About 75 to 85% zone 1 time is a common pattern, offered as a suggestion.
  - Over 10 h per week. 2, at most 3, quality sessions. Add volume to zone 1 rather than adding hard sessions (Seiler, 2010; Muniz-Pumares et al., 2025).
- Volume first. When a rider can add time, add it as zone 1 riding before adding intensity. When a rider loses time, protect the 2 quality sessions and cut easy volume first.
- Consistency over perfection. Spread riding over at least 3 days per week rather than cramming it into weekends (González et al., 2026), and when life gets busy, keep short rides going rather than stopping completely; breaks of 7 days or more were linked to 5 to 8% slower race times in runners (Feely et al., 2022). Present this observational data as a likely benefit, not a guarantee.
- Use the best intensity measure available. Classify zones by power or heart rate when the rider has them. In cyclists, session RPE puts far more time into zones 2 and 3 than power or heart rate do (Sanders et al., 2017), so do not judge the split from RPE alone. Use RPE as a check against the rider's own normal: hard sessions should feel 7 to 9 out of 10 and easy rides 2 to 4. If a rider's power or heart rate shows most rides in zone 2, they are drifting into a threshold heavy pattern.
- Track the polarization index if zone data is available (above 2.0 is polarized; Treff et al., 2019). Use it to spot drift, not as a target.
- Do not promise specific gains from a TID change. Expected differences between models are small compared with the effect of training consistently.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed

- Whether polarized is superior is still debated (Foster et al., 2022; Burnley et al., 2022). Early positive trials were small, short and often unblinded.
- The early finding that threshold heavy plans do worse is not confirmed by the larger pooled analyses (Rosenblat et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026).
- Most trials last 6 to 16 weeks. There is almost no season long or multi year data.
- Sample sizes are small, usually 10 to 60 athletes, and mostly young men. The largest pooled trial dataset had 52 women out of 348 athletes (Rosenblat et al., 2025). Data on masters riders over 50 is very limited.
- The recreational versus competitive split in response (Rosenblat et al., 2025) is a subgroup finding, not a tested rule.
- The largest datasets (100,000 or more athletes) are observational and all in marathon runners. They show what faster runners do, not what would make a given rider faster. No equivalent peer reviewed analysis of amateur cyclists' platform data was found.
- Several key studies are in runners, rowers or mixed sports, not cyclists.
- No peer reviewed trial was found that tests "sweet spot" training as a named method.
- Different studies define zones and count time differently, which makes "80/20" mean different things across papers.
- There is no direct trial of TID in cyclists training under 5 h per week.
