# Fueling, Hydration and Sports Nutrition for Cyclists

## Summary
Carbohydrate is the main fuel for hard and long cycling, and the amount a rider needs scales with how much and how hard they train. The major position stands agree on daily targets from about 3 to 5 g per kg body mass on light days up to 8 to 12 g/kg on very heavy days, and on ride fueling that rises from almost nothing for short rides to 30 to 60 g per hour for most endurance rides and up to about 90 g per hour for rides longer than about 2.5 hours, using glucose plus fructose blends. Protein needs for athletes are about 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg per day, spread across meals, with roughly 0.3 g/kg (about 20 to 25 g) after key sessions.

"Train low" sessions can boost muscle signaling, but pooled trials show no clear performance gain. Ketogenic or low carbohydrate high fat (LCHF) diets show no benefit and impaired economy in elite athletes. Hydration centers on knowing your sweat rate and avoiding both large deficits and drinking more than you sweat. Caffeine at 3 to 6 mg/kg is one of the few supplements with strong evidence. Nitrate (beetroot) helps recreational athletes a little but shows little effect in highly trained riders. Chronic under fueling (low energy availability) harms health and performance and is a medical issue, not a coaching one.

## Size of the evidence
Single nutrition trials are usually small (often 8 to 20 trained men), but several findings rest on large pools: carbohydrate during exercise (136 studies), caffeine (21 meta-analyses), nitrate (73 studies, 1,061 people), protein (1,863 people for resistance training, about 1,150 in endurance pools) and energy availability (1,006 athletes). The biggest real world datasets are 2,135 weighed athletes for hyponatremia, 1,303 athletes for sweat rate and 1,281 runners for gut complaints; these show associations, not cause and effect. The pool is still small for gut training (one trial), the per meal protein dose (six men), glycogen refueling (246 people), dehydration and time trials (122 people), women, older riders and intakes above 90 g/h. No training platform or wearable fueling datasets were found.

## Key findings

### Daily carbohydrate should match the training load
**Evidence: Moderate (expert consensus; pooled data show muscle glycogen tracks intake, but no large trials test the daily bands against performance)**
**Pool: 63 trials, about 641 people (recreational and endurance trained adults, muscle glycogen outcomes)**
The 2016 joint position stand sets daily carbohydrate by load: light 3 to 5 g/kg; about 1 h per day 5 to 7 g/kg; 1 to 3 h per day 6 to 10 g/kg; 4 to 5 h per day 8 to 12 g/kg (Thomas et al., 2016), framed around whether stores are adequate for the day's work (Burke et al., 2011). A 2026 meta-analysis found muscle glycogen rose with carbohydrate intake, with intakes above about 6.5 g/kg/day giving clearly higher stores than lower intakes, though individual responses varied widely (Jones et al., 2026). The grade was lowered from Strong because the large pool measures glycogen, not performance.

### Carbohydrate during rides should scale with duration
**Evidence: Strong**
**Pool: 136 studies, N not reported in abstract (mostly trained men, many cycling time trials and time to exhaustion tests)**
**Real world data: 221 people, in-race intake survey across Ironman, marathon, 100 to 150 km cycle races and pro stage racing, observational**
Position stands give no carbohydrate under about 45 minutes, a little or a mouth rinse for hard 45 to 75 minute efforts, 30 to 60 g/h for 1 to 2.5 hours and up to 90 g/h beyond (Thomas et al., 2016). Glucose alone is oxidized at up to about 60 g/h, so about 90 g/h needs glucose plus fructose, and less is needed at low intensity (Jeukendrup, 2014). A meta-analysis of 136 studies found a clear benefit of carbohydrate over placebo, larger in longer events, in time to exhaustion than time trials, and in less trained people; in that pool, dose and carbohydrate type did not change the size of the benefit (Ramos-Campo et al., 2024). A review of 61 studies found 82% showed a benefit (Stellingwerff & Cox, 2014), and 24 competition style trials in mostly male cyclists found benefit beyond 90 minutes (Pöchmüller et al., 2016). In 14 studies of 2.5 to 3 h exercise, mostly men cycling, glucose plus fructose at about 80 to 140 g/h raised power by about 1 to 9% over glucose alone (Rowlands et al., 2015). In 51 cyclists, gains rose up to about 78 g/h (Smith et al., 2013). In races, intake ranged from 6 to 136 g/h; higher intake was linked with more nausea but faster Ironman times (Pfeiffer et al., 2012).

### The gut is trainable
**Evidence: Limited (one small controlled trial and mechanistic work; gut distress itself is well documented)**
**Pool: one small trial (Cox et al., 2010) plus narrative reviews; no meta-analysis of gut training found**
**Real world data: 1,281 runners, internet survey after a long distance run; 805 recreational runners in China; 247 riders at the Mallorca 312 cycling event; all observational**
A high carbohydrate diet increases intestinal glucose transporters (SGLT1), supporting absorption (Jeukendrup, 2017), and 28 days of high carbohydrate training increased oxidation of ingested glucose in cyclists (Cox et al., 2010). The best protocol is not established. Gut trouble is common: 45% of runners had a complaint and 11% serious ones, more often if not used to drinking on the run (ter Steege et al., 2008); 26% of Chinese runners had race symptoms (Zhao et al., 2025); 38% of amateur cyclists had symptoms in a 312 km event, linked to resting symptoms and nerves, not intake (Martínez et al., 2025). Serious symptoms hit about 31% in Ironman but 4% in a cycle race (Pfeiffer et al., 2012).

### Pre-ride meals and carbohydrate loading
**Evidence: Moderate (glycogen effect is pooled; performance gains come from small trials and observational data)**
**Pool: 63 trials, about 641 people (glycogen outcomes, same pool as above)**
**Real world data: 257 people, London Marathon online survey, observational**
Before rides over 60 minutes, eat 1 to 4 g/kg carbohydrate 1 to 4 hours beforehand; before events under 90 minutes, 7 to 12 g/kg over 24 hours; to load for longer events, 10 to 12 g/kg per day for 36 to 48 hours (Thomas et al., 2016; Burke et al., 2011). The 2026 meta-analysis concluded that intakes above about 8 g/kg/day are needed to raise pre race glycogen clearly (Jones et al., 2026). Marathoners eating over 7 g/kg the day before ran faster after adjusting for sex, size and training (Atkinson et al., 2011), an association, not proof.

### Protein: amount, dose per meal and timing
**Evidence: Strong (daily range, from resistance training pools), Moderate (endurance specific benefit), Limited (per meal dose)**
**Pool: 49 studies, about 1,863 people (healthy adults doing resistance training); for endurance, 19 studies with about 1,162 people and 23 trials with about 1,146 people (healthy and clinical groups)**
Daily protein ranges from 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/day (Thomas et al., 2016; Jäger et al., 2017). In the largest pool, extra protein added muscle and strength, with no further gain above about 1.6 g/kg/day (Morton et al., 2018). In endurance training, protein supplements gave small extra gains in VO2peak, lean mass and time trial performance (Lin et al., 2021) and in time to exhaustion, but not VO2max (Xiao et al., 2025). The per meal dose of about 0.25 to 0.3 g/kg (Thomas et al., 2016) comes largely from one study of six young men after leg resistance exercise (Moore et al., 2009), so it is an approximation for cyclists. Protein also supports mitochondrial remodeling (Moore et al., 2014).

### Recovery nutrition
**Evidence: Moderate (carbohydrate refueling), Limited (rehydration volume)**
**Pool: 29 trials, about 246 people (muscle biopsy studies, mostly trained men)**
**Pool for rehydration volume: not pooled; position stand guidance**
With under 8 hours between fuel demanding sessions, take 1 to 1.2 g/kg/h carbohydrate for 4 hours (Thomas et al., 2016; Kerksick et al., 2017). A meta-analysis found carbohydrate at about 1 g/kg/h clearly sped glycogen refilling compared with water, and taking it at least hourly helped; adding protein did not speed refilling when carbohydrate was around 0.9 to 1 g/kg/h (Craven et al., 2021). So post ride protein is for repair, not faster refueling. With longer recovery, daily targets matter most (Burke et al., 2011). To rehydrate, drink about 125 to 150% of the fluid deficit with sodium (Thomas et al., 2016). The grade for refueling was lowered from Strong because the pool is under 300 people.

### Periodized carbohydrate and "train low"
**Evidence: Moderate (cell signaling), Moderate (no clear performance benefit)**
**Pool: 9 training studies, N not reported in abstract (endurance trained men and women, VO2max at least 55 to 60); signaling pool 19 randomized studies, N not reported in abstract**
Low carbohydrate sessions boost some adaptation signals, but only about 37% of 11 studies showed better performance (Impey et al., 2018). A meta-analysis of 19 studies found some metabolic gene signals rose, with PGC-1α rising only when glycogen differences were large (Diaz-Lara et al., 2025). A meta-analysis of 9 training studies in well trained athletes found no overall performance effect of periodized carbohydrate restriction (Gejl & Nybo, 2021). Single studies differ: "sleep low" helped 21 triathletes (Marquet et al., 2016) but withholding carbohydrate did not change cyclists' training gain (Cox et al., 2010). "Fuel for the work required" pairs low carbohydrate with easy sessions and fuels hard ones (Impey et al., 2018; Burke et al., 2018). The performance grade moved from Limited to Moderate because pooled data now show, fairly consistently, no clear benefit.

### Ketogenic and LCHF diets impair economy and performance in elite athletes
**Evidence: Moderate (no benefit in pooled data; harm shown in well designed elite race walker studies)**
**Pool: 18 studies, N not reported in abstract (athletes and trained adults); a separate pool of 10 studies in endurance athletes**
In the "Supernova" studies, world class race walkers on a ketogenic LCHF diet greatly increased fat oxidation but had worse economy and lost the performance benefit of training, while carbohydrate groups improved (Burke et al., 2017); this was replicated (Burke et al., 2020), and even 5 to 6 days raised oxygen cost by 5 to 8% (Burke et al., 2021). Pooled data are less clear: one meta-analysis of 10 studies found no change in VO2max or time to exhaustion (Cao et al., 2021), and a Bayesian meta-analysis of 18 studies found time trial performance about 3.3% worse on ketogenic diets, with wide uncertainty, and judged them unsuitable for high intensity performance (Koerich et al., 2023). No pool shows a performance benefit.

### Hydration: sweat rate, the 2% guidance and its debate
**Evidence: Strong (individual sweat testing, avoid overdrinking), Limited and disputed (the 2% threshold)**
**Pool: 15 studies, about 122 people (dehydration and endurance performance); 9 studies on drinking during cycling, N not reported in abstract**
**Real world data: 2,135 weighed athletes in endurance events, 488 Boston Marathon runners, and 1,303 athletes sweat tested, observational**
The ACSM recommends starting hydrated, avoiding more than 2% body weight loss, and tailoring plans to individual sweat rate and sodium (Sawka et al., 2007). Overdrinking is the main cause of hyponatremia (blood sodium below 135 mmol/L) (Thomas et al., 2016). In 2,135 athletes, falling sodium tracked weight gain during exercise (Noakes et al., 2005), and 13% of Boston finishers tested had hyponatremia, linked to weight gain, more than 3 L of fluid and finish times over 4 hours (Almond et al., 2005). Weighing before and after is the simplest accurate sweat rate method (Baker, 2017); endurance athletes averaged about 1.28 L/h sweat and 52 mmol/h sodium, with large spread (Barnes et al., 2019).
The 2% rule is disputed. In cycling time trials (5 studies, 39 people), losses up to about 4% did not reduce performance and drinking to thirst did best (Goulet, 2011); across 15 studies, dehydration hurt only fixed intensity lab tests (Goulet, 2013). Others argue for a 2% threshold (Cheuvront & Kenefick, 2014). In a cycling meta-analysis, drinking during a hard 1 h ride slightly worsened performance, while drinking about 0.15 to 0.20 mL/kg/min (roughly 0.6 to 0.8 L/h for 70 kg) improved 1 to 2 h moderate rides by about 2% (Holland et al., 2017). The middle ground: avoid large deficits in heat and long events, and never gain weight during a ride.

### Caffeine
**Evidence: Strong**
**Pool: umbrella review of 21 meta-analyses; endurance time trial pool of 44 studies; N not reported in abstract (mostly young men)**
The ISSN concludes that 3 to 6 mg/kg about 60 minutes before consistently improves performance, most in endurance; 2 mg/kg may work; about 9 mg/kg adds side effects; it works in heat and at altitude and can disturb sleep (Guest et al., 2021). An umbrella review rated the evidence for aerobic endurance as moderate quality, with larger effects for aerobic than anaerobic work, and noted few studies in women and older adults (Grgic et al., 2020). In 44 studies, moderate doses improved time trial power by about 3% and time by about 2%, with some individuals slower (Southward et al., 2018). Moderate habitual caffeine is not a meaningful diuretic (Thomas et al., 2016).

### Nitrate (beetroot juice)
**Evidence: Moderate (recreational), Moderate for little or no benefit (highly trained)**
**Pool: 73 studies, about 1,061 people (healthy adults in endurance tasks); a second pool of 80 studies, N not reported in abstract (mostly young men)**
Nitrate lowers the oxygen cost of submaximal exercise; protocols used about 5 to 9 mmol per day, acute doses about 2.5 hours before (Jones, 2014). Across 80 studies the overall effect was small (d = 0.17), with no clear benefit in 26 studies of well trained athletes (VO2max 65 or higher) and too few studies in women (Senefeld et al., 2020). Across 73 studies, nitrate raised power by about 5 W and time to exhaustion, but did not significantly improve time trial performance (Gao et al., 2021). Responses vary; trial it in training and check anti doping risk (Maughan et al., 2018).

### Energy availability and RED-S
**Evidence: Strong (as a health risk), Moderate (exact thresholds)**
**Pool: 11 cross-sectional studies, about 1,006 athletes (about half women)**
Energy availability is the energy left after exercise. Early studies in women linked about 45 kcal per kg fat free mass per day with health and below about 30 with impaired function (Thomas et al., 2016). The 2023 IOC consensus on REDs describes harms in women and men, highlights low carbohydrate availability, and gives clinicians an assessment tool (Mountjoy et al., 2023). In 1,006 athletes, those with low energy availability had lower body weight and bone density scores and altered hormones (Han et al., 2026); these are cross-sectional associations. Cyclists, as a weight sensitive sport, are a group where this matters.

## Rules for the coach
- Set a daily carbohydrate band from the planned training load: rest or easy skill day 3 to 5 g/kg; about 1 h moderate 5 to 7 g/kg; 1 to 3 h moderate to hard 6 to 10 g/kg; 4 to 5 h or stage race days 8 to 12 g/kg.
- Per ride fueling by duration and intensity:
  - Under 45 min, any intensity: water only; no carbohydrate needed.
  - 45 to 75 min, hard (intervals, race, threshold): optional small amount or a carbohydrate mouth rinse; 0 to 30 g total.
  - 45 to 75 min, easy: water only.
  - 75 min to 2.5 h, endurance or tempo: 30 to 60 g/h, starting in the first 20 to 30 minutes and taken every 15 to 20 minutes.
  - 75 min to 2.5 h, easy recovery or zone 1 to 2 ride: lower end, 0 to 30 g/h, or a planned low carbohydrate session only if the plan calls for it.
  - Over 2.5 h, or any race or hard group ride over 2 h: 60 to 90 g/h using glucose plus fructose products; build toward 90 g/h only after practicing.
  - Above 90 g/h: treat as experimental; only if the rider has trained the gut and tolerates it.
- Gut training: in the 4 to 6 weeks before a target event, practice race fueling rates on at least one long ride per week, increasing by about 10 to 15 g/h every 1 to 2 weeks if comfortable. This is sensible practice with limited trial evidence. Persistent gut symptoms belong with a doctor.
- Pre-ride meal for rides over 60 minutes: 1 to 4 g/kg carbohydrate 1 to 4 hours before (about 1 g/kg at 1 h, up to 4 g/kg at 4 h), low in fat and fiber if the rider has gut issues.
- Carbohydrate loading for races longer than 90 minutes: 10 to 12 g/kg/day for the 36 to 48 hours before (at least 8 g/kg), with low fiber foods; use 7 to 12 g/kg the day before shorter races.
- Protein: target 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/day (about 1.6 g/kg covers most riders), split into 4 or more meals of about 0.3 g/kg (20 to 25 g for most riders, up to about 40 g for large riders), including one soon after hard or long rides.
- Recovery: if the next hard session is within 8 hours, give 1 to 1.2 g/kg/h carbohydrate, at least hourly, for 4 hours, plus about 0.3 g/kg protein for muscle repair. Do not cut carbohydrate to make room for protein; protein does not speed refueling. If the next hard session is a day or more away, just hit daily targets.
- Rehydration: drink 1.25 to 1.5 L per kg of body mass lost, with sodium from food or an electrolyte drink.
- Sweat rate test: weigh nude before and after a 60 minute ride; sweat rate (L/h) = (pre kg minus post kg) + fluid drunk L minus urine L. Repeat in hot and cool conditions.
- Fluid plan: on rides up to about 1 hour, drink to thirst; extra fluid is not needed for performance. On longer rides, drink regularly (about 0.6 to 0.8 L/h for a 70 kg rider as a starting point, adjusted by sweat rate), keep body mass loss under about 2% on long or hot rides, and never finish heavier than you started.
- Sodium: for rides over 2 hours or in heat, use drinks with about 0.5 to 0.7 g sodium per liter, as in the heat consensus (Racinais et al., 2015); consider more for heavy, salty sweaters.
- Hyponatremia safety: if a rider gains weight on long events, or reports headache, nausea, confusion or swelling after drinking a lot, tell them to stop drinking plain fluid and seek urgent medical care.
- Train low: limit to about 1 to 3 easy or moderate sessions per week (for example a fasted or "sleep low" easy ride under 90 minutes), never for intervals, races or key long rides, and never for riders with any signs of low energy availability. Present it as optional, since pooled trials show no clear performance gain.
- Never recommend ketogenic or LCHF diets for performance cycling; if a rider follows one, note the evidence of reduced economy and no proven benefit, and suggest they discuss it with a sports dietitian.
- Caffeine: 3 mg/kg about 60 minutes before key sessions or races is a sensible start; do not exceed 6 mg/kg; avoid caffeine within about 6 hours of bedtime if sleep suffers; trial in training first.
- Nitrate: optional for recreational riders (about 6 to 8 mmol nitrate, such as a concentrated beetroot shot, about 2.5 h before, or daily for 3 to 7 days); tell well trained riders the benefit is unlikely; avoid antibacterial mouthwash around dosing.
- Supplements in general: suggest third party tested products for any rider subject to anti doping rules.
- Energy availability red flags: unplanned weight loss, missed or irregular periods, recurrent bone stress injuries, frequent illness, persistent fatigue, low libido, or falling performance. If any appear, say this may signal low energy availability, stop any weight loss or train low plan, and refer to a doctor and a registered sports dietitian. Do not diagnose REDs.
- Any rider with diabetes, a history of disordered eating, kidney disease, high blood pressure medication, pregnancy, or gut disease: refer to a doctor or registered dietitian before changing carbohydrate, protein, sodium or supplement intake.
- The coach gives general fueling targets, not meal plans or medical nutrition therapy. Personalized plans belong with a registered dietitian.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- Intakes above 90 g/h are used by many pros, but controlled performance data are limited; the best dose study found diminishing returns above about 78 g/h (Smith et al., 2013), and the largest pool found no dose effect (Ramos-Campo et al., 2024).
- Most trials are in young trained men; women and older riders are underrepresented (Grgic et al., 2020).
- "Train low" shows no clear race benefit in pooled trials (Gejl & Nybo, 2021).
- LCHF harm data come mainly from elite race walkers; pooled data across athletes are mixed but show no benefit (Koerich et al., 2023; Cao et al., 2021).
- The 2% body mass loss threshold is disputed and rests on about 122 people in short, mostly lab based trials (Goulet, 2011, 2013; Cheuvront & Kenefick, 2014).
- The 20 g (about 0.3 g/kg) protein dose comes from six young men after resistance exercise (Moore et al., 2009).
- Nitrate is understudied in women (Senefeld et al., 2020).
- Energy availability thresholds came mainly from studies in women, field measurement is imprecise, and pooled data are cross-sectional (Mountjoy et al., 2023; Han et al., 2026).
- Gut training rests on one small trial (Cox et al., 2010).
- Daily carbohydrate bands are consensus; pooled data are on glycogen, not performance (Jones et al., 2026).
