# Training by Time Available

## Summary
How much time a rider has each week changes what kind of training makes sense. When time is short, intensity has to carry more of the load, because hard intervals produce a lot of adaptation per minute. In untrained or recreationally active people, a few minutes of very hard work can match the VO2max gains of several hours of steady riding; this is now backed by more than 1,000 people across many trials. In trained cyclists, adding two interval sessions a week improves time trial power within weeks, but pooled data suggest about 2%, not 5%. These gains are fast and early. Over months and years, extra easy volume is what most well trained riders use to keep improving, and adding more intensity on top of a small base does not keep paying off forever.

Fitness is also cheaper to keep than to build. Reduced training and taper studies show VO2max and short endurance can be held with as few as two hard sessions a week, or much shorter sessions, as long as intensity stays high. Long endurance (rides of 2 hours or more) is the first thing to slip when volume drops. The coach should match the weekly structure to the hours, protect intensity when hours fall, and treat any extra hours as mostly easy riding.

## Size of the evidence
The pool is bigger than the classic studies suggest, but it is mostly short trials in people who were not trained cyclists. The largest syntheses are a sprint interval meta analysis (78 studies, about 1,188 people), an interval versus steady VO2max meta analysis (28 studies, 723 people) and an interval and time trial meta analysis (29 studies, 491 people, about half trained). Intensity split studies pool only 350 to 440 athletes. The biggest real world datasets are from running: training logs of about 119,000 and 300,000 marathon runners. These show what faster runners do, not cause and effect. Evidence is still small for trained cyclists under 5 h a week, maintenance in trained riders, indoor versus outdoor riding, and how many hard sessions an amateur can handle. I found no peer reviewed analysis of large cycling platform data (Strava, TrainingPeaks, Garmin, Zwift, intervals.icu) on weekly hours and performance.

## Key findings

### A few minutes of very hard work can match early gains from much longer steady training
**Evidence: Strong (VO2max in untrained and recreationally active adults); Limited (trained cyclists)**
**Pool: 78 studies, about 1,188 people (mostly untrained or recreationally active adults, all out cycling sprints)**
In 16 active young men, six sessions of 4 to 6 x 30 s all out sprints over 2 weeks (about 2.5 h total) improved time trials and muscle oxidative capacity as much as six 90 to 120 min steady rides (about 10.5 h) (Gibala et al., 2006). A 6 week follow up in 20 active but untrained adults found similar mitochondrial and fat oxidation changes with about 1.5 h per week of sprint intervals versus about 4.5 h per week of steady riding (Burgomaster et al., 2008). Over 12 weeks in sedentary men, 3 x 20 s all out sprints within a 10 minute session, three times a week, raised VO2peak by 19%, the same as 45 min of moderate cycling (Gillen et al., 2016). These were small groups of untrained or recreationally active people over 2 to 12 weeks, and do not show that sprints alone can build a race ready cyclist. The wider pool backs the VO2max part strongly. A meta analysis of 16 randomized trials (318 people) found sprint intervals raised VO2max by about 8%, no different from steady endurance training (Gist et al., 2014). A 2026 meta analysis of 78 studies (1,188 people) found the same 8% gain, that adding more sprints per session did not add VO2max, and that longer programs (more weeks) did (Hutchinson et al., 2026). For a beginner, 2 to 4 sprints may give most of the benefit of 6. Almost none of these people were trained cyclists.

### In trained cyclists, two interval sessions a week added to normal easy riding improve performance quickly
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 29 studies, about 491 people (inactive to trained adults, 258 of them trained; time trial outcomes)**
In 38 highly trained cyclists and triathletes (VO2peak about 64 mL/kg/min), adding two HIIT sessions a week for 4 weeks on top of normal easy training improved 40 km time trial performance by 4.4 to 5.8%, while controls did not change (Laursen et al., 2002). In 20 trained male cyclists, six sessions over 3 weeks improved 40 km time trial most with 8 x 4 min at 85% of peak aerobic power, and also with 12 x 30 s at 175% (Stepto et al., 1999). A small cyclist study favored short intervals for VO2max (Ronnestad et al., 2015), and a narrative review argued that in highly trained athletes gains come mainly from HIIT (Laursen & Jenkins, 2002). These trials were short (3 to 10 weeks). The largest synthesis is more modest. Across 29 interval studies (491 people), trained people improved time trial performance by only about 2%, versus up to 6% in less trained people, and going beyond a minimal interval dose did not seem to add more (Rosenblat et al., 2021). Across 6 trials comparing interval types in at least moderately trained people, long intervals (4 min or more) gave about 2% more time trial gain than sprint intervals (Rosenblat et al., 2020). In 9 studies of 176 national level athletes, interval training raised VO2max more than other training (Ma et al., 2023). Practical meaning: tell a trained rider to expect gains of a few percent, not the 5% seen in some single trials.

### Over the long run, top endurance athletes use high volume with a small share of hard work
**Evidence: Moderate (descriptive)**
**Pool: 85 studies, 137 runner cohorts, N not reported in abstract (elite and recreational marathon runners)**
**Real world data: 119,452 runners (151,813 marathons), Strava type training logs, observational**
Descriptive data from national and international endurance athletes training 10 to 13 times a week converge on about 80% of sessions at low intensity and about 20% containing hard work. Intensification studies in well trained athletes do not give convincing evidence that more HIIT gives long term gains (Seiler, 2010). This is observational data from elite athletes with lots of time, not a guide to 3 to 5 hours. Big data in recreational runners point the same way. In training logs before 151,813 marathons by 119,452 runners, the fastest runners trained more than three times the volume of slower runners, and the extra was almost all easy running; time spent at moderate and hard intensity was fairly stable across levels (Muniz-Pumares et al., 2025). A meta regression of 85 studies found more weekly distance, more runs, and longer long runs all went with faster marathon times (Doherty et al., 2020), and data from about 14,000 wearable users linked training volume and intensity to performance (Emig & Peltonen, 2020). These are runners, and faster people may simply be able to train more; this is association, not cause.

### Polarized is not clearly better than other distributions, and recreational riders may do as well or better with pyramidal
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 17 studies, about 437 people (trained endurance athletes); 13 studies, 348 athletes with individual data**
**Real world data: 119,452 marathon runners, training logs, observational (more than 80% of the fastest used a pyramidal split)**
A 6 week crossover in 12 trained cyclists (about 6.4 h per week) favored polarized over a threshold heavy plan (Neal et al., 2013), as did an early meta analysis of 3 small trials (Rosenblat et al., 2019). A larger 2024 meta analysis (17 studies, 437 people) found polarized slightly better for VO2peak, but only in interventions under 12 weeks and in highly trained athletes, with no difference for time trial, time to exhaustion, or threshold power (Silva Oliveira et al., 2024). A 2025 individual participant network meta analysis (13 studies, 348 athletes) found no overall difference between polarized and pyramidal, with a signal that competitive athletes gain more VO2max from polarized and recreational athletes from pyramidal (Rosenblat et al., 2025). In recreational runners, a plan with more moderate work matched polarized with 17% less training time (Festa et al., 2019). Practical meaning: at low hours, the coach does not need to force a strict 80/20 split. A pyramidal split (most time easy, a solid block of tempo and sweet spot, a small dose of VO2max work) is a reasonable default for a time crunched amateur.

### Fitness is cheaper to maintain than to build, and intensity is the variable to protect
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 27 taper studies, N not reported in abstract (competitive athletes); 21 detraining studies, N not reported in abstract; the core maintenance data are three small Hickson studies in young untrained adults**
**Real world data: about 300,000 recreational marathon runners, training logs, observational**
Hickson's classic reduced training studies took untrained young adults through 10 weeks of 40 min a day, 6 days a week, at high intensity, then cut one variable for 15 weeks. Cutting to 2 or 4 days a week kept VO2max (Hickson & Rosenkoetter, 1981). Cutting session length from 40 to 26 or 13 min kept VO2max and 5 min endurance, but the 13 min group lost about 10% of long endurance (2 h or more) (Hickson et al., 1982). Cutting intensity by one third or two thirds caused VO2max to fall and long endurance to drop by 21% and 30% (Hickson et al., 1985). A 2021 narrative review concluded endurance can be kept for up to 15 weeks with 2 sessions per week or a 33 to 66% volume cut if intensity is kept; strength can be kept with 1 session and 1 set per exercise in younger people, while older people may need 2 sessions and 2 to 3 sets (Spiering et al., 2021). In a 32 week trial, a maintenance strength dose of one third or one ninth of the build dose kept muscle size in young adults (20 to 35 years) but not in older adults (60 to 75 years) (Bickel et al., 2011). The Hickson subjects were not trained cyclists. Taper research in athletes supports the same principle. A meta analysis of 27 studies found the best taper cut volume by 41 to 60% over about 2 weeks while keeping intensity and frequency (Bosquet et al., 2007), and a 2023 meta analysis of 14 endurance studies agreed (Wang et al., 2023). A meta analysis of 21 detraining studies in athletes found VO2max falls after both short and long breaks, more after long ones (Zheng et al., 2022). In training logs from about 300,000 recreational marathon runners, a full break of 7 days or more was linked to a 5 to 8% slower finish than the same runner's undisrupted races (Feely et al., 2022). Practical meaning: a reduced plan beats a full stop.

### HIIT and steady training both raise VO2max, but steady training benefits grow with longer programs
**Evidence: Strong (for untrained and moderately fit adults)**
**Pool: 28 studies, about 723 people (healthy adults 18 to 45); plus 37 studies, 334 people and 16 trials, 318 people in separate meta analyses**
A meta analysis of 28 controlled trials (723 people) in healthy adults aged 18 to 45 found both endurance training and HIIT raise VO2max, with HIIT giving a small extra benefit on average, and both giving more with longer programs (Milanovic et al., 2015). This was mostly in untrained or moderately fit people (mean starting VO2max about 41 mL/kg/min). A separate meta analysis of 37 interval studies (334 people) found an average VO2max gain of about 0.5 L/min in 6 to 13 weeks, larger with longer intervals (Bacon et al., 2013). Results are consistent, but say little about already fit cyclists.

### Commuting and accumulated easy riding count
**Evidence: Limited to Moderate (untrained people)**
**Pool: one trial of 122 employees for fitness gains, plus one 1 year trial in untrained adults**
**Real world data: 263,450 adults, UK Biobank, observational (health outcomes, not performance)**
In 122 employees who began commuting by bike (mean one way trip 8.5 km, more than 3 times a week), maximal power per kg rose 13% in 6 months. Gains were larger in those who started less fit and in those who rode more kilometers (Hendriksen et al., 2000). A 1 year trial in untrained adults also improved cholesterol and blood pressure (de Geus et al., 2008). In 263,450 UK Biobank adults, cycle commuters had lower rates of heart disease, cancer and death over about 5 years than car or public transport commuters (Celis-Morales et al., 2017). That is an association with health, not proof of cause or a measure of race fitness. For a trained rider, commuting mainly adds easy volume, which helps long endurance and fat oxidation but will not replace structured intervals.

### Indoor riding may produce lower power at the same perceived effort, but findings conflict
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 2 small studies, about 32 people (recreational and competitive male cyclists), with conflicting results**
In 12 recreationally trained cyclists told to give the same effort indoors and outdoors over 40 km, outdoor power was about 208 W versus 163 W in the lab, with heart rate also higher outdoors and similar RPE (Mieras et al., 2014). In 20 competitive cyclists doing a maximal 20 min time trial on the same bike and power meter, indoor and outdoor power were about the same (280 versus 284 W), though outdoor power varied much more (Jeffries et al., 2019). So the gap is not reliable and may depend on cooling and setup.

### How much intensity a time crunched rider can tolerate
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: none for 3 or more hard sessions per week over months; 2 per week is what most trials used; the ceiling is coaching convention**
Most successful trials in trained cyclists used 2 hard sessions per week, on top of easy riding (Laursen et al., 2002; Ronnestad et al., 2015). There is little controlled evidence on 3 or more per week for months in amateurs; the common ceiling of 2 to 3 comes from coaching practice. Treat a third hard session as an option only when sleep, mood and performance are good. The largest interval meta analysis found that going beyond a minimal interval dose did not clearly add time trial gains (Rosenblat et al., 2021), which fits a cautious cap.

## Rules for the coach
- Ask for real weekly hours, not ideal hours. Plan for the hours the rider hits in a normal week, then treat extra hours as easy bonus riding.
- Hard sessions mean intervals at or above 88% FTP (sweet spot and up), or RPE 7 or higher. Easy means below about 75% FTP, RPE 2 to 4.
- Hard session cap by hours: under 4 h per week, at most 2 hard sessions; 4 to 10 h, 2 to 3 hard sessions; over 10 h, usually 2 to 3, rarely 4. Never schedule hard sessions on back to back days for a rider under 5 h unless one is a short sprint session.
- Under 5 h per week, intensity carries the plan. At least 2 of the 3 to 4 sessions should contain intervals. Use a pyramidal mix; strict polarized is not required.
- Example interval doses that have trial support: 2 to 6 x 20 to 30 s all out with 4 min easy (novices and short sessions; more than 4 sprints adds little VO2max for beginners, so start low); 8 x 4 min at about 105 to 110% FTP (Stepto's 85% of peak aerobic power); 8 intervals at about 120 to 130% FTP (Laursen's intervals at peak aerobic power, each 60% of time to exhaustion, often an estimated 2 to 3 min); 12 x 30 s near all out (about 200 to 230% FTP) with 4 to 4.5 min easy (175% of peak aerobic power in both trials). Threshold work such as 2 x 20 min at 88 to 95% FTP is standard coaching practice. For a trained rider aiming at time trials or long efforts, favor long intervals (4 min or more) over pure sprints. Tell trained riders to expect gains of a few percent over 4 to 8 weeks, not big jumps. All % FTP targets are coaching translations of study intensities that were set as % of ramp test peak power, so treat them as approximate.
- Keep at least one session of 2 h or more every 1 to 2 weeks when the rider's goals include events longer than 2 h. Long endurance is the first ability lost when sessions get short.
- When a rider's time drops for a busy period (work, family, travel), switch to maintenance rather than a full stop: 2 to 3 sessions per week, keep 1 to 2 of them hard, cut duration before cutting intensity. Expect VO2max and short power to hold for about 8 to 15 weeks this way; warn that long ride endurance may drop.
- Strength maintenance during busy periods: 1 session per week, 1 to 2 hard sets per exercise for riders under about 50; 2 sessions per week and 2 to 3 sets for older riders.
- Count commuting as easy volume (RPE 2 to 4) in the weekly load. Do not let commutes turn into daily threshold efforts. If the rider commutes on a hard interval day, the commute stays easy.
- Indoors, set targets by power and use a fan. Some riders produce less power indoors at the same feel and some do not, so compare the rider's own indoor and outdoor numbers before adjusting targets. Keep indoor endurance rides shorter (60 to 90 min) and more structured than outdoor ones.
- Above about 10 to 12 h per week, add new hours as easy riding first (large runner datasets show faster athletes add volume mostly at easy intensity). Do not add a fourth hard day just because hours went up.
- Progress weekly hours by no more than about 10 to 15% for 2 to 3 weeks, then take a lighter week (about 60 to 70% of normal hours) every 3 to 4 weeks. This is coaching practice, not tested in trials.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- Large interval meta analyses mostly pool untrained or moderately fit people over 2 to 13 weeks. Long term results in trained cyclists at 3 to 5 h per week are not well studied.
- Polarized versus pyramidal is unsettled; the newest analyses find no overall difference.
- There is almost no controlled research on the best intensity split at very low volume (under 4 h per week) in trained cyclists.
- The safe number of hard sessions per week for amateurs over months is based on coaching practice, not trials.
- Maintenance research (Hickson) used young untrained people and heart rate based intensity, not trained cyclists with power data.
- Indoor versus outdoor evidence is two small studies that disagree.
- The large real world datasets on volume and intensity split are from runners. I found no peer reviewed analysis of large cycling platform data on weekly hours and performance.
- The table above is a coaching synthesis of the findings, not a tested prescription.
