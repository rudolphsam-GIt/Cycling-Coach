# Cross Training for Cyclists

## Summary

Training in another endurance sport carries some of its aerobic benefit over to the bike, but never as much as riding does. Pooled trials and reviews agree on the direction: some VO2max gain transfers between modes, running transfers more to cycling than cycling does to running, swimming transfers least, and the more trained the athlete, the more specificity matters. For a cyclist, other sports are best used to keep the aerobic engine running in the off season or through an injury, to load bone, and to stay fresh and motivated, while a smaller amount of riding keeps the bike specific adaptations.

Heart rate thresholds do not carry over between sports. In 371 adults tested on both a treadmill and a bike, heart rate at threshold in one did not predict it in the other, so heart rate TSS (hrTSS) for a run or a ski day needs a threshold from that sport, or it will be wrong. Heart rate also overstates the training load in stop start, isometric or cold sports: in recreational downhill skiers, heart rate averaged 77% of max while oxygen uptake was only 45% of max, and recreational hockey players spent about 30 minutes per game above 85% of predicted max heart rate. In those sports, session RPE (effort out of 10 times minutes) is a useful cross check; it has been validated in 36 studies across many sports.

Every sport carries its own costs that TSS does not capture. New runners get injured at more than twice the hourly rate of experienced runners, and the muscle damage from unfamiliar jumping or downhill work lowers cycling time trial power for about 2 days. Nordic skiers often develop cold air asthma. Backcountry skiing adds avalanche risk, which is a safety question, not a training one. Downhill skiing and hockey add impact and injury risk. On the positive side, road cyclists tend to have low bone density, and weight bearing sports such as running are associated with better bone.

## Size of the evidence

The pool is small and old for the central question. The only meta-analysis of running and cycling transfer (2026) found just 7 randomized trials, about 143 people, all published between 1974 and 2003, and none measured cycling performance. The classic reviews of cross training (1994) and of cycling versus running physiology (2009) rest on small lab studies. The largest single dataset is 371 adults who did both a treadmill and a bike test, which shows heart rate thresholds are sport specific.

No trial has tested whether Nordic skiing, skate skiing, ski touring, downhill skiing or hockey improves cycling performance. What exists for those sports is physiology: what they demand, measured in small groups (6 to 38 recreational downhill skiers, 11 ski mountaineers, 113 recreational hockey players), plus reviews of elite skiers and an energy cost compendium covering over 1,100 activities. Injury data are larger: 13 studies pooled for running injuries per hour, 31 studies on cycling and bone, 8 studies pooled for asthma in Nordic skiers, and over 1,200 complete avalanche burials for survival times. Session RPE has 36 validation studies across sports. No study has validated hrTSS outside cycling and running, and no large platform dataset (Strava, Garmin, TrainingPeaks) on cyclists' cross training has been published.

## Key findings

### Some aerobic fitness transfers between sports, but specific training always does more
**Evidence: Moderate (some transfer exists), Limited (how much reaches cycling performance)**
**Pool: 7 randomized trials, about 143 people (untrained to college runners, 5 trials men only, 2 women only); one 8 week trial of 30 trained runners; narrative reviews of small lab studies**
A 1994 review concluded that some VO2max gain transfers from one mode to another, that cross training effects never exceed those of training in the sport itself, and that specificity matters more the better trained the athlete (Tanaka, 1994). The same review saw a place for cross training in general fitness, during injury rehabilitation, and during overtraining or mental fatigue (Tanaka, 1994). A 2026 meta-analysis of 7 randomized trials found no clear difference in VO2max or running performance between run only training and cycling or mixed training over 4 to 10 weeks (treadmill VO2max g = -0.32, cycle VO2max g = -0.34, running performance g = 0.02, none significant), but warned that this does not show the modes are interchangeable; the trials were small, old and at some risk of bias (Menges et al., 2026). In 30 trained runners, adding swimming improved a 3.2 km run less than adding the same extra time running (13 s against 26 s faster), and only the running group improved threshold speed (Foster et al., 1995). VO2max itself is specific to the mode an athlete trains in (Millet et al., 2009).

### Running transfers to cycling better than the reverse; swimming transfers least
**Evidence: Moderate (direction, from reviews), Limited (size)**
**Pool: narrative reviews of small lab and training studies; the 2026 meta-analysis (7 trials, about 143 people) did not test this direction directly**
Both major reviews conclude that running training transfers more to cycling than cycling training transfers to running (Tanaka, 1994; Millet et al., 2009), and that swim training gives the least transfer to VO2max in other modes (Tanaka, 1994). Triathletes, who train both, show similar VO2max on a treadmill and a bike, while specialists score higher in their own sport (Millet et al., 2009). Running and Nordic skiing load the legs in ways cycling does not (impact, eccentric work, upper body), so some of the fitness they build is not cycling fitness; this is an inference from the specificity findings, not a measured value.

### Heart rate thresholds are sport specific, so hrTSS needs a threshold for each sport
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 371 adults (304 men, 67 women) tested on treadmill and cycle ergometer within 3 weeks; 19 swimmers and triathletes tested in swimming and cycling**
In 371 people tested on both a treadmill and a bike, heart rate at the individual anaerobic threshold and at 4 mmol/L lactate did not correlate between running and cycling, and the authors concluded heart rate guidance is only reliable when it comes from a test in that sport (Roecker et al., 2003). Reviews agree that heart rate differs between running and cycling at both maximal and submaximal intensity (Millet et al., 2009). In 10 triathletes, maximal heart rate was about 14 beats lower in swimming than in cycling (175 against 189), while threshold as a share of VO2max did not differ (Roels et al., 2005). TrainingPeaks scores a workout's hrTSS from the threshold heart rate set for that sport when one exists, and falls back to the default threshold otherwise (TrainingPeaks, help documentation). A ski or running day scored against a cycling threshold will therefore be off in either direction, and by an amount that cannot be predicted from the cycling number alone.

### Heart rate overstates the load in stop start, isometric and cold sports; session RPE is a useful check
**Evidence: Moderate (session RPE validity), Limited (heart rate against metabolic load in downhill skiing and hockey)**
**Pool: 36 validation studies of session RPE across sports and ages; 6 older recreational downhill skiers with measured oxygen uptake; 38 recreational downhill skiers; 113 recreational hockey players with Holter monitoring**
**Real world data: none for hrTSS outside cycling and running**
In 6 recreational downhill skiers aged about 61, oxygen uptake while skiing averaged 45% of VO2max and blood lactate stayed near 2 mmol/L, while heart rate averaged 77% and peaked at 98% of max (Krautgasser et al., 2011). In 38 recreational skiers, slope grade and speed drove heart rate and RPE more than ability, sex or age (Seifert et al., 2017). Reviews of downhill skiing describe a mix of aerobic, anaerobic and strength demands with large isometric and eccentric muscle work (Turnbull et al., 2009); static contractions and cold raise heart rate without a matching rise in oxygen use, so hrTSS from a downhill day likely overstates the aerobic training it gave (inference). In recreational hockey, every player's heart rate passed 85% of predicted max and stayed there about 30 minutes per game (Atwal et al., 2002). Session RPE (effort 0 to 10 times minutes) was valid and reliable across 36 studies in many sports, sexes and ages (Haddad et al., 2017), which makes it a reasonable second estimate when heart rate and feel disagree.

### Nordic and skate skiing: whole body, high aerobic demand, with cold air risks
**Evidence: Moderate (demands and asthma prevalence), Limited (transfer to cycling, untested)**
**Pool: review of elite skiers; energy cost compendium of 1,114 activities; 8 studies pooled for asthma prevalence (30 in the wider review); a survey of 351 competitive skiers and 338 controls**
Cross country skiing is among the most demanding endurance sports, using arms and legs together, with elite skiers among the highest VO2max values recorded (Sandbakk & Holmberg, 2017). The 2024 compendium lists moderate classic skiing at 8.5 METs, brisk skiing at 11.3, skate skiing at 13.3 and maximal uphill skiing at 15.5, compared with 6.8 for slow ski walking (Herrmann et al., 2024). Because the upper body shares the work, some of a ski day's load is not cycling specific (inference). Asthma is common in competitive Nordic skiers: 21% in a meta-analysis of 8 studies, often undiagnosed, linked to breathing large volumes of cold, dry air (Mäki-Heikkilä et al., 2020); in 351 Finnish competitive skiers it was 26% against 9% in the general population, and higher with more training (Mäki-Heikkilä et al., 2022). No study has tested whether ski training improves cycling performance.

### Backcountry ski touring: long, steady climbing with little load on the way down, and avalanche risk
**Evidence: Limited (physiology, small groups), Moderate (avalanche survival times, large observational data)**
**Pool: 11 ski mountaineers in a field study of slope and speed; perspective review of ski mountaineering; 1,247 complete avalanche burials (301 Canada, 946 Switzerland, 1980 to 2005)**
**Real world data: 1,247 complete burials, observational**
Uphill skinning is sustained endurance work at a heart rate and effort a rider can hold for hours, with the energy cost per vertical metre lowest on steeper tracks when the skier can keep a decent speed (Praz et al., 2016). Races last from minutes to many hours with up to about 2,000 m of climbing (Bortolan et al., 2021). The compendium has no ski touring entry, but lists maximal uphill skiing at 15.5 METs and snowshoeing at 5.3 to 10 METs (Herrmann et al., 2024). Descents add little aerobic load but some leg fatigue and heart rate from effort and cold (inference from downhill data). Avalanche burial is the main hazard: in 1,247 complete burials, about half survived overall, survival fell steeply after the first 15 to 35 minutes, and fast extrication by companions mattered most (Haegeli et al., 2011). Avoiding avalanches is far more effective than surviving one.

### Downhill skiing and snowboarding: low to moderate aerobic load, high eccentric and impact load
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: review of 29 on snow and 9 off snow studies (mostly elite racers); 6 and 38 recreational skiers in field studies**
Downhill skiing draws on aerobic, anaerobic and strength systems at once, with technical skill the strongest influence on racing performance (Turnbull et al., 2009). For recreational skiers the aerobic load is modest, about 45% of VO2max on average with low lactate (Krautgasser et al., 2011), and the compendium lists light to vigorous downhill skiing at 4.3 to 8.0 METs for active time only, excluding lifts (Herrmann et al., 2024). The demanding part for a cyclist is eccentric and isometric work in the thighs, which can leave soreness that affects riding in the following days (inference, see the muscle damage finding below). Knee injuries are a known risk of the sport; injury rates were not pooled for this file.

### Ice hockey: repeated near maximal efforts, with heart warnings for some older recreational players
**Evidence: Limited (hockey data), Moderate (heavy exertion as a heart attack trigger, and protection from regular exercise)**
**Pool: 113 male recreational hockey players with Holter monitoring; 1,228 heart attack patients in a case crossover study**
In 113 recreational players (mostly over 35), maximum heart rate averaged 184, 76% of monitoring sessions passed age predicted max, heart rate stayed above 85% of predicted max for about 30 minutes, recovery after play was slow in 70%, and 15 sessions showed ST segment depression and 2 non sustained ventricular tachycardia (Atwal et al., 2002). Age predicted max heart rate has a wide spread between people, so "exceeding" it is not by itself a warning, as the journal's editorial on the study pointed out. In 1,228 heart attack patients, heavy exertion raised the risk in the following hour, but far less in people who exercised regularly than in sedentary people (Mittleman et al., 1993). Hockey therefore counts as a hard session for load, and a fit, regularly training cyclist carries much lower exertion risk than a weekend only player, though symptoms during play still need a doctor.

### Running: good for bone and transfer, but new runners get hurt and get sore
**Evidence: Moderate (injury rates per hour), Limited (bone in cyclists, observational; muscle damage and cycling, small trials)**
**Pool: 13 studies pooled for running injuries per 1,000 hours; 31 studies on cycling and bone; 43 men (27 cyclists, 16 runners); 17 people in a randomized trial of muscle damage and cycling time trials**
Novice runners were injured at 17.8 per 1,000 hours of running against 7.7 for recreational runners, so per hour a new runner carries more than twice the risk (Videbæk et al., 2015). Unfamiliar jumping that caused muscle damage lowered 15 minute cycling time trial power and raised the oxygen cost of riding 48 hours later, without changing how hard the effort felt (Burt & Twist, 2011); running, especially downhill, and downhill skiing produce the same kind of eccentric damage in new participants (inference). Road cycling does not appear to build bone, and road cyclists often have low spine density (Olmedillas et al., 2012). In 43 men, cyclists had osteopenia of the spine or hip far more often than runners (63% against 19%) despite similar age, weight and activity (Rector et al., 2008). These bone data show associations, not proof that adding running builds bone in a cyclist.

### Swimming, rowing and ski erg: aerobic and upper body work with little leg transfer
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: narrative review of small training studies; 19 swimmers and triathletes; energy cost compendium**
Swim training gives the least transfer of VO2max to other modes (Tanaka, 1994), and maximal heart rate is lower in the water than on a bike (Roels et al., 2005). Rowing and ski ergometers work the whole body; the compendium lists double poling on a ski erg at 10.5 to 18.0 METs (Herrmann et al., 2024). These are useful for general fitness, upper body strength and recovery days, but they do little for cycling specific leg fitness (inference from specificity). Swimming adds no impact for bone.

### Off season and breaks: other sports slow the loss of fitness
**Evidence: Moderate (detraining), Limited (cross training as a substitute)**
**Pool: narrative reviews of detraining studies; the transfer studies above**
With an insufficient training stimulus, highly trained athletes lose VO2max and blood volume quickly in the first weeks, and 6 to 20% of VO2max over longer breaks (Mujika & Padilla, 2000a; 2000b). Because aerobic fitness partly transfers between sports (Tanaka, 1994; Menges et al., 2026), steady skiing, running or other endurance work is a reasonable way to keep the aerobic base through winter or an injury, while some riding keeps the bike specific gains (inference). Snowshoeing (5.3 to 10 METs), skating (5.5 to 9 METs), hiking and team sports also count as aerobic training at their own intensities (Herrmann et al., 2024); climbing and yoga add little aerobic load.

## Rules for the coach

- Count every sport in training load, as TrainingPeaks does. Score cross training with hrTSS using that sport's own threshold heart rate. Never score a run or a ski day against the cycling threshold.
- Sport thresholds: ask the athlete for a threshold heart rate from a test or a hard race in that sport (running, Nordic or skate skiing). Running and skiing thresholds are often a few beats above cycling, but the gap varies between people and cannot be predicted from the cycling number, so do not apply a fixed offset. Until a sport threshold exists, estimate load with session RPE (effort 0 to 10 times minutes, roughly TSS like when an RPE of 7 to 8 for an hour feels like threshold) and say it is an estimate (convention).
- Treat hrTSS from downhill skiing, snowboarding, hockey and other stop start sports as an upper estimate. When it disagrees clearly with the athlete's RPE, use the RPE based number and tell the athlete why (heart rate rises with static muscle work and cold more than oxygen use does). Count only active time, not lifts and breaks, for downhill days.
- A ski day can replace a ride. A long Nordic or backcountry day stands in for the weekend long ride; plan it by duration and heart rate zone with a target hrTSS, and keep a bike option as fallback for poor snow or weather. Descents in ski touring add little load; base the target on the climbing time.
- Keep some riding through a cross training season. At least 1 to 2 rides a week, one with some intensity, keeps bike specific fitness while other sports carry the aerobic base. Increase riding in the 6 to 8 weeks before the season's first important event. This is coaching convention built on the specificity findings, not a tested dose.
- Eccentric and impact load is not in TSS. After a first run of the season, a long downhill ski day, a hockey game or a hike with a big descent, avoid key bike intervals for about 48 hours and expect higher heart rate for the same watts while sore.
- New runners (or returning after months off): start with 2 to 3 short runs a week of 10 to 20 minutes, walk breaks allowed, on soft surfaces when possible, and add time gradually (roughly 10% a week as a convention). Per hour, new runners get hurt more than twice as often as experienced runners.
- Hockey games and skate skiing races count as hard sessions. Do not stack them with a hard bike day before or after; put an easy day next to them.
- Recommend weight bearing activity (running, hiking, jumping, plus heavy lifting from the strength topic) for road only riders, especially masters riders, as a reasonable step for bone health; present the bone evidence in cyclists as an association.
- Fueling for long ski days follows the nutrition topic: plan carbohydrate per hour as for a long ride of the same duration, and warm drinks or food that will not freeze.
- Swimming, rowing and ski erg work are fine as easy aerobic days, recovery and upper body training. Do not count them as leg training or as a replacement for key bike sessions.
- The athlete's enjoyment of other sports is a valid reason to include them. Variety helps motivation and mental freshness in the off season; do not talk an athlete out of a sport they love for small cycling gains.
- Safety rule, backcountry skiing: never encourage entering avalanche terrain to reach a training target. Remind athletes who tour off piste to check the regional avalanche forecast, carry a beacon, shovel and probe, travel with a trained partner, and take an avalanche course. Turning back always beats finishing the planned hrTSS.
- Safety rule, chest symptoms: chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness or palpitations during hockey or any sport means stop and see a doctor before training hard again. Cough, wheeze or chest tightness after hard skiing in cold air also warrants a doctor's check for exercise induced asthma; a face covering or buff over the mouth in very cold air is a reasonable comfort measure (convention).
- Other sports follow the environment topic for cold and altitude: ski days are often at altitude and in cold, both of which change heart rate and pacing.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed

- No trial has tested whether Nordic skiing, skate skiing, ski touring, downhill skiing or hockey improves cycling performance. Advice to use them is built on physiology and the general transfer findings.
- The only meta-analysis of running and cycling transfer has 7 small trials from 1974 to 2003, measured running outcomes, and could not test cycling performance. The claim that running transfers to cycling better than the reverse comes from narrative reviews.
- hrTSS has not been validated against measured training load in any sport other than cycling and running. How much it overstates downhill skiing and hockey load is not known; the downhill data come from 6 older recreational skiers.
- How far a ski or running threshold heart rate sits from the cycling threshold varies between people; the 371 person study shows it cannot be predicted, not what the usual gap is.
- Bone data in cyclists are cross sectional; no trial has shown that adding running to a cyclist's training raises bone density.
- The 48 hour drop in cycling power after muscle damage comes from small trials using jumps, not from running or skiing directly.
- Injury rates for recreational downhill skiing, hockey and ski touring were not pooled for this file.
- Recommendations for how much riding to keep during a cross training season are coaching convention.
- No large platform dataset (Strava, Garmin, TrainingPeaks) on cyclists' cross training has been published.
