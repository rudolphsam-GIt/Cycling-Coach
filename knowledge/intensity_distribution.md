# Training Intensity Distribution (Polarized, Pyramidal, Threshold, Sweet Spot)

## Summary

Training intensity distribution (TID) means how a rider's weekly training time is split across easy, moderate and hard efforts. Research usually uses three zones. Zone 1 is easy riding below the first lactate or ventilatory threshold (you can talk in full sentences). Zone 2 is the band between the first and second thresholds (steady, "tempo" to "threshold" riding, where sweet spot sits). Zone 3 is above the second threshold (hard intervals). A **polarized** plan puts most time in zone 1, a meaningful slice in zone 3, and very little in zone 2. A **pyramidal** plan also puts most time in zone 1, then less in zone 2, then the least in zone 3. A **threshold** plan puts a large share of time in zone 2.

The short version of the evidence is this. Elite endurance athletes almost always do the large majority of their training easy, and plans built around a big base of easy riding (polarized or pyramidal) tend to beat plans built mostly around threshold work. Early small trials suggested polarized was clearly best. Larger and newer pooled analyses (2024 and 2025) find polarized and pyramidal give about the same time trial gains, with polarized possibly a bit better for VO2max in competitive athletes and pyramidal possibly a bit better for recreational athletes. For an amateur with limited hours, the most important things are riding consistently, keeping most riding genuinely easy, and doing about two well executed hard sessions per week. The exact split between polarized and pyramidal matters much less than total volume and consistency.

## Key findings

### Elite endurance athletes do most of their training at low intensity
**Evidence: Moderate**
Observational work in well trained athletes shows a consistent pattern. In junior cross country skiers, about 75% of endurance sessions were in zone 1, 8% in zone 2 and 17% in zone 3, and the split was similar whether measured by heart rate or session RPE (Seiler & Kjerland, 2006). A review of descriptive studies in athletes training 10 to 13 times per week found roughly 80% of sessions performed at low intensity and about 20% containing high intensity work, and concluded that adding more high intensity interval training to already well trained athletes has not shown convincing long term performance gains (Seiler, 2010). A later review found that most retrospective studies actually show a pyramidal pattern, with some world class athletes shifting to polarized in certain phases of the season (Stöggl & Sperlich, 2015). This is descriptive data from highly trained athletes with large volumes, so it tells us what successful athletes do, not that it is optimal for everyone.

### Easy base plus hard work beats a threshold heavy plan, at least in short trials
**Evidence: Moderate**
In 12 trained male cyclists in a crossover design, 6 weeks of polarized training (about 6.4 h per week; 80% zone 1, 0% zone 2, 20% zone 3) gave larger gains than threshold training (about 7.5 h per week; 57% zone 1, 43% zone 2) in peak power output (8% vs 3%), lactate threshold (9% vs 2%) and high intensity exercise capacity, even with less weekly time. Both blocks improved 40 km time trial performance (Neal et al., 2013). In 48 well trained runners, cyclists, triathletes and skiers over 9 weeks, polarized training produced the largest gains in VO2peak (+11.7%), ramp time to exhaustion and peak power, while threshold and high volume training produced little further change (Stöggl & Sperlich, 2014). In subelite runners over 5 months, more zone 1 (about 80%) beat more zone 2 (about 67% zone 1, 25% zone 2) for a 10.4 km race when zone 3 was held equal (Esteve-Lanao et al., 2007). A 2019 meta-analysis of three RCTs found a moderate effect favoring polarized over threshold for time trial performance, but the included trials were few and of modest quality (PEDro 4 to 5 out of 10) (Rosenblat et al., 2019). A review of runners concluded that plans with at least 70% low intensity and no more than 30% threshold plus high intensity work gave better results than threshold plans (Campos et al., 2022).

### Polarized and pyramidal give similar time trial gains
**Evidence: Moderate**
A 2024 meta-analysis of 17 studies (437 athletes) found polarized was slightly better for VO2peak (SMD 0.24), but only in interventions shorter than 12 weeks and in highly trained athletes. For time trial performance, time to exhaustion and power or speed at the second threshold, polarized was no better than other distributions (time trial SMD -0.01) (Silva Oliveira et al., 2024). A 2025 network meta-analysis using individual participant data from 13 studies (348 athletes) found no difference between polarized and pyramidal for VO2max or time trial performance, and no significant difference between polarized and any other model (Rosenblat et al., 2025). In national elite rowers over 11 weeks, polarized was not better than pyramidal when zone 1 was held near 93% in both groups (Treff et al., 2017). In 60 well trained runners over 16 weeks, the best results came from 8 weeks of pyramidal followed by 8 weeks of polarized (about 1.5% faster 5 km), better than either model alone or the reverse order (Filipas et al., 2022). The 2022 published debate between leading researchers shows the field has not reached agreement on whether polarized is "optimal" (Foster et al., 2022; Burnley et al., 2022).

### Competitive athletes may respond better to polarized, recreational athletes to pyramidal
**Evidence: Limited**
In the 2025 individual participant network meta-analysis, a subgroup analysis found competitive athletes tended to improve VO2max more with polarized, while recreational athletes tended to improve more with pyramidal (Rosenblat et al., 2025). This is one subgroup finding from pooled small studies and has not been tested directly in a trial designed for it. The 2024 meta-analysis similarly found the polarized advantage for VO2peak only in highly trained athletes (Silva Oliveira et al., 2024).

### In recreational athletes most models work, and moderate intensity can save time
**Evidence: Limited**
In 30 recreational runners over 10 weeks, both polarized (about 77/3/20) and a between thresholds plan (about 46/35/19) improved 10 km time, by 5.0% and 3.6%; the difference was not significant overall, though runners who most closely followed the polarized plan improved more (Muñoz et al., 2014). In 38 recreational runners over 8 weeks with load matched by TRIMP, a polarized plan (77/3/20) and a moderate focused plan (40/50/10) gave similar gains, but the focused plan took about 17% less total time (29.9 h vs 24.8 h) (Festa et al., 2019). In 15 moderately trained triathletes over 6 weeks, polarized and moderate distributions gave similar results (Röhrken et al., 2020). These are runners and triathletes with small samples, but they suggest that when time is the main limit, some zone 2 work is a reasonable way to get load in.

### Total volume and consistency matter at least as much as the split
**Evidence: Limited**
Several of the trials above held total load or time roughly constant, which means TID was tested on top of a fixed volume. When load is matched, differences between models are small (Festa et al., 2019; Rosenblat et al., 2025). The descriptive data show elite athletes reach their 80/20 type split at very high volumes, 10 to 13 sessions per week (Seiler, 2010), and the polarization concept was developed in athletes for whom volume is near its ceiling (Treff et al., 2019). There is no direct trial testing what split is best at 4 to 6 hours per week in cyclists, so applying elite ratios to low volume riders is an extrapolation.

### How you count the zones changes the answer
**Evidence: Moderate**
Different quantification methods (heart rate, session RPE, lactate, pace) can classify the same training differently (Campos et al., 2022), although in one group of junior skiers heart rate and session RPE gave very similar splits (Seiler & Kjerland, 2006). Treff et al. (2017) counted zone 1 and zone 2 as time in heart rate zone but zone 3 as the duration of the hard intervals, which shows that studies do not all count the same way. A simple polarization index (log10 of zone 1 times zone 3 times 100 divided by zone 2, with zone values as percentages) above 2.0 identifies a polarized week (Treff et al., 2019).

### Sweet spot training
**Evidence: Limited**
"Sweet spot" (roughly 88 to 94% FTP) is a popular coaching term for the upper part of zone 2. A search of Europe PMC for peer reviewed trials on "sweet spot" cycling training found none. The closest evidence is threshold and between thresholds groups in the trials above, which did not beat easy base plus hard work models in trained athletes (Neal et al., 2013; Stöggl & Sperlich, 2014), but performed about as well in recreational runners while saving time (Festa et al., 2019). Treat sweet spot as a reasonable, time efficient zone 2 tool, not a proven superior method.

## Rules for the coach

Zone mapping used below is a common practical approximation, not a value taken from the cited studies. Research zones were set from lab thresholds, and FTP only approximates the second threshold. Zone 1 is below about 75% FTP (RPE 1 to 4 out of 10). Zone 2 is about 76 to 100% FTP (RPE 5 to 7), with sweet spot at 88 to 94% FTP (RPE 6 to 7). Zone 3 is above 100% FTP (RPE 8 to 10).

- Default for all riders is a low intensity base. Keep at least 70% of weekly riding time in zone 1, and aim for 75 to 85% when hours allow (Campos et al., 2022; Seiler, 2010).
- Count intensity by session, not just by minutes. A week of 3 to 5 rides should usually contain no more than 2 hard sessions (zone 2 intervals or zone 3 intervals). The rest are easy.
- Easy days must be easy. Cap zone 1 rides at about 75% FTP and RPE 3 to 4. If heart rate drifts above the first threshold on a long ride, back off.
- Do not force a choice between polarized and pyramidal. Either is acceptable (Rosenblat et al., 2025; Silva Oliveira et al., 2024). Use these defaults.
  - Recreational rider, under 8 h per week, or less than 2 years of structured training. Use pyramidal. Example week is 1 sweet spot or threshold session plus 1 VO2max session plus easy rides.
  - Competitive rider, 8 or more h per week, VO2max likely above about 55 mL/kg/min. Polarized is reasonable, especially in a short block of 6 to 12 weeks targeting VO2max. Example is 2 zone 3 sessions plus easy volume, minimal zone 2.
- Periodize when there is a goal event. Use pyramidal in the base and build phase, then shift toward polarized for the final 6 to 8 weeks before the event (Filipas et al., 2022, runners).
- Adjustments by hours available.
  - 3 to 5 h per week. 2 quality sessions of 45 to 75 min plus 1 to 2 easy rides. A higher zone 2 share (up to about 30 to 40% of time) is acceptable because it adds load in less time (Festa et al., 2019; Muñoz et al., 2014). Still keep at least 1 genuinely easy ride.
  - 6 to 10 h per week. 2 quality sessions plus 3 to 4 easy rides, including 1 long ride of 2 to 4 h. Target 75 to 85% zone 1 time.
  - Over 10 h per week. 2, at most 3, quality sessions. Add volume to zone 1 rather than adding hard sessions (Seiler, 2010).
- Volume first. When a rider can add time, add it as zone 1 riding before adding intensity. When a rider loses time, protect the 2 quality sessions and cut easy volume first.
- Use session RPE as a check. Hard sessions should feel 7 to 9 out of 10 and easy sessions 2 to 4. If a rider reports most rides at 5 to 6, they are drifting into a threshold heavy pattern.
- Track the polarization index if zone data is available. A value above 2.0 is polarized (Treff et al., 2019). Do not chase a number, use it to spot drift.
- Do not promise specific gains from a TID change. Expected differences between models are small compared with the effect of training consistently.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed

- Whether polarized is truly superior is still debated by leading researchers (Foster et al., 2022; Burnley et al., 2022). Early positive trials were small, short (6 to 12 weeks) and often unblinded.
- Most trials last 6 to 16 weeks. There is almost no data on which model is better over a full season or multiple years.
- Sample sizes are small, usually 10 to 60 athletes, and mostly young men. Data on women and masters riders over 50 is very limited.
- The recreational versus competitive split in response (Rosenblat et al., 2025) is a subgroup finding, not a tested rule.
- Several key studies are in runners, rowers or mixed sports, not cyclists.
- No peer reviewed trial was found that tests "sweet spot" training as a named method.
- Different studies define zones and count time differently, which makes "80/20" mean different things across papers.
- There is no direct trial of TID in cyclists training under 5 h per week.
