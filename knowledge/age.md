# Training Across the Lifespan

## Summary
Endurance performance peaks around age 35, falls slowly until about 50 to 60, then falls faster (Tanaka & Seals, 2008). Race results from more than a million finishers show the same shape. The main cause is a falling VO2max; economy does not seem to decline. In trained people, VO2max still drops, roughly 5 to 15% per decade, faster after 70, and part of the drop comes from riders training less and easier. Masters riders who keep training hard keep far more fitness, strength and muscle than sedentary peers. The person to person spread is huge: age alone is a poor predictor of what an individual older cyclist can do (Pollock et al., 2015).

Older riders often report more fatigue after hard days, but a meta analysis of 36 studies found age does not increase measured muscle damage; recovery spacing by age is coaching convention. The practical approach is to keep high intensity, add strength and impact work, eat enough protein, and space the hardest sessions by how the rider recovers. Long term endurance athletes, especially men, have a higher risk of atrial fibrillation. The coach cannot diagnose anything, but should push for medical review when symptoms appear. For juniors, the priority is broad athletic development, sensible load during growth spurts, and enjoyment, not early adult style volume.

## Size of the evidence
The evidence is large where it describes decline and small where it guides training. The biggest datasets are race results (about 840,000 Ironman 70.3, 411,000 Ironman and 300,000 marathon finishers), fitness registries (FRIEND 22,379 tests, Aerobics Center 20,318 adults, Baltimore 810 adults followed 8 years, HUNT 3,816) and an HRmax meta analysis of 18,712 people. AF data pool more than 650,000 people and youth development data 6,000 to 9,000 athletes. All are observational: patterns, not cause. Training questions rest on small pools: masters cyclist studies of 9 to 114 riders, small recovery studies, and strength and protein trials mostly in non athletes. I found no peer reviewed analysis of large cycling platform data (Strava, TrainingPeaks, Garmin, Zwift, intervals.icu) on age and training.

## Key findings

### Performance declines slowly to 50 to 60, then faster
**Evidence: Strong (descriptive; large, consistent cross sectional and longitudinal data)**
**Pool: small lab studies in cyclists (32 and 114 men) plus a narrative review; the size comes from observational data below**
**Real world data: about 840,100 Ironman 70.3 finishers, 410,881 Ironman finishers and 300,757 marathon and half marathon runners, race results, observational**
Peak endurance performance holds until about 35, declines modestly until 50 to 60, then declines more steeply. VO2max is the main driver, and economy does not change with age in trained adults (Tanaka & Seals, 2008). In 32 masters cyclists, VO2max averaged 60.7, 54.2 and 45.9 mL/kg/min, and peak power 434, 392 and 324 W, at 35 to 44, 45 to 54 and 55 plus; thresholds as a share of max were higher in the oldest group, so FTP may hold up better than VO2max power (Peiffer et al., 2008). In 114 male cyclists, ramp test peak power peaked about 30 and then fell about 2.4 W per year, but age explained only 25% of the spread (Balmer et al., 2005). In Ironman 70.3 (840,100 finishers) the cycling split started to slow at about 34 to 35 (Jackel et al., 2020); in full Ironman (410,881 finishers) at 35 to 39 in men and 30 to 34 in women (Kach et al., 2018). In 300,757 marathon and half marathon runners, times barely changed from 20 to 49 and slowed 2.6 to 4.4% per decade from 50 to 69 (Leyk et al., 2007). Masters track cycling bests suggest maximal aerobic power falls about 16% per decade after 45 (Capelli et al., 2016). Race data include only people still racing, so they may flatter the decline.

### Trained people still lose VO2max, but from a much higher level
**Evidence: Moderate (findings conflict on rate)**
**Pool: two meta analyses of cross sectional data, N not reported in abstract (men; women), plus small masters follow up studies**
**Real world data: 22,379 lab tests (FRIEND), 20,318 adults (Aerobics Center Longitudinal Study), 810 adults followed about 8 years (Baltimore Longitudinal Study), 3,816 adults (HUNT), observational, mostly general population**
Over about 8 years, masters endurance athletes (about 62 years) who kept training lost VO2max at about 5.5% per decade, versus 12% per decade in sedentary peers, and their maximal heart rate did not fall (Rogers et al., 1990). In a 20 year follow up of older track athletes, VO2max fell about 8 to 15% per decade even with continued vigorous training; the few who greatly cut their training fell faster (18% then 34%) (Pollock et al., 1997). Cross sectional meta analyses differ: in men, absolute decline was similar across sedentary, active and trained groups (about 4 to 4.6 mL/kg/min, 6.8 to 8.7% per decade; Wilson & Tanaka, 2000); in women it was largest in trained women (6.2 mL/kg/min) with similar relative decline (10 to 10.9%; Fitzgerald et al., 1997). Trained people stay far fitter at every age; they start higher and often cut training as they age. Large cohorts show the decline speeds up with age. In the Baltimore study the loss rose from 3 to 6% per decade in the 20s and 30s to more than 20% per decade in the 70s, regardless of activity level (Fleg et al., 2005). In 20,318 adults, fitness fell faster after 45 (Jackson et al., 2009). FRIEND cycle tests fell about 16% per decade on average (Kaminsky et al., 2022), and HUNT about 3.5 mL/kg/min per decade (Loe et al., 2013). Practical meaning: plan on roughly 5 to 10% decline per decade for a masters rider who keeps training into the 50s and 60s, expect faster loss after 70, and much faster if training drops.

### Maximal heart rate falls with age, and 220 minus age underestimates it in older people
**Evidence: Strong**
**Pool: 351 studies, about 18,712 people (healthy adults), plus a lab validation in 514**
A meta analysis of 351 studies (18,712 people) and a lab validation in 514 people found HRmax is predicted by 208 minus 0.7 x age, independent of sex and activity level (Tanaka et al., 2001). The 220 minus age formula underestimates HRmax in older adults. Measured values beat formulas. In the 20 year follow up, HRmax fell about 5 to 7 beats per minute per decade (Pollock et al., 1997).

### Recovery in older athletes: not clearly slower, but older riders feel more fatigued
**Evidence: Moderate (muscle damage) / Limited (cycling recovery)**
**Pool: 36 studies, N not reported in abstract (younger versus older adults, muscle damage); cycling specific recovery studies are small single groups**
A 2025 meta analysis of 36 studies found no greater loss of muscle function after damaging exercise in older adults; soreness and creatine kinase rises were actually larger in the young (Fernandes et al., 2025). This weakens the view that older people recover from damage more slowly. Reviews note most studies do not separate aging from inactivity (Borges et al., 2016; Fell & Williams, 2008). After a 55 km trail run, masters runners recovered more slowly (Easthope et al., 2010). In cyclists, 3 consecutive days of 30 min time trials did not reduce performance in either age group, but veteran riders (about 45 years) reported more soreness, more fatigue and worse recovery (Fell et al., 2008). After HIIT (6 x 30 s at 175% of peak power), masters cyclists (about 56 years) actually showed faster parasympathetic reactivation than young cyclists (Borges et al., 2017). Heart rate and RPE load measures behaved the same in both ages (Borges et al., 2020). Summary: measured recovery shows little age effect; older riders may feel more fatigued, which matters for adherence even if performance holds.

### Training load tolerance and keeping intensity high
**Evidence: Moderate (observational) / Moderate (trials in non athletes)**
**Pool: 14 studies, about 429 people (healthy middle aged and older adults, interval versus steady training)**
Much of the age related decline may come from older athletes training less and easier (Tanaka & Seals, 2008); in the 20 year follow up, those who kept vigorous training lost less VO2max (Pollock et al., 1997). Reduced training studies in young people show intensity is key for keeping VO2max (Hickson et al., 1985; Spiering et al., 2021). In 14 trials (429 middle aged and older adults), interval training raised VO2max more than steady training, by about 1.1 mL/kg/min (Poon et al., 2021). These were not athletes, and there are few long term trials in masters cyclists. Keep regular hard work rather than drifting into only steady riding.

### Strength training helps older endurance athletes and maintenance needs a bigger dose
**Evidence: Strong (strength gains in older adults) / Limited (older endurance athletes)**
**Pool: 47 studies, about 1,079 people (adults 50 plus); 25 randomized trials, N not reported in abstract (adults 65 plus)**
In 40 masters athletes aged 40 to 81, thigh muscle and strength did not decline with age (cross sectional; Wroblewski et al., 2011). In 9 masters and 8 young endurance athletes, 3 weeks of knee extension training raised strength 17.8% versus 5.9% and improved cycling efficiency in masters (Louis et al., 2012). Older adults needed a larger dose than young adults to keep muscle size after a strength block (Bickel et al., 2011). Older people may need 2 sessions per week and 2 to 3 sets to maintain muscle size (Spiering et al., 2021). In 47 studies (1,079 adults 50 plus), strength training raised strength by about 24 to 33%, more with higher intensity (Peterson et al., 2010). In 25 trials in adults 65 plus, gains were largest with longer programs and about 70 to 79% of 1RM (Borde et al., 2015). The endurance athlete studies are small.

### Road cycling does little for bone, and masters cyclists can have low bone density
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 31 studies, N not reported in abstract (cyclists, mostly cross sectional); exercise for bone: 43 trials, about 4,320 people (postmenopausal women)**
A systematic review of 31 studies concluded road cycling does not appear to give a meaningful bone building benefit, and adult road cyclists in regular training often have low bone density at key sites such as the lumbar spine; mountain biking or other sports may help (Olmedillas et al., 2012). In male masters cyclists (about 51 years) with 20 years of racing and little weight bearing exercise, spine and hip bone density was lower than in age matched controls, and 15% had T scores below minus 2.5 (Nichols et al., 2003). In 101 postmenopausal women with low bone mass, 8 months of twice weekly supervised heavy resistance and impact training (5 x 5 above 85% 1RM) raised spine bone density 2.9% versus a 1.2% loss in controls (Watson et al., 2018). A Cochrane review of 43 trials (4,320 postmenopausal women) found strength training helped hip bone density and mixed programs helped the spine, with low quality reporting (Howe et al., 2011). A second review found 9 of 10 cycling studies linked competitive cycling with low bone density (Abrahin et al., 2016).

### Older adults need more protein per meal (anabolic resistance)
**Evidence: Moderate (daily total) / Limited (per meal amounts)**
**Pool: 49 studies, about 1,863 people (healthy adults doing strength training); 36 studies, about 1,682 people (non frail older adults)**
Pooled lab data showed muscle protein synthesis plateaued at about 0.40 g/kg body mass per meal in older men (about 71 years) versus about 0.24 g/kg in young men (Moore et al., 2015). The PROT-AGE group recommends at least 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/day for people over 65, and 1.2 g/kg/day or more for those who exercise (Bauer et al., 2013). A review argued masters endurance athletes may need more post exercise protein than younger athletes, with little direct data (Doering et al., 2016). These per meal numbers come from small acute lab studies; larger trial pools are more cautious. In 49 trials (1,863 people), extra protein added modestly to strength training gains, the benefit shrank with age, and nothing more was gained above about 1.6 g/kg/day (Morton et al., 2018). In 36 trials (1,682 non frail older adults), protein supplements did not improve muscle or strength, likely because most already ate enough (Ten Haaf et al., 2018). Practical meaning: hitting an adequate daily total matters more than supplements.

### Long term endurance athletes have higher atrial fibrillation risk, especially men
**Evidence: Moderate (observational)**
**Pool: 22 studies, about 656,750 people (exercise intensity and AF); athlete versus control meta analyses of 1,550, 8,901 and 9,113 people**
**Real world data: 52,755 male ski racers, Swedish registries, observational**
An early meta analysis of 6 case control studies (1,550 people, mean age 51, 93% men) found athletes had about 5 times the odds of AF compared with controls (Abdulla & Nielsen, 2009). Later meta analyses found odds ratios of 2.34 (9 studies, 8,901 people, highest in men and under 60; Li et al., 2018), 1.64 (8 studies, 9,113 people, raised under 54 but not clearly at 54 plus; Ayinde et al., 2018) and 2.46 (N not reported in abstract, higher under 55; Newman et al., 2021). Among 52,755 male Vasaloppet ski racers, those with the most races and fastest times had more arrhythmia, mainly AF and slow rhythms (Andersen et al., 2013). A meta analysis of 22 studies (656,750 people) found moderate activity lowered AF risk in both sexes, while vigorous exercise was linked to higher risk in men but lower risk in women (Mohanty et al., 2016). All observational; absolute risk for one person stays low, and exercise has large overall heart benefits.

### Medical screening before hard training
**Evidence: Moderate (expert consensus)**
**Pool: none, expert consensus**
The ACSM screening model bases the need for medical clearance on current activity level, signs or symptoms or known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, and the planned intensity (Riebe et al., 2015). It notes exercise is safe for most people and that cardiac events are often preceded by warning signs.

### Juniors (under 18): develop the athlete, manage load during growth
**Evidence: Moderate (consensus and large observational data) / Limited (cycling specific)**
**Pool: 71 study reports, about 9,241 athletes (participation patterns); 51 reports, 6,096 athletes; 6 studies, 5,736 youth athletes (injury)**
**Real world data: 1,345 Italian and 307 Belgian youth road cyclists, federation results, observational**
The IOC consensus calls for youth participation that stays inclusive, enjoyable and sustainable (Bergeron et al., 2015). The NSCA says all youth should build general athleticism, including supervised strength training, and that high competition volume without preparatory conditioning raises overuse injury risk (Lloyd et al., 2016). In 80 Norwegian juniors, future World Tour riders had raced more and had higher aerobic power at 18 (6.9 vs 6.2 W/kg) (Svendsen et al., 2018); this does not justify high volume in younger children. Larger data support broad development. In 9,241 athletes, adult world class athletes had more multisport practice as children, started their main sport later and progressed more slowly than national class peers, while the opposite predicted junior success (Barth et al., 2022; Gullich et al., 2022). In 5,736 youth athletes, early specializers had about 37% higher injury risk (Carder et al., 2020). In cycling, results before under 17 did not predict elite success in 307 Belgian riders (Mostaert et al., 2022), and predictive value grew from under 17 onward in 1,345 Italian riders (Gallo et al., 2022). All observational. On gearing, the UCI removed its junior road gear limit of 7.93 m per pedal revolution from 1 January 2023. Press reports said some federations (for example the Dutch and Belgian) planned to keep limits, and youth limits stay in some countries such as Britain (CyclingTips, 2022; check national rules).

## Rules for the coach
- Always ask age and sex. Use measured HRmax when available; otherwise estimate with 208 minus 0.7 x age, not 220 minus age. Prefer power zones based on a current FTP test over age formulas.
- Retest FTP every 6 to 8 weeks for riders over 50. Do not treat a small yearly drop as failure; about 5 to 10% decline in VO2max per decade is typical even in trained riders, and faster after 70.
- Keep high intensity at every age. For riders 40 plus with no medical restriction, include 1 to 2 sessions per week with work at or above 105% FTP (RPE 8 to 9), unless in a planned rest block.
- Recovery spacing by decade is a starting default from coaching practice (Pool: none, coaching convention). Measured muscle damage does not rise with age, so adjust to the rider's own recovery reports rather than age alone:
  - 40s: same structure as younger riders. 2 to 3 hard sessions per week. Recovery week every 4 weeks.
  - 50s: 2 hard sessions per week as default, a third only if sleep, mood and performance are good. At least 48 h between the two hardest sessions. Recovery week every 3 weeks.
  - 60s: 2 hard sessions per week, at least 48 to 72 h apart. Shorter VO2max sets (for example 4 to 6 x 2 to 3 min at 110 to 120% FTP). Recovery week every 3 weeks.
  - 70 plus: 1 to 2 hard sessions per week, built up gradually over several weeks. Emphasize balance, strength and enjoyment. Recovery week every 2 to 3 weeks.
- If an older rider reports more soreness or fatigue, add an extra easy day before reducing the intensity of key sessions. Ask about subjective recovery every week; older riders may feel worse even when performance is fine.
- Strength training for all riders 40 plus: 2 sessions per week, 2 to 3 sets per exercise, building to about 70 to 85% 1RM (about 6 to 10 reps near failure) once technique is solid; heavier loads for bone if cleared. In busy periods, never drop below 1 session per week.
- Bone: for road only cyclists, especially 50 plus and anyone with low bone density history, add impact or weight bearing work (jumps, hopping, stair work, running, or heavy lifting) at least 2 to 3 times per week if cleared to do so. Suggest asking a doctor about a bone density scan for long term road only riders over 50 or after a low impact fracture.
- Protein for riders 50 plus: first make sure the daily total is at least 1.2 g/kg; 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg/day is a reasonable target in heavy training, with little gain above 1.6. About 0.4 g/kg per meal across 3 to 4 meals is a sensible target from small lab studies. Do not push supplements on a rider who already eats enough. Include a protein serving after hard sessions. Riders with kidney disease must follow their doctor's advice on protein.
- Medical clearance language: before starting high intensity training, advise a check with a doctor for any rider who is currently inactive and over about 45, has known heart, lung, metabolic or kidney disease, or has symptoms. The coach must not diagnose.
- Stop and refer immediately (no hard training until seen by a doctor) if the rider reports chest pain or pressure, fainting or near fainting, unusual breathlessness, a racing, pounding or irregular heartbeat, an unexplained performance drop with high heart rate, or erratic heart rate readings. Say: "These symptoms need a doctor's review before more hard training. I can't tell what's causing them."
- Do not cut a rider's training simply because they are an endurance athlete with AF risk; overall exercise is strongly beneficial. If a rider has diagnosed AF or another heart condition, follow their cardiologist's limits.
- Juniors under 18:
  - Under 14: no structured interval blocks. Do not judge talent on under 15 results. Mostly varied riding, skills, other sports and games. Keep cycling to a share of total activity, with at least 1 to 2 rest days per week.
  - 14 to 16: introduce structure gradually. 1 to 2 hard sessions per week. Supervised strength training with good technique is encouraged.
  - 16 to 18: closer to adult structure, but increase weekly hours by no more than about 10% at a time, and keep at least 1 full rest day per week.
  - During growth spurts (fast height gain), hold or reduce load, watch for knee, heel and back pain, and refer persistent pain to a doctor or physio.
  - Check national federation gearing rules for youth riders before prescribing big gear work. Prefer high cadence skills over big gear strength efforts for under 16s.
  - Ask about enjoyment and other sports regularly. Avoid year round single sport specialization before mid teens.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- The rate of VO2max decline in trained versus sedentary people conflicts between longitudinal and cross sectional data; training cutbacks likely explain much of this.
- Most masters cycling data are cross sectional, small, and in men. Data in female masters cyclists are sparse.
- Recovery studies in older cyclists are small; the muscle damage meta analysis did not test cycling training. Spacing by decade is untested.
- Little long term trial evidence compares different intensity doses in masters cyclists.
- Bone data in cyclists are mostly cross sectional; the best intervention trial (LIFTMOR) was in postmenopausal women, not cyclists.
- AF evidence is observational, with possible confounding. The sex difference (higher risk in men with vigorous exercise) is based on few studies of women.
- Junior gearing rules vary by country. Claims that big gears harm growing knees are not well tested.
- Junior training recommendations come mostly from expert consensus and multisport observational data, not cycling specific trials.
- The large performance datasets are race results and general population registries. I found no peer reviewed cycling platform data on age and training.
- Protein per meal targets rest on small acute lab studies.
