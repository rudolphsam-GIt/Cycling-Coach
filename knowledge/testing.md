# FTP and Threshold Testing (20 Minute Test, Ramp Test, Critical Power, MLSS, LTHR, Zones)

## Summary

Functional threshold power (FTP) is meant to be the highest power a rider can hold in a roughly steady state for about an hour. Most riders estimate it as 95% of the average power from a 20 minute all out effort, or as 75% of the best 1 minute power from a ramp test. On average across groups of trained cyclists, the 20 minute method lands close to lab markers such as the lactate threshold and the maximal lactate steady state (MLSS), and a 20 minute test is very repeatable (about 3% variation test to test). For an individual rider, though, the error is large, often 5 to 10% or more either way, the 0.95 factor overestimates 60 minute power for some riders, and most riders cannot actually hold FTP for 60 minutes (typical time to exhaustion is about 35 to 50 minutes). The ramp test 75% factor is an app convention with no published validation we could find.

Critical power (CP) is the best supported physiological boundary between sustainable (heavy) and unsustainable (severe) exercise. It is estimated from 2 to 5 maximal efforts of different lengths (for example 3, 7 and 12 minutes) and comes with W', the fixed amount of work available above CP. CP sits a little above FTP and MLSS on average and can be held for about 20 to 30 minutes. For an app, FTP is a practical anchor for zones, but the coach should treat it as an estimate with a margin of about plus or minus 5%, retest under the same conditions, and use the rider's response (RPE, heart rate drift, ability to finish sessions) to adjust it.

## Key findings

### The 20 minute test with a 0.95 factor matches lab thresholds on average, but not for every individual
**Evidence: Moderate**
In 23 trained men, FTP from the 20 minute test (FTP20), actual 60 minute time trial power (FTP60) and the individual anaerobic threshold did not differ on average and were strongly correlated (r = 0.61 to 0.88). Mean bias was small (1 to 5 W), but individual limits of agreement were wide, from about plus or minus 35 W up to about plus or minus 60 W, and the authors said FTP20 and FTP60 should not be used interchangeably for an individual (Borszcz et al., 2018). In 15 trained and well trained cyclists, FTP was within 1.4% of MLSS power on average with a typical error of 4.7%, but 95% limits of agreement of plus or minus 9.2%; agreement was better in well trained (plus or minus 7.4%) than in trained riders (plus or minus 11.8%) (Klitzke Borszcz et al., 2019). In 20 male cyclists, FTP did not differ from the lactate threshold in trained riders (peak power above 4.5 W/kg), but was 6.5% lower than lactate threshold in recreational riders, so FTP may underestimate threshold in less fit riders (Valenzuela et al., 2018). In 46 road cyclists, FTP correlated strongly with several lactate markers (Dmax, modified Dmax, 4 mmol/L), but differed from the 2 mmol/L and baseline plus 1 mmol/L markers (Sitko et al., 2022a).

### The 0.95 factor can overestimate MLSS and 60 minute power
**Evidence: Limited**
In 11 trained cyclists and triathletes, MLSS was 26 W below 20 minute power; 95% of 20 minute power still overestimated MLSS by about 12 W, and the best average factor was 91% (Lillo-Beviá et al., 2022). In 8 trained male cyclists, 60 minute mean power was 309 W and 20 minute power was about 343 W, a ratio of about 0.90, not 0.95 (calculated from MacInnis et al., 2019). In the same study, both 20 minute (R squared 0.92) and 4 minute (R squared 0.95) power strongly predicted 60 minute power. Samples are small, but the direction is consistent: a single 0.95 factor runs high for some riders.

### Most riders cannot hold FTP for an hour
**Evidence: Moderate**
Time to exhaustion at FTP20 averaged 50.9 minutes with a wide spread (plus or minus 15.7 minutes) (Borszcz et al., 2018). In another group of 13 cyclists it averaged 33.7 minutes, blood lactate kept rising to 6.7 mmol/L, and the authors concluded FTP is not a valid marker of the heavy to severe boundary (Wong et al., 2022). Time to exhaustion at FTP rises with fitness level: median 35 minutes in recreational, 42 in trained, 47 in well trained and 51 in professional cyclists (Sitko et al., 2022b). FTP was 57 to 60 W below the respiratory compensation point in 15 cyclists regardless of warm up (Barranco-Gil et al., 2020).

### The 20 minute test is reliable when the protocol is held constant
**Evidence: Moderate**
With a familiarization trial, the standard protocol (about 45 minute warm up including a 5 minute effort, then 20 minutes all out) gave a test to test variation of 2.9% (intraclass correlation 0.97) in 25 trained cyclists (Klitzke Borszcz et al., 2020). Repeated 20 minute tests had a standard error of about 3 W and a smallest detectable change of about 9 W in 11 trained riders (Lillo-Beviá et al., 2022), and a typical error of 4.6 W in 8 trained riders (MacInnis et al., 2019). The warm up changes the result: 20 minute power was about 5% higher (270 vs 256 W) after a short or self selected warm up than after the standard warm up containing a 5 minute hard effort, mainly because pacing changed (Tramontin et al., 2022). A meta-analytic review of performance tests found typical variation of about 0.9 to 2.0% for constant power tests from 1 minute to 3 hours in athletes, larger variation in non athletes, and about 1.2% improvement from the first to the second trial due to learning (Hopkins et al., 2001).

### Ramp tests and the 75% factor
**Evidence: Limited (the 75% factor is convention)**
App ramp tests estimate FTP as 75% of the best 1 minute power reached in a stepwise ramp to exhaustion (TrainerRoad, n.d.). We found no peer reviewed study validating the 75% factor. Related lab work shows peak power from an incremental test, together with VO2max, predicted FTP well (r = 0.93) in 40 mixed trained and untrained adults (Denham et al., 2020), and a regression using peak power, body mass and power at 4 mmol/L lactate predicted FTP in 70 triathletes (McGrath et al., 2022). Peak incremental power is one of the most reliable measures (about 0.9% variation; Hopkins et al., 2001), but the ratio between ramp peak and threshold differs between riders. Riders with high anaerobic capacity will tend to get an FTP that is too high from a ramp, and very aerobic riders one that is too low; this is reasoning from physiology, not a tested finding.

### Critical power and W' describe the power duration relationship
**Evidence: Strong (concept), Moderate (field estimation)**
For efforts of about 2 to 30 minutes, time to exhaustion falls on a hyperbolic curve described by CP (the highest power sustainable without steadily using up a limited reserve) and W' (the size of that reserve, in kJ); time to exhaustion = W' / (power minus CP) (Jones et al., 2010). CP separates the heavy domain, where oxygen uptake and lactate can stabilize, from the severe domain, where they cannot and VO2max is reached (Poole et al., 2016). Jones et al. (2019) argue CP, not MLSS, is the true maximal metabolic steady state; MLSS as normally measured falls somewhat below CP, and exercise at CP is sustainable for only about 20 to 30 minutes.

A 3 minute all out test gave end power within about 5 W of CP in 8 of 10 active people (Vanhatalo et al., 2007), but in 6 elite track cyclists it overestimated CP by about 50 W (402 vs 351 W) and underestimated W' (Bartram et al., 2017). Field CP from 3, 7 and 12 minute maximal efforts agreed closely with lab CP (Karsten et al., 2014) and was reliable (variation 2.4 to 6.5%); CP taken from the best 3, 7 and 12 minute powers in training and racing data had a standard error of 5.2% and variation of 3.5% (Karsten et al., 2015). A review of power profiling recommends multi effort CP testing with set rules (number of trials, recovery, terrain) and avoiding single effort predictions such as FTP (Leo et al., 2022).

### How FTP, MLSS and CP relate
**Evidence: Moderate**
On average the order is roughly MLSS at or slightly below FTP, and CP a little above FTP. CP exceeded FTP by 7 W on average in 17 trained cyclists, with individual limits of agreement from minus 19 to plus 33 W, so the two should not be swapped for an individual (Karsten et al., 2020). MLSS is defined as the highest constant power where blood lactate rises no more than 1 mmol/L between minute 10 and minute 30 (Beneke, 2003). Time to exhaustion at MLSS averaged about 38 minutes in moderately trained men (Fontana et al., 2009) and about 50 minutes in another group, with poor test to test reliability of that time (variation 24.6%) (Faude et al., 2017). So "the power you can hold for an hour" is not the same as MLSS or CP for most riders; all three describe a zone near the top of steady state, not a single exact number.

### Zone systems
**Evidence: Moderate (3 zone physiology), Limited (7 zone percentages are convention)**
Descriptive studies of elite endurance athletes show about 80% of sessions at low intensity (below the first lactate threshold, about 2 mmol/L) and about 20% at high intensity, which underlies the 3 zone model: zone 1 below the first threshold, zone 2 between the first and second threshold, zone 3 above the second threshold (Seiler, 2010). A review of intensity prescription found fixed percentages of maximal values (VO2max, max heart rate, peak power) do not reliably put different people in the same physiological domain; the first lactate or ventilatory threshold is supported for the moderate to heavy boundary, and CP is the best supported marker of the heavy to severe boundary (Jamnick et al., 2020). The widely used Coggan 7 zone system sets zones as fixed % FTP (zone 1 below 55%, zone 2 56 to 75%, zone 3 76 to 90%, zone 4 91 to 105%, zone 5 106 to 120%, zone 6 121 to 150%, zone 7 maximal sprint). These cut offs come from coaching practice, not validation studies.

### Lactate threshold heart rate (LTHR)
**Evidence: Limited (convention)**
A common field method (popularized in coaching books, not tested in the studies found here) is to ride a solo 30 minute all out effort and take the average heart rate of the last 20 minutes as LTHR. Heart rate at FTP matched heart rate at MLSS within about 1.4% on average, but with individual limits of agreement of plus or minus 8.2% (Klitzke Borszcz et al., 2019). Heart rate at a fixed power rises with heat, dehydration, fatigue and duration, so LTHR is a useful cross check rather than a precise anchor.

### How often to retest
**Evidence: Limited (convention)**
Retesting every 4 to 8 weeks, or at the start and end of each training block, is coaching convention. The reliability data give a useful filter: a change smaller than about 3% (or about 9 W for a 300 W rider) between two 20 minute tests is within normal test to test noise (Klitzke Borszcz et al., 2020; Lillo-Beviá et al., 2022).

## Rules for the coach

- Treat any FTP as an estimate with about plus or minus 5% uncertainty. Show it as a single number for zones, but describe threshold work as a range (95 to 105% FTP).
- 20 minute test protocol: 15 to 20 minute warm up, then the same warm up every time (choose either the standard warm up with a 5 minute hard effort or a short warm up, and never switch between them). Then 20 minutes all out, evenly paced, on the same trainer or same road. FTP = 0.95 x average 20 minute power.
- For riders who are new to testing, count the first test as familiarization; expect about 1 to 2% improvement on the second test from learning alone.
- If a rider's FTP came from a 20 minute test and they fail to complete 2 x 20 minutes at 95 to 100% FTP at RPE 7 to 8 out of 10, or blow up during threshold sessions repeatedly, lower FTP by 3 to 5% (factor 0.90 to 0.92 instead of 0.95).
- Recreational riders (roughly below 4.5 W/kg at ramp peak) may have an FTP from the 20 minute test that is slightly low relative to lab threshold; if threshold sessions feel easy (RPE 5 or less out of 10 at 100% FTP), raise FTP by 2 to 3%.
- Ramp test: FTP = 0.75 x best 1 minute power, as a convention. Flag riders with a strong sprint or short power profile (ramp FTP may be too high) and very aerobic, time trial riders (ramp FTP may be too low). For these riders, confirm with a 20 minute test or a CP test within 2 weeks.
- Do not compare FTP from a ramp test with FTP from a 20 minute test as if they were the same measurement. Track each test type separately.
- Do not assume FTP can be held for 60 minutes. Expect 35 to 50 minutes at 100% FTP, longer in more trained riders. For 40 km time trials or hour efforts, target about 95 to 100% FTP depending on fitness and duration.
- Critical power option: ride maximal efforts of 3, 7 and 12 minutes (30 minutes easy recovery between them on one day, or on separate days), then fit CP and W'. Use this when a rider wants W' for pacing or when FTP seems off.
- Do not use the 3 minute all out test for well trained or elite riders; it can overestimate CP by a large amount.
- Estimating from ride data: if the rider has true maximal efforts in the last 4 to 6 weeks, use best 3, 7 and 12 minute powers to estimate CP, or best 20 minute power x 0.95 for FTP. Only use efforts the rider confirms were all out. Flag the estimate as lower confidence than a formal test.
- Retest every 4 to 8 weeks, at the end of a build block, after an easy (recovery) week, and after any break of 2 weeks or more. Test when rested (no hard session in the previous 48 hours).
- Only change zones when a new test differs from the old one by 3% or more. Smaller changes are within test noise unless supported by session data.
- If session data show the rider repeatedly completing threshold intervals at RPE 6 or less with stable heart rate, suggest a retest early rather than guessing a new FTP.
- LTHR: set from a solo 30 minute all out effort (average heart rate of the final 20 minutes). Use it as a cross check and for riders without a power meter. Heart rate in hot conditions, when dehydrated or late in long rides runs higher at the same power, so do not lower power targets just because heart rate is above zone in those conditions.
- Default zone system for the app: Coggan 7 zones by % FTP (zone 1 below 55%, zone 2 56 to 75%, zone 3 76 to 90%, zone 4 91 to 105%, zone 5 106 to 120%, zone 6 121 to 150%, zone 7 maximal). For intensity distribution planning, map to 3 zones: low (below about 75% FTP, the approximate first threshold), moderate (about 76 to 100% FTP), high (above 100% FTP). The 75% boundary is approximate; if lab or lactate data give the first threshold, use that instead.
- Always label zones as based on FTP from a specific test type and date, so old or mixed values are visible.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed

- The 0.95 factor is an average. In one small sample the 60 minute to 20 minute power ratio was about 0.90, and the best average factor for MLSS in another small sample was 0.91, so 0.95 may run high for many riders. Individual ratios were not reported in the abstracts reviewed.
- Whether FTP is a true physiological threshold is disputed. Some researchers argue it sits below the heavy to severe boundary and should not be treated as one (Wong et al., 2022; Leo et al., 2022); others find it a practical stand in for MLSS in trained riders (Klitzke Borszcz et al., 2019).
- The ramp test 75% factor has no published validation that we could find; it comes from app documentation.
- Whether CP or MLSS is the true maximal metabolic steady state is an ongoing debate (Jones et al., 2019).
- Most validation samples are small (8 to 25 riders), mostly male, and mostly trained rather than recreational or elite. Data in women and masters riders are scarce.
- The 4 to 8 week retest interval and the 30 minute LTHR method are convention without direct testing.
- The 7 zone % FTP boundaries are not validated; they do not guarantee the same physiological domain for every rider.
- Field CP estimates depend on efforts being truly maximal, on recovery between efforts, and on terrain; W' estimates are much less precise than CP.
