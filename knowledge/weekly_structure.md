# Structuring the Training Week

## Summary
A training week (a "microcycle") is the basic building block of any plan. The research gives a few clear anchors and leaves a lot to coaching judgment. The anchors are: most riding should be easy, with a small number of hard sessions; after a hard interval day the body needs roughly 48 hours before it is fully ready for another one, while easy rides cause little lasting fatigue; at least about 3 sessions per week are needed to keep improving once a rider is reasonably fit; and fitness can be held for weeks on as few as 2 sessions per week if those sessions stay hard. Long rides build the kind of endurance that short daily rides do not.

What the research does not settle is the exact layout: which weekday gets the intervals, whether the long ride is on Saturday or Sunday, or how many rest days to take. These are mostly coaching convention shaped by work and family time. This file gives templates for 3, 4, 5 and 6 riding days per week and marks which parts come from evidence and which are convention.

Intensity terms used here (approximate, convention): easy or endurance 56 to 75% FTP (RPE 2 to 4), tempo 76 to 90% FTP (RPE 5 to 6), threshold 91 to 105% FTP (RPE 7), VO2max intervals 106 to 120% FTP (RPE 8 to 9), sprints or anaerobic above 120% FTP (RPE 9 to 10).

## Key findings

### Keep most sessions easy and limit hard sessions to about 2 per week in normal weeks
**Evidence: Moderate**
Descriptive data from successful endurance athletes show about 80% of sessions at low intensity and about 20% containing hard work, and adding more HIT to already well trained athletes has not shown long term gains (Seiler, 2010). Champion skiers did about 500 sessions per year with roughly 90% of training time below the first lactate threshold and about 23% of sessions classed as HIT (Tønnessen et al., 2014). Trials in trained cyclists that produced good gains used about 2 HIT sessions per week mixed with high volumes of easy riding (Sylta et al., 2016; Rønnestad et al., 2014a). In a 9 week trial of well trained endurance athletes (runners, cyclists, triathletes, skiers), a polarized program (mostly easy plus HIT) improved VO2peak more than threshold focused, HIT only or high volume only programs (Stöggl and Sperlich, 2014), though this was a small, mixed sport study.

### Recovery after hard sessions takes about 48 hours; easy riding recovers within a day
**Evidence: Moderate**
A review of heart rate based recovery found complete cardiac autonomic recovery takes up to 24 h after low intensity exercise, 24 to 48 h after threshold intensity, and at least 48 h after high intensity exercise, with fitter people recovering faster and duration mattering less than intensity (Stanley, Peake and Buchheit, 2013). In highly trained runners, 60 to 120 min below the first ventilatory threshold caused almost no autonomic disturbance, while threshold and interval sessions caused a similar, larger delay, and less trained people recovered more slowly after the same intervals (Seiler, Haugen and Kuffel, 2007). After cycling repeated sprints (10x10 s), neuromuscular fatigue was fully restored after 48 h but not after 24 h (Milioni et al., 2021, n = 10). After 6x30 s cycling intervals, physical performance in trained young and masters cyclists recovered at similar rates, but masters riders felt less recovered and more sore at 48 h (Borges et al., 2018, n = 17). Note that heart rate recovery is not the same as full recovery of energy stores or muscle (Stanley, Peake and Buchheit, 2013).

### Refuelling supports back to back training, but I could not verify a precise glycogen recovery time
**Evidence: Moderate (for the guidance) / Limited (for exact hours)**
Nutrition reviews recommend matching daily carbohydrate to the training load (in g per kg body mass), starting carbohydrate intake soon after the session when the gap to the next session is under 8 h, and spreading meals more freely when the recovery period is about 24 h (Burke, Kiens and Ivy, 2004; Burke et al., 2011). The abstracts reviewed do not give a fixed number of hours for full glycogen restoration, so the app should not state one as fact. The common claim that hard days need 48 to 72 h for glycogen alone is not supported by what was checked here; the 48 h figure above comes from autonomic and neuromuscular data.

### Alternating hard and easy days reduces "monotony", which is linked to illness
**Evidence: Limited**
In 25 experienced athletes, a high percentage of illnesses happened when athletes exceeded individual thresholds of training strain, where strain combines weekly load and monotony (how similar each day's load is) (Foster, 1998). This supports varying daily load (hard days truly hard, easy days truly easy) rather than moderate effort every day. It is an observational study and did not test specific weekly layouts.

### Back to back hard days and "crash" blocks can work in short doses for trained athletes
**Evidence: Moderate**
Trained cyclists who did 5 HIT sessions in one week (then 1 per week for 3 weeks) gained more than those spreading 2 per week (Rønnestad, Hansen and Ellefsen, 2014a). Elite junior alpine skiers did 15 HIT sessions (4x4 min at 90 to 95% HRmax) in 11 days and improved VO2max by 6%, but jump power was still lower 7 days later, suggesting lasting muscle fatigue (Breil et al., 2010). Elite cyclists did 6 consecutive days of HIT then a 5 day taper and improved more than with a taper alone (Rønnestad and Vikmoen, 2019). These blocks were always followed by several easier days. In triathletes, pushing overload far enough to cause a real drop in performance (functional overreaching) removed the benefit and raised infection rates (Aubry et al., 2014). All of these studies are small and mostly in trained to elite athletes.

### Frequency: at least 3 sessions per week to improve; 2 hard sessions per week can maintain
**Evidence: Limited**
A pooled review of mostly non athletes found that 2 sessions per week can improve less fit people, but once VO2max is above about 50 mL/kg/min, at least 3 sessions per week were needed (Wenger and Bell, 1986). In recently trained young adults, VO2max was maintained for 15 weeks when frequency dropped from 6 to 2 or 4 days per week at the same intensity and duration (Hickson and Rosenkoetter, 1981), and when daily duration was cut by up to two thirds (Hickson et al., 1982). Cutting intensity instead lost fitness (Hickson et al., 1985). These are old, small studies in non cyclists, so treat them as directional.

### Long sessions matter for long event endurance
**Evidence: Limited**
When daily training was cut from 40 to 13 minutes, VO2max and 5 minute endurance were kept, but endurance of 2 h or more fell by about 10% (Hickson et al., 1982). Elite endurance athletes accumulate large volumes of long, easy training (Seiler, 2010; Tønnessen et al., 2014). This supports keeping a weekly long ride for riders targeting events longer than about 2 h. Putting it on the weekend is coaching convention based on available time.

### Same total volume: more frequent sessions vs fewer longer ones
**Evidence: Limited**
Direct trials in trained cyclists comparing, for example, 5x1 h vs 3x1 h 40 min at matched volume were not found. One study in untrained men (one leg knee extension) found training twice every second day, with the second session started at low glycogen, gave greater gains in endurance time and some enzymes than training once daily at the same total work (Hansen et al., 2005). That is a lab model, not a cycling program, and it is mainly about low glycogen training. The practical reasoning for cyclists is: frequency of at least 3 to 4 sessions per week matters for fitter riders (Wenger and Bell, 1986), while one longer ride per week covers long endurance (Hickson et al., 1982).

### Rest days
**Evidence: Limited**
No trial was found that tests the number of full rest days for amateur cyclists. Champion skiers trained almost every day and rarely took rest days even before key races (Tønnessen et al., 2014), but they train full time. Because easy riding causes little autonomic disturbance in trained athletes (Seiler, Haugen and Kuffel, 2007), an easy ride can often replace a rest day for experienced riders. One to two rest days per week for time limited amateurs is convention, and is mostly dictated by schedule.

## Rules for the coach
- Weekly intensity mix: at least 75 to 80% of sessions easy (56 to 75% FTP, RPE 2 to 4). Hard sessions (threshold or VO2max, RPE 7 to 9): 1 per week for 3 day weeks or beginners, 2 per week by default, 3 maximum in normal weeks for experienced riders.
- Space hard sessions at least 48 h apart in normal weeks (for example Tuesday and Thursday, or Tuesday and Saturday). For riders over about 50, or those reporting high soreness or poor sleep, allow 72 h.
- The day after a hard session is either rest or easy riding only (under 75% FTP, RPE 2 to 4, under 90 min unless it is the long ride).
- Easy rides under about 2 h at RPE 2 to 4 do not need a recovery day after them and can be placed on any day.
- Back to back hard days: allowed only for riders with at least 1 year of structured training, at most 2 consecutive hard days in normal weeks, and always followed by at least 1 rest or easy day.
- HIT blocks ("crash" weeks): at most 1 block every 4 to 8 weeks, 4 to 6 HIT sessions in 5 to 7 days, VO2max sessions at 106 to 120% FTP (for example 5x4 min or 5x5 min, RPE 8 to 9), followed by 3 to 5 easy or rest days. Stop the block early if interval power drops more than about 5% within a session or RPE for the same power rises by 2 or more points.
- Long ride: one per week for riders targeting events over 2 h, 1.5 to 2.5 times the length of the typical weekday ride, mostly at 56 to 75% FTP. Default it to Saturday or Sunday (convention), and do not place a VO2max session the day before.
- Minimum frequency: 3 riding days per week to make progress; 2 sessions per week (with hard efforts kept) can hold fitness for a few weeks during busy periods.
- Weekday time limited riders (45 to 75 min): use weekday slots for the hard sessions (intervals fit short sessions) and easy spins; put volume on the weekend.
- Rest days: at least 1 full rest day per week for riders on 3 to 5 days; 6 day riders can use 1 rest day or 1 very easy short day. If the rider reports persistent fatigue, add a rest day before cutting hard sessions.
- Fuel: on days with hard or long sessions, and when the next session is less than about 8 h away, prompt the rider to eat carbohydrate soon after the session. Do not quote a fixed hours figure for glycogen recovery.
- Keep strength or other sports out of the 24 h before a key interval session where possible (convention).
- If the rider loses a day, drop an easy ride first, then shorten the long ride, and keep the hard sessions. If two hard sessions would end up less than 48 h apart, drop or downgrade the second one to tempo or endurance.

### Example templates (Monday to Sunday)
These layouts follow the evidence on intensity mix, 48 h spacing and the long ride. The exact days are convention, built around a Monday to Friday work week. Swap days freely if the 48 h spacing and hard then easy order are kept.

**3 days per week (beginner or very busy, about 3 to 6 h)**
- Mon: rest
- Tue: hard. Threshold or VO2max, 60 to 75 min (for example 3x10 min at 91 to 100% FTP, RPE 7, or 5x3 min at 106 to 115% FTP, RPE 8)
- Wed: rest
- Thu: endurance with some tempo, 60 to 75 min (for example 2x15 min at 76 to 88% FTP, RPE 5 to 6)
- Fri: rest
- Sat: rest or optional short spin
- Sun: long ride, 2 to 3.5 h at 56 to 75% FTP (RPE 2 to 4)
Notes: with only 3 days, one session can be hard and the others moderate. A second true hard session (Thursday) is fine for fitter riders if the long ride stays easy.

**4 days per week (about 5 to 8 h)**
- Mon: rest
- Tue: hard. VO2max or threshold, 60 to 90 min
- Wed: rest or optional 45 min easy
- Thu: hard. Threshold or tempo, 60 to 90 min (for example 2x20 min at 88 to 95% FTP, RPE 6 to 7)
- Fri: rest
- Sat: endurance 1.5 to 2 h at 56 to 75% FTP, optional short sprints
- Sun: long ride 2.5 to 4 h at 56 to 75% FTP
Notes: Tuesday and Thursday are 48 h apart. The weekend is easy volume, so the 2 hard sessions are 20 to 25% of sessions.

**5 days per week (about 7 to 11 h)**
- Mon: rest
- Tue: hard. VO2max, 60 to 90 min (for example 5x4 min at 106 to 120% FTP, RPE 8 to 9)
- Wed: easy 60 to 75 min at 56 to 70% FTP
- Thu: hard. Threshold, 60 to 90 min
- Fri: rest
- Sat: long ride 3 to 4.5 h at 56 to 75% FTP, optionally including 20 to 40 min of tempo
- Sun: endurance 1.5 to 2.5 h at 56 to 75% FTP
Notes: Saturday long then Sunday endurance is a common weekend pairing (convention). If a group ride on Saturday is hard, count it as the second hard session and make Thursday easy.

**6 days per week (experienced, about 9 to 15 h)**
- Mon: rest
- Tue: hard. VO2max, 75 to 90 min
- Wed: easy 60 to 90 min
- Thu: hard. Threshold or race specific, 75 to 90 min
- Fri: easy 45 to 60 min, optional 4 to 6 short sprints or openers
- Sat: long ride 3.5 to 5 h at 56 to 75% FTP, or a hard group ride or race (then make Thursday easy)
- Sun: endurance 2 to 3 h at 56 to 75% FTP
Notes: a third hard session is allowed only for experienced riders who recover well, and never on 3 consecutive days.

**HIT block week (experienced riders only, used as one week in a block plan)**
- Mon: hard (VO2max). Tue: hard (VO2max). Wed: easy 60 min. Thu: hard (VO2max). Fri: hard (VO2max). Sat: easy 1.5 to 2 h. Sun: hard (VO2max) or easy, then 3 to 5 easier days. This follows the shape of the block studies (5 HIT sessions in a week; Rønnestad, Hansen and Ellefsen, 2014a) but the exact day order is convention.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- No trials compare specific weekly layouts (which days get intervals, Saturday vs Sunday long ride). Templates are convention built on general principles.
- Recovery timelines come mostly from heart rate variability data, small samples, runners and sprint protocols. Recovery after long threshold or VO2max cycling sessions has not been mapped well.
- A precise glycogen recovery time was not verified from sources in this review; the 48 to 72 h figure often quoted for glycogen is not supported here.
- Evidence on minimum frequency comes from old studies in untrained or recently trained people and pooled data mostly from non athletes.
- Frequency vs session length at matched volume has not been tested in trained cyclists. The one relevant study used single leg knee extension in untrained men.
- The number of rest days needed by amateurs is not studied; it depends heavily on life stress, sleep and age.
- Masters riders may need more time between hard sessions, but the evidence is mainly about feeling less recovered rather than measured performance (Borges et al., 2018).
- The % FTP and RPE ranges are practical translations, not values used in most of the cited studies.
