# Strength Training for Cyclists (Heavy Lifting, Concurrent Training, Maintenance, Bone Health)

## Summary

Adding heavy lower body strength training to normal bike training improves cycling performance in trained cyclists. Across controlled studies, riders who lifted 2 times per week for roughly 10 to 25 weeks improved 40 to 45 minute all out power, maximal aerobic power and sprint power more than riders who only rode. The gains seem to come from better cycling efficiency, better short power, and an ability to hold a higher share of VO2max for long efforts, not from a higher VO2max. Heavy loads (about 4 to 10 reps per set, close to the heaviest load the rider can lift for that many reps) have the best evidence in cycling. Explosive and plyometric work has less and more mixed support. The effect is moderate in size, the studies are small (often 6 to 12 riders per group), and much of the data comes from one Norwegian research group.

Lifting and riding in the same program does not seem to blunt maximal strength or muscle size much in recent meta-analyses, but explosive strength gains can be reduced when both are done in the same session, and very high riding volume does reduce strength gains. Once strength is built, 1 heavy session per week keeps it during the race season, while stopping lifting loses the benefit within about 8 weeks. Road cyclists, especially masters riders, often have low bone mineral density because cycling does not load the skeleton. Heavy lifting plus impact work (jumps) is the most practical way to protect bone.

## Key findings

### Heavy strength training improves time trial and long effort performance in trained cyclists
**Evidence: Moderate**
A 2026 meta-analysis of 17 studies (262 cyclists, 60 female, mean VO2max about 61 mL/kg/min) found heavy strength training (at or above 80% of 1 rep max, 1 to 3 sessions per week, 5 to 25 weeks) improved cycling performance (time to exhaustion and time trial combined, effect size 0.46), cycling efficiency (effect size 0.35) and anaerobic power (effect size 0.56) compared with riding only. It did not change VO2max, power at VO2max or maximal metabolic steady state. The authors rated the certainty of evidence as low (Llanos-Lagos et al., 2026). An earlier review concluded that the most compelling evidence for an added benefit to cycling performance comes from heavy, not explosive, strength training, and that heavy training is the recommended method for improving cycling economy (Rønnestad & Mujika, 2014). A 2010 systematic review of 5 randomized trials in highly trained cyclists found that the 3 studies showing improved performance replaced part of the riding with resistance training, while the 2 that simply added lifting on top of full riding volume found no improvement (Yamamoto et al., 2010).

Individual trials in well trained riders: 12 weeks of 2 weekly sessions (4 lower body exercises, 3 sets of 4 to 10 RM) improved 40 minute all out power and power at 2 mmol/L lactate, and increased thigh muscle size without increasing body mass (Rønnestad et al., 2010a, n = 11 vs 9). In young elite cyclists, 25 weeks of heavy strength training improved maximal aerobic power and 40 minute all out power more than riding alone, with no change in VO2max or cycling economy; the 40 minute gain was linked to reaching peak torque earlier in the pedal stroke (Rønnestad et al., 2015, n = 9 vs 7). 8 weeks of half squats (4 sets of 4 RM, 3 times per week) improved cycling economy by 4.8% and time to exhaustion at maximal aerobic power by 17% with no change in body weight (Sunde et al., 2010, n = 8 vs 5).

### Cycling economy and holding a higher share of VO2max are the likely mechanisms
**Evidence: Moderate**
In 19 well trained female cyclists, 11 weeks of heavy strength training (2 times per week, 4 exercises, 3 sets of 4 to 10 RM) improved 40 minute all out power, cycling economy and the fraction of VO2max sustained during the trial; VO2max did not change. Gains in performance correlated with gains in quadriceps size (r = 0.73) and with a shift away from fast IIX fibers toward IIA fibers (Vikmoen et al., 2016). A review comparing the same group's male and female studies concluded the benefit is similar in both sexes, women may get a larger economy gain, and that riders should not avoid an increase in muscle size since it appears to be part of how the benefit works (Vikmoen & Rønnestad, 2021). Proposed mechanisms include delayed recruitment of less efficient type II fibers, fiber type shifts toward IIA, better neuromuscular efficiency and faster force development (Rønnestad & Mujika, 2014; Mujika et al., 2016).

### Heavy vs explosive vs plyometric training
**Evidence: Limited**
Direct comparisons in cyclists are scarce. Rønnestad and Mujika (2014) found the strongest cycling evidence for heavy loads. In 15 competitive road cyclists, 20 weeks of combined maximal and explosive strength training improved maximal strength, bike sprint power and power at VO2max, without reported harm (Beattie et al., 2017, n = 6 vs 9). Replacing 37% of riding with explosive strength work for 9 weeks preserved short sprint performance but did not improve time trial performance versus riding only (Bastiaans et al., 2001, n = 6 vs 8). Replacing part of in season training with explosive single leg jumps plus high resistance, low cadence (60 to 70 rpm) bike sprints for 4 to 5 weeks improved 1 km and 4 km time trial power by about 8% (Paton & Hopkins, 2005, n = 9 vs 9), but the bike sprints make it impossible to credit the jumps alone. Practical reading: heavy lifting is the base; explosive and plyometric work is an optional add on, mainly for sprint and bone goals.

### Concurrent training interference is small for maximal strength and muscle size
**Evidence: Moderate**
An early meta-analysis of 21 studies found that adding endurance training reduced strength, power and muscle growth compared with strength training alone, and that interference grew with the frequency and duration of endurance sessions. Running caused more interference than cycling (Wilson et al., 2012). A larger updated meta-analysis of 43 studies found no meaningful loss of maximal strength (standardized mean difference -0.06) or muscle size (-0.01) from concurrent training, but a small loss of explosive strength (-0.28). The explosive loss appeared when both were done in the same session, not when sessions were separated by at least 3 hours, and did not depend on cycling vs running, age or training status (Schumann et al., 2022). In trained cyclists specifically, doing heavy strength training on top of a high riding volume gave smaller gains in strength, muscle size, jump height and force development than the same lifting program done by non endurance trained people (Rønnestad et al., 2012). So interference is real for very high volume riders, but it does not cancel the performance benefit shown in the trials above.

### Order and spacing of lifting and riding on the same day
**Evidence: Limited**
A meta-analysis of 10 studies found that lifting before endurance work in the same session gave about 7% more lower body dynamic strength gain than the reverse order, with no effect of order on muscle size, static strength, VO2max or body fat (Eddens et al., 2018). Separating sessions by 3 or more hours removed the explosive strength penalty seen when both are in one session (Schumann et al., 2022). A 2026 narrative review on cyclists stresses that the benefit depends on intensity, how close sets go to failure, and the time gap from endurance sessions (de Pablos et al., 2026). No cycling trial has tested whether lifting the day before key intervals harms those intervals; keeping heavy leg days away from key bike sessions is coaching convention.

### One session per week maintains gains in season; stopping loses them
**Evidence: Limited**
After 12 weeks of 2 sessions per week, well trained cyclists who switched to 1 heavy session per week for the first 13 weeks of the race season kept their gains in muscle size and strength, and further improved maximal aerobic power and 40 minute power more than riders who only rode (Rønnestad et al., 2010b, n = 6 vs 6). In elite cyclists, stopping strength training for 8 weeks at the start of the season after 25 weeks of lifting led to losses in squat jump, maximal aerobic power and 30 second sprint power compared with riders who never lifted (Rønnestad et al., 2016, n = 7 vs 7). Rønnestad and Mujika (2014) recommend about 1 low volume, high intensity session per week in season.

### Program design that worked in trials
**Evidence: Moderate (for the general recipe), Limited (for exact numbers)**
The best studied protocol uses 4 lower body exercises (half squat, leg press one leg at a time, one legged standing hip flexion, and toe raise), 3 sets of 4 to 10 RM, 2 times per week for 10 to 12 weeks (Rønnestad et al., 2010a; protocol listing from a congress summary of that work). The review by Rønnestad and Mujika (2014) advises 2 sessions per week in the build period in a daily undulating pattern, 2 to 3 sets of 4 to 10 RM with about 2 to 3 minutes rest, moving the lifting phase as fast as possible even when the bar moves slowly, learning technique with light loads first, and reducing riding load for the first 2 to 3 weeks because of heavy, sore legs. A 2026 review recommends 1 to 2 weekly sessions of 3 to 5 compound lower body movements at 60 to 85% of 1 RM, 2 to 4 sets each, stopping each set before much slowing (10 to 20% velocity loss), and notes high torque, low cadence work on the bike may help when gym access is limited (de Pablos et al., 2026). The meta-analysis found no moderating effect of program length or weekly frequency, so the data cannot yet pick between 1 and 3 sessions per week (Llanos-Lagos et al., 2026).

### Exercise selection
**Evidence: Limited**
Exercises should train the main cycling muscles in movements similar to pedaling (Rønnestad & Mujika, 2014). Squat variants and leg press, including single leg versions, are the most studied. Deadlifts and hip hinge movements are reasonable compound choices but have not been tested on their own in cycling trials. For core training, 8 weeks of trunk strength work (3 sessions per week) in young female mountain bikers improved trunk strength and reduced side to side bike movement, but did not change physiological cycling economy (Blechschmied et al., 2026). Core work is reasonable for trunk strength and bike control, not as a direct economy tool.

### Body mass worries are mostly unfounded with this kind of program
**Evidence: Limited**
The main trials report increased thigh muscle size without an increase in body mass (Rønnestad et al., 2010a) or no change in body weight (Sunde et al., 2010). A 2026 review concludes that properly programmed resistance training does not appear to hurt performance through unwanted muscle growth (de Pablos et al., 2026). Higher lower body lean mass is linked to higher power per kg of lean mass in female cyclists (Mujika et al., 2016).

### Cyclists, especially masters road cyclists, often have low bone density
**Evidence: Moderate (that it is low), Limited (for what fixes it in cyclists)**
A systematic review of 31 studies concluded that road cycling does not appear to give any meaningful bone benefit, and adult road cyclists in regular training have low bone density at key sites such as the lumbar spine; mountain biking or mixing in other sports may reduce this (Olmedillas et al., 2012). In recreational men aged 20 to 59, cyclists had lower whole body and spine bone density than runners; 63% of cyclists had osteopenia of the spine or hip vs 19% of runners, and cyclists were 7 times more likely to have spinal osteopenia (Rector et al., 2008). Master cyclists (about 51 years, 10 or more years of racing with little weight bearing exercise) had lower spine and hip bone density than age matched non athletes, and 15% had osteoporosis level T scores (Nichols et al., 2003). Over 7 years, master cyclists lost more total body bone density than non athletes; in the whole sample, those who did weight training or impact exercise lost less spine and hip bone density, and 6 of 19 cyclists went from osteopenia to osteoporosis (Nichols & Rauh, 2011).

### Heavy loading and impact build bone, even in older adults with low bone mass
**Evidence: Moderate (in non cyclists)**
In postmenopausal women with low bone mass, 8 months of twice weekly, 30 minute supervised high intensity resistance and impact training (5 sets of 5 reps above 85% of 1 RM, plus impact loading) increased lumbar spine bone density by 2.9% vs a 1.2% loss in a low intensity home program, with only 1 minor adverse event (Watson et al., 2018). In men aged 45 and older with low bone mass, the same style of program improved lumbar spine bone density by 4.1% and hip (trochanter) bone density by 2.8% compared with controls (Harding et al., 2020). These trials were not in cyclists, but cyclists share the key problem of low skeletal loading. Nichols and Rauh (2011) recommend weight training, plyometrics or other high impact work alongside cycling.

## Rules for the coach

- Recommend lower body heavy strength training to every road and time trial cyclist who has the time, framed as a performance tool, not just injury prevention. Expect a moderate gain in 40 to 60 minute power and sprint power, not a VO2max gain.
- Base phase (build strength): 2 sessions per week for 10 to 12 weeks minimum. Use 3 to 4 compound lower body exercises (for example half squat or back squat, single leg leg press or split squat, hip hinge such as Romanian deadlift, plus a calf raise or hip flexion exercise). 2 to 4 sets per exercise, 2 to 3 minutes rest between sets.
- Load progression in the base phase: weeks 1 to 3 at about 10 to 12 reps with a load the rider could lift 2 to 3 more times (RPE about 7 out of 10), technique first. Weeks 4 to 8 at 6 to 8 RM loads (RPE 8). Weeks 9 to 12 at 4 to 6 RM loads (RPE 8 to 9). Do not take sets to failure; stop when bar speed clearly slows.
- Cue every rep: lower under control, push up as fast as possible.
- In the first 2 to 3 weeks of a new lifting block, cut planned bike intensity (fewer or shorter sessions above 90% FTP) because legs will be heavy and sore.
- Schedule the lifting base phase in the off season or early base, when bike intensity is lowest.
- In season maintenance: 1 session per week, 2 to 3 sets of 3 to 5 exercises at heavy loads (4 to 6 RM, RPE 8), short session (about 30 to 45 minutes). Never drop lifting entirely for more than 4 weeks during the season unless the rider wants to accept losing the gains within about 8 weeks.
- Same day order: if strength is the priority that day, lift first, then ride easy (below 75% FTP). If a key bike session is the priority, ride first and lift at least 3 hours later, or lift on a different day.
- Do not place a heavy leg session within about 24 hours before a key interval session, VO2max test or race (convention, not tested).
- Pair heavy lifting days with hard bike days where possible (hard day after hard day, then an easy day), so easy days stay easy. This is convention.
- Riders with very high riding volume (for example more than 15 hours per week) should expect smaller strength gains; keep lifting volume low and intensity high.
- Taper: keep 1 short heavy session in race weeks, at least 3 to 4 days before the target event; drop lifting in the final 3 days (convention).
- Core work: 2 to 3 short sets of trunk exercises 2 to 3 times per week is fine for trunk strength and bike control. Do not promise it improves cycling economy.
- Do not hold back strength training from female cyclists; the benefit is at least as large as in men.
- Bone health: for any masters rider (40 or older), any rider with a history of low bone density, stress fracture or crash fracture, and any rider who only cycles, include impact and heavy loading year round. Add 2 to 3 sets of 5 to 10 jumps (for example countermovement jumps, box jumps or skipping) 2 to 3 times per week once the rider has lifted for at least 4 weeks without problems, plus heavy lifts (5 reps at RPE 8 or higher) at least 1 time per week all year.
- Suggest masters road cyclists ask their doctor about a bone density (DXA) scan. Riders with known osteoporosis should start bone loading under supervision.
- Weight concern: reassure that the trial programs increased leg muscle size with no gain in body mass. Monitor body mass if the rider cares; if it rises more than 1 to 2% with no performance gain, reduce sets.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed

- Most cycling strength trials are small (6 to 12 riders per group) and many come from the same research group in Norway. The 2026 meta-analysis rated the certainty of evidence as low.
- No consensus exists on the best weekly frequency (1 vs 2 vs 3 sessions) or program length; the meta-analysis found no moderating effect of either.
- The 2010 systematic review found that adding lifting on top of full riding volume sometimes failed to help, while replacing some riding with lifting worked better. It is unclear how much riding should be cut.
- The value of explosive and plyometric training for endurance cycling performance (as opposed to sprinting) is unclear.
- Effects on power at lactate threshold are mixed across studies.
- Same day order evidence comes mostly from general populations, not cyclists, and looks at strength outcomes rather than cycling performance.
- Specific exercise choices beyond squat and leg press (deadlift, single leg work, core) have not been tested head to head in cyclists.
- The sets, reps and RPE numbers in the rules are a practical translation of the trial protocols, not tested dose response values.
- Bone loading trials (LIFTMOR) were in older adults with low bone mass, not cyclists. Whether lifting fully offsets the bone loss from high volume cycling is unknown.
- Long term data (more than 1 season) on strength training in cyclists are almost absent.
