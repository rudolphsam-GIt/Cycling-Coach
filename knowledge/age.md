# Training Across the Lifespan

## Summary
Endurance performance peaks around age 35, falls slowly until about 50 to 60, then falls faster (Tanaka & Seals, 2008). The main cause is a falling VO2max, driven by lower maximal heart rate, stroke volume and oxygen extraction. Economy does not seem to decline. In trained people, VO2max still drops, roughly 5 to 15% per decade depending on the study, and a big part of the drop comes from riders doing less and easier training as they age. Masters riders who keep training hard keep far more fitness, strength and muscle than sedentary peers. The person to person spread is huge: age alone is a poor predictor of what an individual older cyclist can do (Pollock et al., 2015).

Older riders may recover a bit more slowly from very damaging sessions, and they often feel more sore and tired than younger riders after hard days even when performance is the same. The evidence is mixed and mostly small. The practical approach is to keep high intensity, add strength and impact work for muscle and bone, raise protein per meal, and give a little more room between the hardest sessions. Long term endurance athletes, especially men, have a higher risk of atrial fibrillation. The coach cannot diagnose anything, but should push for medical review when symptoms appear. For juniors, the priority is broad athletic development, sensible load during growth spurts, and enjoyment, not early adult style volume.

## Key findings

### Performance declines slowly to 50 to 60, then faster
**Evidence: Strong (consistent cross sectional and longitudinal data)**
Peak endurance performance holds until about 35, declines modestly until 50 to 60, then declines more steeply. VO2max is the main driver; lactate threshold falls too, but probably as a result of the VO2max drop, and economy does not change with age in trained adults (Tanaka & Seals, 2008). In 32 masters cyclists aged 35 to 73, VO2max averaged 60.7, 54.2 and 45.9 mL/kg/min, and peak power 434, 392 and 324 W, in the 35 to 44, 45 to 54 and 55 plus groups. Economy did not differ between groups, and ventilatory thresholds as a share of max were higher in the oldest group (Peiffer et al., 2008). That last point means older riders sit closer to their ceiling at threshold, so FTP may hold up better than VO2max power. This was a cross sectional study of men only.

### Trained people still lose VO2max, but from a much higher level
**Evidence: Moderate (findings conflict on rate)**
Over about 8 years, masters endurance athletes (about 62 years) who kept training lost VO2max at about 5.5% per decade, versus 12% per decade in sedentary peers, and their maximal heart rate did not fall (Rogers et al., 1990). In a 20 year follow up of older track athletes, VO2max fell about 8 to 15% per decade even with continued vigorous training; the few who greatly cut their training fell faster (18% then 34%) (Pollock et al., 1997). Large cross sectional meta analyses tell a different story. In men, the absolute decline was similar across sedentary, active and endurance trained groups (about 4 to 4.6 mL/kg/min per decade), and relative decline was 6.8 to 8.7% per decade (Wilson & Tanaka, 2000). In women, the absolute decline was largest in endurance trained women (6.2 mL/kg/min per decade) but the relative decline was similar across groups (10 to 10.9% per decade) (Fitzgerald et al., 1997). Both meta analyses found that trained people stay far fitter at every age. The likely explanation is that trained people start higher and often cut their training as they age. Practical meaning: plan on roughly 5 to 10% decline in VO2max per decade for a masters rider who keeps training, faster after 60 to 70, and much faster if training drops.

### Maximal heart rate falls with age, and 220 minus age underestimates it in older people
**Evidence: Strong**
A meta analysis of 351 studies and a lab validation in 514 people found HRmax is predicted by 208 minus 0.7 x age, independent of sex and activity level (Tanaka et al., 2001). The 220 minus age formula underestimates HRmax in older adults. Individual variation is large, so measured values beat formulas. In the 20 year follow up, HRmax fell about 5 to 7 beats per minute per decade (Pollock et al., 1997).

### Recovery in older athletes: probably slightly slower after muscle damage, findings mixed
**Evidence: Limited**
A narrative review concluded that the effect of age on performance and recovery may be smaller than thought, that inactivity explains much of the decline, and that there may be an age effect on recovery but more research is needed (Borges et al., 2016). Another review noted that most studies do not separate aging from inactivity and use damaging exercise that does not look like normal training (Fell & Williams, 2008). After a 55 km trail run, masters (about 46 years) and young runners showed similar fatigue and muscle damage, but recovery was slower in the masters (Easthope et al., 2010). This was in runners, and downhill running causes more muscle damage than cycling. In well trained cyclists, 3 consecutive days of 30 min time trials did not reduce performance in either age group, but veteran riders (about 45 years) reported more soreness, more fatigue and worse recovery (Fell et al., 2008). After HIIT (6 x 30 s at 175% of peak power), masters cyclists (about 56 years) actually showed faster parasympathetic reactivation than young cyclists (Borges et al., 2017). Heart rate and RPE based training load measures behaved the same in masters and young cyclists when heart rate was expressed relative to maximum (Borges et al., 2020). Summary: cycling, which is low in muscle damage, may show little age effect on recovery; damaging sessions and strength work may need more recovery in older riders; and older riders feel more fatigued, which matters for adherence even if performance holds.

### Training load tolerance and keeping intensity high
**Evidence: Moderate (observational) / Limited (trials)**
Tanaka and Seals (2008) argue that much of the age related decline in endurance performance is driven by a fall in the intensity and volume of training that older athletes do. In the 20 year follow up, those who stayed at elite level or kept moderate to vigorous training lost less VO2max than the two who greatly cut back (Pollock et al., 1997). Reduced training studies also show intensity is the key variable for keeping VO2max (Hickson et al., 1985; Spiering et al., 2021), though those subjects were young. There are few long term randomized trials of different intensity doses in masters cyclists. The best supported approach is to keep regular hard work, especially VO2max type efforts, rather than drifting into only steady riding.

### Strength training helps older endurance athletes and maintenance needs a bigger dose
**Evidence: Moderate**
In 40 masters athletes aged 40 to 81 who trained 4 to 5 times a week, thigh muscle area and quadriceps strength did not decline with age, suggesting much of typical muscle loss reflects disuse (Wroblewski et al., 2011). This was cross sectional. In 9 masters (about 52 years) and 8 young endurance athletes, 3 weeks of knee extension strength training (3 sessions a week, 10 x 10 at 70% of 1RM) raised strength by 17.8% in masters versus 5.9% in young, and improved cycling efficiency in masters, closing the age gap (Louis et al., 2012). Older adults (60 to 75) needed a larger weekly dose than young adults to keep muscle size after a strength block; one third or one ninth of the original dose kept size in the young but not the old (Bickel et al., 2011). Older people may need 2 strength sessions per week and 2 to 3 sets per exercise to maintain muscle size (Spiering et al., 2021).

### Road cycling does little for bone, and masters cyclists can have low bone density
**Evidence: Moderate**
A systematic review of 31 studies concluded road cycling does not appear to give a meaningful bone building benefit, and adult road cyclists in regular training often have low bone density at key sites such as the lumbar spine; mountain biking or mixing in other sports may reduce this (Olmedillas et al., 2012). In male masters cyclists (about 51 years) with 20 years of racing and little weight bearing exercise, spine and hip bone density was lower than in age matched controls, and 15% had T scores below minus 2.5 (Nichols et al., 2003). In older track athletes, those who did weight training had higher arm bone density (Pollock et al., 1997). In postmenopausal women with low bone mass, 8 months of twice weekly, 30 minute supervised heavy resistance and impact training (5 x 5 at more than 85% 1RM) raised spine bone density by 2.9% versus a 1.2% loss in controls, with one minor adverse event (Watson et al., 2018). That trial was supervised and in women, not cyclists.

### Older adults need more protein per meal (anabolic resistance)
**Evidence: Moderate**
Pooled lab data showed muscle protein synthesis plateaued at about 0.40 g/kg body mass per meal in older men (about 71 years) versus about 0.24 g/kg in young men (Moore et al., 2015). The PROT-AGE group recommends at least 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/day for people over 65, and 1.2 g/kg/day or more for those who exercise (Bauer et al., 2013). A review on masters endurance athletes argued they may benefit from more post exercise protein than the roughly 20 g or 0.25 g/kg used for younger athletes, with attention to leucine, but noted little direct data in older athletes (Doering et al., 2016).

### Long term endurance athletes have higher atrial fibrillation risk, especially men
**Evidence: Moderate (observational)**
An early meta analysis of 6 case control studies (mean age 51, 93% men) found athletes had about 5 times the odds of AF compared with controls (Abdulla & Nielsen, 2009). A 2021 meta analysis found an odds ratio of 2.46, with higher risk in mixed sport athletes and in athletes under 55 (Newman et al., 2021). Among 52,755 Vasaloppet ski racers (men), those who completed the most races and those who finished fastest had higher rates of arrhythmia, mainly AF and slow heart rhythms (Andersen et al., 2013). A meta analysis found moderate activity lowered AF risk in both sexes, while vigorous exercise was linked to higher risk in men but lower risk in women (Mohanty et al., 2016). These are observational studies, and the absolute risk for any one person remains low. Regular exercise still has large overall heart health benefits.

### Medical screening before hard training
**Evidence: Moderate (expert consensus)**
The ACSM screening model bases the need for medical clearance on current activity level, signs or symptoms or known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, and the planned intensity (Riebe et al., 2015). It notes exercise is safe for most people and that cardiac events are often preceded by warning signs.

### Juniors (under 18): develop the athlete, manage load during growth
**Evidence: Moderate (consensus) / Limited (cycling specific)**
The IOC consensus on youth athletic development calls for an evidence informed approach to developing healthy, resilient and capable young athletes, with participation that stays inclusive, enjoyable and sustainable at all levels (Bergeron et al., 2015). The NSCA position statement on long term athletic development says all youth should build general athleticism, including strength training with proper supervision, and that young athletes with high competition volume and no preparatory conditioning are at higher risk of overuse injury (Lloyd et al., 2016). In 80 Norwegian junior road cyclists, those who reached World Tour level by 23 had already raced more and placed better at 18, and had higher maximal aerobic power at 18 (6.9 vs 6.2 W/kg) (Svendsen et al., 2018). This is about older juniors who were already selected, so it does not justify high volume in younger children. On gearing, the UCI removed its junior (under 19) road gear limit of 7.93 m per pedal revolution from 1 January 2023, reportedly driven largely by the lack of compatible junior cassettes. Press reports said some national federations (for example the Dutch and Belgian) planned to keep limits, and that youth (under 16) limits stay in place in some countries such as Britain (CyclingTips, 2022, plus secondary reports; check the rider's national federation rules).

## Rules for the coach
- Always ask age and sex. Use measured HRmax when available; otherwise estimate with 208 minus 0.7 x age, not 220 minus age. Prefer power zones based on a current FTP test over age formulas.
- Retest FTP every 6 to 8 weeks for riders over 50. Expect slower long term trends and do not treat a small yearly drop as failure; about 5 to 10% decline in VO2max per decade is typical even in trained riders.
- Keep high intensity at every age. For riders 40 plus with no medical restriction, include 1 to 2 sessions per week with work at or above 105% FTP (RPE 8 to 9), unless in a planned rest block.
- Recovery spacing by decade (coaching practice, guided by limited evidence):
  - 40s: same structure as younger riders. 2 to 3 hard sessions per week. Recovery week every 4 weeks.
  - 50s: 2 hard sessions per week as default, a third only if sleep, mood and performance are good. At least 48 h between the two hardest sessions. Recovery week every 3 weeks.
  - 60s: 2 hard sessions per week, at least 48 to 72 h apart. Shorter VO2max sets (for example 4 to 6 x 2 to 3 min at 110 to 120% FTP). Recovery week every 3 weeks.
  - 70 plus: 1 to 2 hard sessions per week, built up gradually over several weeks. Emphasize balance, strength and enjoyment. Recovery week every 2 to 3 weeks.
- If an older rider reports more soreness or fatigue, add an extra easy day before reducing the intensity of key sessions. Ask about subjective recovery every week; older riders may feel worse even when performance is fine.
- Strength training for all riders 40 plus: 2 sessions per week, 2 to 3 sets per exercise, building to heavy loads (about 5 to 8 reps near failure, or above 80 to 85% 1RM once technique is solid). In busy periods, never drop below 1 session per week.
- Bone: for road only cyclists, especially 50 plus and anyone with low bone density history, add impact or weight bearing work (jumps, hopping, stair work, running, or heavy lifting) at least 2 to 3 times per week if cleared to do so. Suggest the rider ask a doctor about a bone density scan if they are a long term road only rider over 50, or have had a low impact fracture.
- Protein for riders 50 plus: aim for about 0.4 g/kg per meal across 3 to 4 meals, and at least 1.2 g/kg/day; 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg/day is a reasonable target in heavy training. Include a protein serving after hard sessions. Riders with kidney disease must follow their doctor's advice on protein.
- Medical clearance language: before starting high intensity training, advise a check with a doctor for any rider who is currently inactive and over about 45, has known heart, lung, metabolic or kidney disease, or has symptoms. The coach must not diagnose.
- Stop and refer immediately (no further hard training until seen by a doctor) if the rider reports chest pain or pressure, fainting or near fainting, unusual breathlessness, a racing, pounding or irregular heartbeat, a sudden unexplained drop in performance with high heart rate readings, or heart rate readings that jump erratically. Say: "These symptoms need a doctor's review before more hard training. I can't tell what's causing them."
- Do not cut a rider's training simply because they are an endurance athlete with AF risk; overall exercise is strongly beneficial. If a rider has diagnosed AF or another heart condition, follow their cardiologist's limits.
- Juniors under 18:
  - Under 14: no structured interval blocks. Mostly varied riding, skills, other sports and games. Keep cycling to a share of total activity, with at least 1 to 2 rest days per week.
  - 14 to 16: introduce structure gradually. 1 to 2 hard sessions per week. Supervised strength training with good technique is encouraged.
  - 16 to 18: closer to adult structure, but increase weekly hours by no more than about 10% at a time, and keep at least 1 full rest day per week.
  - During growth spurts (fast height gain), hold or reduce load, watch for knee, heel and back pain, and refer persistent pain to a doctor or physio.
  - Check national federation gearing rules for youth riders before prescribing big gear work. Prefer high cadence skills over big gear strength efforts for under 16s.
  - Ask about enjoyment and other sports regularly. Avoid year round single sport specialization before mid teens.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- The rate of VO2max decline in trained versus sedentary people conflicts between longitudinal studies (trained decline slower) and cross sectional meta analyses (similar or faster absolute decline in trained). Training cutbacks with age likely explain much of this.
- Most masters cycling data are cross sectional, small, and in men. Data in female masters cyclists are sparse.
- Recovery studies in older athletes are small and mixed. Slower recovery is clearest after muscle damaging exercise (often running), less clear after cycling intervals.
- The recovery spacing by decade is a coaching synthesis, not tested in trials.
- Little long term trial evidence compares different intensity doses in masters cyclists.
- Bone data in cyclists are mostly cross sectional; the best intervention trial (LIFTMOR) was in postmenopausal women, not cyclists.
- AF evidence is observational, with possible confounding. The sex difference (higher risk in men with vigorous exercise) is based on few studies of women.
- Junior gearing rules vary by country and change over time. Claims that big gears harm growing knees are not well supported, but also not well tested.
- Junior training recommendations come mostly from expert consensus, not cycling specific trials.
