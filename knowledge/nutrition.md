# Fueling, Hydration and Sports Nutrition for Cyclists

## Summary
Carbohydrate is the main fuel for hard and long cycling, and the amount a rider needs scales with how much and how hard they train. The major position stands agree on daily targets from about 3 to 5 g per kg body mass on light days up to 8 to 12 g/kg on very heavy days, and on ride fueling that rises from almost nothing for short rides to 30 to 60 g per hour for most endurance rides and up to about 90 g per hour for rides longer than about 2.5 hours, using glucose plus fructose blends. Protein needs for athletes are about 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg per day, spread across meals, with roughly 0.3 g/kg (about 20 to 25 g) after key sessions.

Deliberately training with low carbohydrate availability for some sessions ("train low") can boost muscle signaling, but performance gains are inconsistent. Long term ketogenic or low carbohydrate high fat (LCHF) diets reduce exercise economy and have impaired race performance in elite endurance athletes. Hydration advice centers on knowing your own sweat rate, starting rides hydrated, and avoiding both large fluid deficits and drinking more than you sweat. Caffeine at 3 to 6 mg/kg is one of the few supplements with strong evidence. Nitrate (beetroot) helps recreational athletes a little but shows little effect in highly trained riders. Chronic under fueling (low energy availability) harms health and performance and is a medical issue, not a coaching one.

## Key findings

### Daily carbohydrate should match the training load
**Evidence: Strong**
The 2016 joint position of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada and the American College of Sports Medicine sets daily carbohydrate targets by load: light (low intensity or skill work) 3 to 5 g/kg/day; moderate (about 1 h per day) 5 to 7 g/kg/day; high (endurance program of 1 to 3 h per day of moderate to high intensity) 6 to 10 g/kg/day; very high (4 to 5 h per day of moderate to high intensity) 8 to 12 g/kg/day (Thomas et al., 2016). These ranges come from Burke and colleagues' earlier consensus, which framed carbohydrate intake in terms of whether stores are adequate for the day's work ("high" versus "low" carbohydrate availability) rather than one fixed number (Burke et al., 2011). The ranges are guidelines, adjusted to energy goals and training response, and timing across the day can be shifted to put carbohydrate around key sessions.

### Carbohydrate during rides should scale with duration
**Evidence: Strong**
For rides under about 45 minutes, carbohydrate is not needed. For sustained hard efforts of 45 to 75 minutes, small amounts or even a carbohydrate mouth rinse can help. For 1 to 2.5 hours, 30 to 60 g/h. For 2.5 to 3 hours or more, up to 90 g/h (Thomas et al., 2016). Jeukendrup's guidelines are similar: around 1 hour a mouth rinse or small amounts can improve performance; a single sugar source (glucose) can be oxidized at up to about 60 g/h, which is the target for 2 to 3 hour exercise; and about 90 g/h for longer events, which requires multiple transportable carbohydrates (glucose plus fructose) to allow higher absorption and avoid gut buildup (Jeukendrup, 2014). The advice is independent of body mass and training status but should be lowered when absolute intensity is low, because less carbohydrate is burned (Jeukendrup, 2014). A systematic review of 61 studies found that 82% showed a performance benefit from carbohydrate versus placebo, with larger benefits as exercise duration increased; for efforts longer than 2 hours, high delivery rates (around 90 g/h) of glucose and fructose mixtures were beneficial (Stellingwerff & Cox, 2014). In a four site study of 51 cyclists and triathletes (2 h steady ride then a 20 km time trial), performance improved in a curved dose response up to about 78 g/h, with smaller gains above that (Smith et al., 2013).

### The gut is trainable
**Evidence: Moderate**
Gastric emptying, stomach comfort and intestinal carbohydrate absorption adapt to regular intake; a high carbohydrate diet increases intestinal glucose transporters (SGLT1), which supports higher absorption during exercise and likely less gut distress (Jeukendrup, 2017). In trained cyclists, 28 days of training with high carbohydrate availability (including carbohydrate during sessions) increased oxidation of ingested glucose during cycling compared with an energy matched low carbohydrate group (Cox et al., 2010). Most human studies are small, and the best protocol and timeline for gut training are not established (Jeukendrup, 2017).

### Pre-ride meals and carbohydrate loading
**Evidence: Strong**
Before exercise longer than 60 minutes, eat 1 to 4 g/kg carbohydrate 1 to 4 hours beforehand, choosing foods that suit the rider and limiting high fat, high protein and high fiber choices if they cause gut issues (Thomas et al., 2016). For general fueling before events under 90 minutes, 7 to 12 g/kg over 24 hours. For carbohydrate loading before events longer than 90 minutes of sustained or intermittent work, 10 to 12 g/kg per 24 hours for 36 to 48 hours (Thomas et al., 2016; Burke et al., 2011). Low fiber, low residue carbohydrate sources make these targets easier to reach.

### Protein: amount, dose per meal and timing
**Evidence: Strong (daily range), Moderate (per meal dose in endurance athletes)**
Daily protein to support adaptation, repair and turnover generally ranges from 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/day, with higher intakes for short periods of intense training or energy restriction (Thomas et al., 2016). The ISSN position stand gives a similar 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg/day range for most exercising people, a per serving dose of about 0.25 g/kg (or 20 to 40 g) spread every 3 to 4 hours, and notes that endurance athletes should prioritize adequate carbohydrate, with protein helping recovery (Jäger et al., 2017). Per meal, about 0.25 to 0.3 g/kg (about 15 to 25 g) of high quality protein after key sessions and every 3 to 5 hours across multiple meals is recommended (Thomas et al., 2016). This dose comes largely from Moore et al. (2009), who found muscle protein synthesis after leg resistance exercise plateaued at 20 g of egg protein in six young men; this is a small study in resistance exercise, so it is an approximation for cyclists. Reviews argue that protein also matters for endurance athletes for remodeling of muscle and mitochondrial proteins after long training (Moore et al., 2014).

### Recovery nutrition
**Evidence: Strong (carbohydrate refueling), Moderate (rehydration volume)**
When there are fewer than 8 hours between two fuel demanding sessions, take 1 to 1.2 g/kg/h of carbohydrate for the first 4 hours, then resume daily targets (Thomas et al., 2016). The ISSN nutrient timing stand gives similar advice when recovery time is under 4 hours (about 1.2 g/kg/h carbohydrate, or about 0.8 g/kg/h carbohydrate combined with 0.2 to 0.4 g/kg/h protein) and stresses evenly spaced protein feedings across the day (Kerksick et al., 2017). When recovery time is longer, meeting total daily targets is the main goal (Burke et al., 2011). To rehydrate, drink about 125 to 150% of the remaining fluid deficit, roughly 1.25 to 1.5 L for every kg lost, with sodium from food or drinks (Thomas et al., 2016).

### Periodized carbohydrate and "train low"
**Evidence: Moderate (cell signaling), Limited (performance)**
Training some sessions with low carbohydrate availability (twice a day training, fasted training, withholding carbohydrate after a session, or "sleep low, train low") boosts cell signaling and some markers of oxidative adaptation, especially when sessions start within a certain range of muscle glycogen, but performance outcomes are mixed; in a review of 11 studies, about 37% showed performance improvements and 63% no change (Impey et al., 2018). Impey and colleagues proposed "fuel for the work required": match carbohydrate to the demands of each session, so low carbohydrate days pair with easy or moderate sessions and hard sessions are well fueled (Impey et al., 2018). In 21 trained triathletes eating the same daily carbohydrate (6 g/kg), a 3 week "sleep low" timing strategy improved submaximal cycling efficiency and 10 km run time compared with a control group (Marquet et al., 2016). In contrast, 28 days of withholding carbohydrate during training in trained cyclists changed metabolism but did not change the size of the performance gain from training (Cox et al., 2010). A 2018 expert group published shared definitions for these terms because confusion about "train low" and related strategies is common (Burke et al., 2018).

### Ketogenic and LCHF diets impair economy and performance in elite athletes
**Evidence: Moderate (strong design, but elite race walkers rather than cyclists)**
In the first "Supernova" study, world class race walkers who adapted to a ketogenic LCHF diet for 3 weeks of intensified training greatly increased fat oxidation but had worse economy (higher oxygen cost at race speeds) and lost the performance benefit of the training block, while high carbohydrate and periodized carbohydrate groups improved (Burke et al., 2017). A repeat study confirmed the impairment (Burke et al., 2020). Even 5 to 6 days of LCHF increased oxygen cost by 5 to 8% at race related speeds, and acute carbohydrate restoration before racing did not fully reverse the performance impairment (Burke et al., 2021). These studies used race walkers, so the findings are applied to cycling by analogy.

### Hydration: sweat rate, the 2% guidance and its debate
**Evidence: Strong (individual sweat testing, avoid overdrinking), Moderate and disputed (the 2% threshold)**
The ACSM position stand recommends starting exercise hydrated, preventing excessive dehydration (more than 2% body weight loss from water deficit) and large electrolyte changes, and tailoring fluid plans because sweat rates and sweat sodium vary widely; sweat rate can be estimated from body mass before and after exercise (Sawka et al., 2007). The 2016 position stand also says fluid deficits above 2% body mass can compromise aerobic performance, especially in hot weather, and that overdrinking beyond sweat losses is the main cause of exercise associated hyponatremia (blood sodium below 135 mmol/L) (Thomas et al., 2016). Weighing before and after is the simplest accurate whole body sweat rate method, while sweat sodium tests vary a lot with method (Baker, 2017). Among 1,303 athletes tested, endurance athletes averaged about 1.28 L/h sweat and about 52 mmol/h of sodium loss, with large individual spread (Barnes et al., 2019).
The 2% rule is disputed. A meta-analysis of cycling time trials found that losses up to about 4% body mass did not reduce out of doors type, self paced performance compared with staying hydrated, and that drinking to thirst did better than drinking below thirst (Goulet, 2011). A follow up meta-analysis found dehydration reduced performance only in fixed intensity lab protocols, not self paced time trials (Goulet, 2013). Others argue the evidence supports a 2% or greater threshold for impaired endurance performance driven by loss of blood volume (Cheuvront & Kenefick, 2014). The practical middle ground is to avoid large deficits in heat and long events, and to never gain weight during a ride.

### Caffeine
**Evidence: Strong**
The ISSN position stand concludes that caffeine consistently improves performance at 3 to 6 mg/kg body mass, that aerobic endurance shows the most consistent benefits, that doses as low as 2 mg/kg may work, and that very high doses (around 9 mg/kg) increase side effects without being required; timing is most often 60 minutes before exercise, but gums and gels may act faster (Guest et al., 2021). It is supported for endurance work in heat (3 to 6 mg/kg) and at altitude (4 to 6 mg/kg), and it can disturb sleep and cause anxiety in some people (Guest et al., 2021). A meta-analysis of 44 studies found moderate doses improved time trial mean power by about 3% and completion time by about 2%, with some individuals performing worse (Southward et al., 2018). Caffeine at moderate habitual intakes is not a meaningful diuretic (Thomas et al., 2016).

### Nitrate (beetroot juice)
**Evidence: Moderate (recreational), Limited (highly trained)**
Dietary nitrate lowers the oxygen cost of submaximal exercise and can improve performance in some situations (Jones, 2014). Effective protocols used about 5 to 9 mmol nitrate per day (roughly 0.5 L of beetroot juice, or concentrated shots) for 1 to 15 days, with acute doses usually taken about 2.5 hours before exercise; several studies in highly trained endurance athletes found no effect (Jones, 2014). A meta-analysis of 80 studies found a small overall benefit (d = 0.17), no clear benefit in well trained endurance athletes (VO2max 65 mL/kg/min or higher), and too few studies in women to conclude (Senefeld et al., 2020). The IOC lists nitrate among the few supplements with good evidence but stresses that responses vary and that supplements should be trialed in training first and checked for anti doping risk (Maughan et al., 2018).

### Energy availability and RED-S
**Evidence: Strong (as a health risk), Moderate (exact thresholds)**
Energy availability is the energy left for body functions after subtracting exercise energy. In studies first done in women, about 45 kcal per kg fat free mass per day was associated with energy balance and health, while chronic intake below about 30 kcal/kg FFM/day was linked to impaired body functions (Thomas et al., 2016). The 2023 IOC consensus on Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) describes harms to health and performance in both women and men exposed to problematic low energy availability, highlights the role of low carbohydrate availability, and introduces a clinical assessment tool (REDs CAT2) for diagnosis by clinicians (Mountjoy et al., 2023). Cyclists, as a weight sensitive endurance sport, are a group where this matters.

## Rules for the coach
- Set a daily carbohydrate band from the planned training load: rest or easy skill day 3 to 5 g/kg; about 1 h moderate 5 to 7 g/kg; 1 to 3 h moderate to hard 6 to 10 g/kg; 4 to 5 h or stage race days 8 to 12 g/kg.
- Per ride fueling by duration and intensity:
  - Under 45 min, any intensity: water only; no carbohydrate needed.
  - 45 to 75 min, hard (intervals, race, threshold): optional small amount or a carbohydrate mouth rinse; 0 to 30 g total.
  - 45 to 75 min, easy: water only.
  - 75 min to 2.5 h, endurance or tempo: 30 to 60 g/h, starting in the first 20 to 30 minutes and taken every 15 to 20 minutes.
  - 75 min to 2.5 h, easy recovery or zone 1 to 2 ride: lower end, 0 to 30 g/h, or a planned low carbohydrate session only if the plan calls for it.
  - Over 2.5 h, or any race or hard group ride over 2 h: 60 to 90 g/h using glucose plus fructose products; build toward 90 g/h only after practicing.
  - Above 90 g/h: treat as experimental; only if the rider has trained the gut and tolerates it.
- Gut training: in the 4 to 6 weeks before a target event, practice race fueling rates on at least one long ride per week, increasing by about 10 to 15 g/h every 1 to 2 weeks if comfortable.
- Pre-ride meal for rides over 60 minutes: 1 to 4 g/kg carbohydrate 1 to 4 hours before (about 1 g/kg at 1 h, up to 4 g/kg at 4 h), low in fat and fiber if the rider has gut issues.
- Carbohydrate loading for races longer than 90 minutes: 10 to 12 g/kg/day for the 36 to 48 hours before, with low fiber foods; use 7 to 12 g/kg the day before shorter races.
- Protein: target 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/day, split into 4 or more meals of about 0.3 g/kg (20 to 25 g for most riders, up to about 40 g for large riders), including one soon after hard or long rides.
- Recovery: if the next hard session is within 8 hours, give 1 to 1.2 g/kg/h carbohydrate for 4 hours plus about 0.3 g/kg protein. If the next hard session is a day or more away, just hit daily targets.
- Rehydration: drink 1.25 to 1.5 L per kg of body mass lost, with sodium from food or an electrolyte drink.
- Sweat rate test: weigh nude before and after a 60 minute ride at typical intensity, add fluid drunk and subtract urine; sweat rate (L/h) = (pre kg minus post kg) + fluid drunk L minus urine L. Repeat in hot and cool conditions.
- Fluid plan: aim to keep body mass loss under about 2% on long or hot rides, never finish heavier than you started, and allow drinking to thirst on shorter rides in cool weather.
- Sodium: for rides over 2 hours or in heat, use drinks with about 0.5 to 0.7 g sodium per liter, as in the heat consensus (Racinais et al., 2015); consider more for heavy, salty sweaters.
- Train low: limit to about 1 to 3 easy or moderate sessions per week (for example a fasted or "sleep low" easy ride under 90 minutes), never for intervals, races or key long rides, and never for riders with any signs of low energy availability.
- Never recommend ketogenic or LCHF diets for performance cycling; if a rider follows one, note the evidence of reduced economy and suggest they discuss it with a sports dietitian.
- Caffeine: 3 mg/kg about 60 minutes before key sessions or races is a sensible start; do not exceed 6 mg/kg; avoid caffeine within about 6 hours of bedtime if sleep suffers; trial in training first.
- Nitrate: optional for recreational riders (about 6 to 8 mmol nitrate, such as a concentrated beetroot shot, about 2.5 h before, or daily for 3 to 7 days); tell well trained riders the benefit is uncertain; avoid antibacterial mouthwash around dosing.
- Supplements in general: suggest third party tested products for any rider subject to anti doping rules.
- Energy availability red flags: unplanned weight loss, missed or irregular periods, recurrent bone stress injuries, frequent illness, persistent fatigue, low libido, or performance falling despite training. If any appear, say this may be a sign of low energy availability, stop any weight loss or train low plan, and refer to a doctor and a registered sports dietitian. Do not diagnose REDs.
- Any rider with diabetes, a history of disordered eating, kidney disease, high blood pressure medication, pregnancy, or gut disease: refer to a doctor or registered dietitian before changing carbohydrate, protein, sodium or supplement intake.
- The coach gives general fueling targets, not meal plans or medical nutrition therapy. Personalized plans belong with a registered dietitian.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- Intakes above 90 g/h are used by many pros, but controlled performance data are limited; the best dose study found diminishing returns above about 78 g/h (Smith et al., 2013).
- Whether "train low" improves race performance in trained cyclists is unresolved; signaling benefits are clearer than performance benefits (Impey et al., 2018; Cox et al., 2010; Marquet et al., 2016).
- LCHF harm data come from elite race walkers; studies specific to cyclists at similar quality are scarce.
- The 2% body mass loss threshold is disputed; self paced time trial meta-analyses show little effect up to about 4% in cyclists, but these were short and mostly lab based (Goulet, 2011, 2013; Cheuvront & Kenefick, 2014).
- Sweat sodium testing methods vary, and patch tests may not represent whole body losses (Baker, 2017).
- The 20 g (about 0.3 g/kg) protein dose comes from a small resistance exercise study in young men; optimal post ride protein for cyclists, women and older riders is less certain (Moore et al., 2009).
- Nitrate appears to have little effect in highly trained riders and is understudied in women (Senefeld et al., 2020).
- Energy availability thresholds (30 and 45 kcal/kg FFM) came mainly from studies in women, and measuring energy availability in the field is imprecise (Thomas et al., 2016; Mountjoy et al., 2023).
- Gut training protocols and timelines are not standardized (Jeukendrup, 2017).
