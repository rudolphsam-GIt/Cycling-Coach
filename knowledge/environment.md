# Heat, Cold, Altitude and Air Quality for Cyclists

## Summary
Heat is the environmental stress with the best evidence base for cyclists. Repeated training in the heat for about 1 to 2 weeks, with sessions of at least 60 minutes that raise core temperature and cause sweating, reduces heat strain and improves performance in hot conditions. Whether heat training also improves performance in cool conditions is mixed: pooled data suggest a small rise in VO2max, but trials in trained cyclists disagree on performance. Longer heat training blocks (around 5 weeks) can raise hemoglobin mass in elite athletes. When racing in heat, arriving acclimatized and hydrated and using cooling before and during the event help limit the drop in performance. Race data from millions of marathon finishers show times slow and medical emergencies rise as temperature climbs.

Altitude lowers endurance performance right away, by roughly 6 to 7% per 1,000 m in pooled lab data. Living at moderate altitude (about 2,000 to 2,500 m) while training lower ("live high, train low") can raise red cell mass and sea level performance, but individual responses vary widely and the dose has to be large: weeks of exposure and many hours per day. Passive hypoxic exposure and training in hypoxia show no clear benefit for athletes. Cold weather is mainly a safety and clothing issue. Air pollution and wildfire smoke are health issues, and the coach should follow the official Air Quality Index (AQI) rather than invent thresholds.

## Size of the evidence
Lab trials in this area are small (often 7 to 20 people, mostly young men), but the pools are now fairly large for the main questions. Heat acclimation has a meta-analysis of 96 articles; cooling has 59 studies with 563 men; acute altitude has 53 studies with 798 people; hypoxic training has 62 studies with 1,332 people. The biggest datasets are observational race records: about 1.8 million marathon finishers across six major marathons for temperature, 1.28 million New York finishers, 2.76 million finishes for air pollution, 315,919 half marathon starters for heat emergencies, and health cohorts of 52,061 people on cycling and pollution. These show associations, not cause and effect, and come from running, not cycling. The pool is still small for heat training in cool conditions, hemoglobin gains from heat (about 160 athletes from one research group), cold effects on performance, women, and exercise specific air quality thresholds (none exist). No published Strava, Garmin or Zwift performance datasets on heat, altitude or pollution were found.

## Key findings

### Heat acclimation reduces strain and improves performance in the heat
**Evidence: Strong**
**Pool: 96 studies, N not reported in abstract (mostly young men); a second pool of 35 studies with about 100 to 230 people per outcome**
Exercise heat acclimation improves sweating and skin blood flow, lowers body temperature and heart rate, expands plasma volume, and lowers the risk of serious heat illness; the size of the adaptation depends on the intensity, duration, frequency and number of exposures (Périard et al., 2015). Controlled hyperthermia protocols and outdoor acclimatization in race like conditions appear most effective (Périard et al., 2015). The 2015 consensus calls heat acclimatization the single most important step for the heat, recommending exposures over 1 to 2 weeks, sessions of at least 60 minutes per day, and a core temperature of about 38.5 °C or more (Racinais et al., 2015). A meta-analysis of 96 articles found a moderate benefit on performance and capacity in the heat with any regimen, with longer regimens (over 14 days) giving the largest adaptations (Tyler et al., 2016). Another pool of 35 studies found gains in time to exhaustion, time trials and mean power (Benjamin et al., 2019), and a pool of 11 randomized trials (215 people, 91% men) found better time trial performance (Rahimi et al., 2019). Adaptations decay slowly: heart rate gains fade by about 2.3% per day without heat, and re-acclimation is 8 to 12 times faster than decay (Daanen et al., 2018). Later reviews and the 2023 IOC consensus agree (Périard et al., 2021; Racinais et al., 2023).

### Heat training as a tool for performance in temperate conditions
**Evidence: Moderate (small VO2max gain), Limited (temperate performance)**
**Pool: 28 articles, N not reported in abstract (mostly trained men); performance in cool conditions rests on a few trials of 7 to 18 cyclists**
In 12 trained cyclists, 10 days of heat acclimation raised VO2max by 5% and time trial work by 6% in cool conditions (Lorenzo et al., 2010). Later studies did not replicate this: 2 weeks of outdoor heat training in 18 cyclists did not change VO2max or a 43 km cool time trial (Karlsen et al., 2015), and a crossover study of 7 men improved performance in the heat but not at 18 °C (Keiser et al., 2015). A meta-analysis of 28 articles found a small VO2max gain in thermoneutral conditions compared with control groups (g = 0.30), larger in the heat (Waldron et al., 2021). Post exercise sauna or hot baths (10 studies, 199 people) had trivial effects on performance, with low certainty (Solomon & Laye, 2025). The consensus calls temperate benefits possible but circumstantial in highly trained athletes (Racinais et al., 2015).

### Longer heat training can raise hemoglobin mass
**Evidence: Limited (randomized and consistent, but one research group and about 160 athletes)**
**Pool: at least 5 controlled trials, about 160 elite or well trained athletes (cyclists, cross country skiers and biathletes), all from one Norwegian and Danish research group**
Five 50 to 60 minute heat sessions per week for 5 weeks raised hemoglobin mass in 23 elite cyclists (Rønnestad et al., 2021), in 25 cross country skiers (Rønnestad et al., 2022), and by about 4% in women and 3.5% in men among 47 elite cyclists, with better lactate threshold, VO2max and 15 minute power in thermoneutral conditions (Lundby et al., 2023). Three weekly heat sessions maintained hemoglobin mass after an altitude camp in 18 elite cyclists (Rønnestad et al., 2025) and added to it during a moderate altitude camp in 46 skiers and biathletes (Rønnestad et al., 2026). The grade was lowered from Moderate because the pool is small and not yet independently replicated.

### Racing in the heat: hydration and cooling
**Evidence: Strong (hydration basics), Moderate (cooling)**
**Pool: 59 studies, about 563 people (male athletes, about 34 °C); head and neck cooling 63 studies, about 618 people (87% male)**
**Real world data: 1,791,972 finishers of six major marathons, 1,280,557 New York City Marathon finishers and 315,919 starters over seven years of a large half marathon, observational**
Athletes should start euhydrated (about 6 mL/kg every 2 to 3 hours before), minimize dehydration without gaining weight, use drinks with about 0.5 to 0.7 g/L sodium for sessions over 1 hour, and take 30 to 60 g/h carbohydrate (up to 90 g/h beyond 2.5 hours); losses of 2% or more can impair performance in the heat (Racinais et al., 2015). Precooling (about 1 L of ice slurry, ice vests, fans) may benefit sustained events (Racinais et al., 2015). Across 59 studies, cooling before or during exercise reduced the performance loss in the heat, with an effect size of about 0.30 for self paced exercise like time trials; no women were included (van de Kerkhof et al., 2024). Precooling helped prolonged exercise but impaired sprints in 28 articles (Tyler et al., 2015); head and neck cooling effects are largely perceptual (Stevens et al., 2025). Caffeine at 3 to 6 mg/kg still works in heat (Guest et al., 2021). Wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) is an environmental index, not individual heat strain (Racinais et al., 2015).
Race records show the same direction in real conditions. Across 1.8 million marathon finishers, speed fell and withdrawals rose once temperature passed an optimum that was lower for faster runners (El Helou et al., 2012). In New York, hot races added about 8 minutes on average (Knechtle et al., 2021), and slower runners slowed more as WBGT rose from 5 to 25 °C (Ely et al., 2007). In 315,919 half marathon starters, ambulance calls, collapses and non finishers rose with air temperature (Carlström et al., 2019). Across 62 studies, endurance events including cycling had the highest heat illness rates per participant (Gamage et al., 2020), and among 343 heat stroke cases in a hot 7 mile race, younger runners were most at risk (Belval et al., 2020). These are mainly running data and show associations.

### Cold weather riding
**Evidence: Moderate (safety guidance), Limited (performance)**
**Pool: no pooled trials; ACSM position stand and reviews**
The ACSM position stand states that exercise can be done safely in most cold environments with proper risk management: know the signs of hypothermia, frostbite and non freezing cold injury, check the forecast, choose clothing to the individual, use the wind chill index with extra watchfulness below -27 °C (-18 °F), and monitor people with asthma or heart disease closely (Castellani et al., 2006). Aerobic performance appears reduced in the cold and strength and power fall as muscle temperature drops, but this is poorly studied; exhaustive exercise can blunt shivering ("thermoregulatory fatigue") (Castellani & Tipton, 2015). Cycling adds self generated wind, so wind chill on the bike is worse than the forecast; this is a practical inference, not a measured guideline. Marathon data suggest best performance at cool temperatures (El Helou et al., 2012), which says little about very cold riding.

### Acute altitude lowers aerobic power and pacing must change
**Evidence: Strong**
**Pool: 53 studies, about 798 people (acute hypoxia under 24 hours, continuous and intermittent exercise)**
A meta-regression of 53 studies found acute hypoxia cut performance and capacity by about 18% on average, with a loss of about 6.5% per 1,000 m of altitude; time trials fell about 16%, while efforts shorter than 2 minutes were not impaired (Deb et al., 2018). Smaller studies agree: VO2max fell about 6.3% per 1,000 m in 8 athletes, already measurable at 800 m (Wehrlin & Hallén, 2006), and 5 minute power in 10 cyclists fell about 5.8%, 10.3% and 19.8% at 1,200, 2,200 and 3,200 m, with mis-pacing at altitude (Clark et al., 2007). Moderate altitude lowers VO2max by about 15 to 20% (Saunders et al., 2009). For events where drag dominates, thinner air partly offsets the loss; an hour record model put the best elevation at about 2,500 m for acclimatized riders and 2,000 m for unacclimatized riders (Bassett et al., 1999). On climbs the aerobic loss dominates (inference). The "small samples" caveat was removed because pooled data now cover about 800 people.

### Live high, train low can improve sea level performance, with wide individual variation
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 62 studies, about 1,332 people (610 athletes, 722 non athletes) for VO2max; 51 studies, N not reported in abstract, for performance**
In 39 competitive runners, 4 weeks living at 2,500 m and training at 1,250 m raised VO2max by 5% and red cell mass by 9% and improved 5,000 m time (Levine & Stray-Gundersen, 1997). Responders had a larger early rise in erythropoietin; non-responders did not increase red cell volume (Chapman et al., 1998). A meta-analysis of 51 studies found natural live high train low best for elite and sub elite athletes, about 4% more power in elite athletes, but with substantial placebo, nocebo and training camp effects (Bonetti & Hopkins, 2009). A 2026 meta-analysis of 62 studies found live high train low raised VO2max in athletes by about 2.2 mL/kg/min more than control (Kohlbrenner et al., 2026). Raw data from 17 studies showed hemoglobin mass rose about 1.1% per 100 hours of exposure and stayed about 3.3% higher for up to 20 days (Gore et al., 2013). More than 3 weeks above about 2,000 m and adequate iron are needed for the red cell response; keep high intensity efforts short (Saunders et al., 2009).

### Hypoxic dose: hours per day and weeks matter
**Evidence: Moderate (reviews and pooled data), Limited (exact thresholds)**
**Pool: 62 studies, about 1,332 people (passive and training in hypoxia, athletes and non athletes); 35 studies, about 524 people (training in hypoxia)**
A review put the best living altitude at about 2,200 to 2,500 m for about 4 weeks, with a minimum of about 12 hours per day and more benefit beyond 16 hours (Millet et al., 2010). (See also Wilber, 2007.) A double blind, placebo controlled study in 16 cyclists found 4 weeks of 16 hours per day at simulated 3,000 m did not change hemoglobin mass, VO2max or 26 km time trial power (Siebenmann et al., 2012). Pooled data agree for athletes: passive hypoxic exposure and live low, train high did not raise VO2max in athletes, though they did in non athletes (Kohlbrenner et al., 2026), and aerobic training in hypoxia did not beat the same training in normal air for VO2max or performance, with signs of publication bias (Dorelli et al., 2025). This raises doubt about home tents at typical doses and about altitude masks.

### Air pollution and wildfire smoke
**Evidence: Moderate (health effects), Limited (exercise specific thresholds)**
**Pool: agency guidance (EPA), not exercise trials**
**Real world data: 2,756,553 finishes in six World Marathon Majors (performance), and cohorts of 52,061 Danish adults (mortality), observational**
Exercise raises breathing volume and the dose of inhaled pollutants. Ozone peaks on hot, bright afternoons, carbon monoxide impairs performance, and riders should avoid busy roads (Carlisle & Sharp, 2001; Giles & Koehle, 2014). Higher race day nitrogen dioxide (NO2) was linked to slower marathon times, about 1.4 to 1.6 minutes per µg/m3, with larger effects in recreational than elite runners; PM2.5 was not significant (Donaldson et al., 2026). In 334 collegiate runners, higher PM2.5 and ozone in the 3 weeks before a race were linked to slower times even within Good to Moderate AQI (Cusick et al., 2023). For health, the benefits of cycling and sport on mortality were not removed by high NO2 in 52,061 adults (Andersen et al., 2015), and a model found that at typical urban PM2.5 (22 µg/m3) the benefits of cycling far outweigh pollution harm, while at 100 µg/m3 harm exceeded benefit after about 1.5 hours of cycling per day (Tainio et al., 2016). The U.S. AQI runs from Good (0 to 50) through Moderate (51 to 100), Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups (101 to 150), Unhealthy (151 to 200), Very Unhealthy (201 to 300) and Hazardous (301 and above) (U.S. EPA AirNow, n.d.). EPA smoke guidance advises lowering effort, shortening time outdoors, avoiding strenuous activity when smoky, and treating coughing, wheezing or shortness of breath as a sign to reduce exposure at once (U.S. EPA, n.d.).

## Rules for the coach
- Heat acclimation before a hot race or hot season: plan 7 to 14 consecutive or near consecutive days of heat sessions of 60 to 90 minutes, at easy to moderate intensity, in conditions that cause heavy sweating and a clear rise in core temperature (hot room, outdoor heat, or extra clothing indoors). Most of the benefit comes in the first week; longer blocks give more.
- Schedule the block to finish in the final 1 to 2 weeks before the hot event; if done earlier, add 1 to 2 heat sessions per week to maintain it, since adaptation fades over weeks. A few heat sessions re-acclimate quickly.
- During the first days of heat training, reduce planned intensity or volume; heat sessions count as training stress.
- Heat training for temperate racing: describe it as possibly helpful, not guaranteed. For riders who want to try it, a block of about 5 sessions of 50 minutes per week for 5 weeks is the protocol with published gains in hemoglobin mass, so far from one research group. Do not promise gains from post ride sauna alone.
- Heat safety: stop a heat session and cool down at once if the rider reports dizziness, confusion, nausea, headache, chills or stops sweating. Advise urgent medical care for confusion or collapse in heat, which can be heat stroke. Riders with heart disease, prior heat stroke, or on medications that affect heat tolerance should get medical clearance before heat training.
- Hot race day: drink about 6 mL/kg every 2 to 3 hours before, use 0.5 to 0.7 g/L sodium drinks for rides over 1 hour, keep body mass loss under about 2%, and never finish heavier than you started. Expect slower times as temperature rises, and more so for less trained riders.
- Precooling for time trials and hot races: about 1 L of ice slurry or cold drink in the 30 to 60 minutes before the start, plus ice vest or cold towels during warm up; shorten the warm up in severe heat. Practice it in training first. Avoid heavy precooling before short sprint efforts.
- Lower target power in heat: expect a lower sustainable power until acclimatized and pace by heart rate and feel as well as watts.
- Cold weather: dress in layers chosen for the rider, protect hands, feet, ears and face, and plan for the descent and stops. Advise extra caution or an indoor ride when the wind chill is below about -27 °C (-18 °F), on icy roads, or for riders with asthma or heart conditions. Warm up longer before hard efforts in the cold.
- Riding at altitude: for each 1,000 m above about 500 to 800 m, lower threshold based power targets by roughly 5 to 7% for an unacclimatized rider, and more above about 2,500 m. Short sprints under about 2 minutes need little change. Pace the first efforts conservatively.
- Racing at altitude without time to acclimatize: start conservatively, use the lower power targets above, and keep carbohydrate and fluid intake up.
- Altitude camps: to target red cell gains, live at about 2,000 to 2,500 m for at least 3 to 4 weeks; train hard sessions lower if possible; keep high intensity efforts short with long recoveries; increase sleep and fueling in the first days.
- Iron before altitude: advise riders to have iron status checked by their doctor before an altitude block and not to start iron supplements without medical advice.
- Hypoxic tents: tell riders that red cell gains appear to need at least about 12 hours per day (16 or more is better) for at least 3 to 4 weeks, and that a placebo controlled study in cyclists and pooled data in athletes found no clear benefit from passive exposure. Do not promise results. Do not recommend altitude masks or short train high sessions for VO2max.
- Altitude illness: if a rider at altitude reports severe headache, vomiting, unusual breathlessness at rest, confusion or poor coordination, tell them to stop, descend and seek medical care.
- Air quality: check the local AQI (or the AirNow Fire and Smoke Map in the U.S.) before outdoor rides.
  - AQI 0 to 50: no change.
  - AQI 51 to 100: no change for most riders; riders who are unusually sensitive to air pollution should consider shorter or easier rides.
  - AQI 101 to 150: riders with asthma, heart or lung disease, older riders and children should move hard sessions indoors or cut them short; others can ride but should avoid long, hard efforts if symptoms occur.
  - AQI 151 to 200: move hard or long sessions indoors for everyone.
  - AQI above 200: indoor training only.
  - Any visible or smelled smoke, or any coughing, wheezing or chest tightness: shorten or stop the ride and move indoors.
- Ozone: on high ozone days, ride in the morning rather than the afternoon. On any day, prefer routes away from heavy traffic.
- On ordinary air quality days, tell riders the health benefit of riding outweighs the pollution risk; the limits above matter on poor air days.
- These AQI rules are the app's conservative interpretation of EPA categories, not an exercise specific research threshold. Riders with asthma or heart or lung disease should follow their doctor's advice on exercising in poor air.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- Whether heat acclimation improves performance in cool conditions is disputed; pooled data show a small VO2max gain, but cyclist trials disagree (Lorenzo et al., 2010; Karlsen et al., 2015; Keiser et al., 2015; Waldron et al., 2021).
- Hemoglobin mass gains from heat training come from one research group, about 160 athletes (Lundby et al., 2023; Rønnestad et al., 2021, 2022, 2025, 2026).
- Heat and cooling studies are mostly in men; the largest cooling pool had no women (van de Kerkhof et al., 2024).
- The largest heat and pollution datasets are marathon running, not cycling, and are observational (El Helou et al., 2012; Donaldson et al., 2026).
- Live high train low results vary widely between individuals, and placebo and training camp effects are hard to remove (Chapman et al., 1998; Bonetti & Hopkins, 2009).
- Normobaric (tent) protocols show no clear benefit for athletes (Siebenmann et al., 2012; Kohlbrenner et al., 2026).
- The 12 hours per day minimum hypoxic dose comes from a review, not a dose finding trial (Millet et al., 2010).
- The 6.5% per 1,000 m loss is pooled from mostly short lab tests in unacclimatized people (Deb et al., 2018); losses in long events and acclimatized riders may differ.
- Cold effects on cycling performance are poorly studied (Castellani & Tipton, 2015).
- There are no validated AQI thresholds specific to training intensity or duration; the coach rules for air quality are precautionary.
