# Structuring the Training Week

## Summary
A training week (a "microcycle") is the basic building block of any plan. The research gives a few clear anchors and leaves a lot to coaching judgment. The anchors are: most riding should be easy, with a small number of hard sessions; after a hard interval day the body needs roughly 48 hours before it is fully ready for another one, while easy rides cause little lasting fatigue; at least about 3 sessions per week are needed to keep improving once a rider is reasonably fit; and fitness can be held for weeks on as few as 2 sessions per week if those sessions stay hard. Long rides build the kind of endurance that short daily rides do not.

What the research does not settle is the exact layout: which weekday gets the intervals, whether the long ride is on Saturday or Sunday, or how many rest days to take. These are mostly coaching convention shaped by work and family time. This file gives templates for 3, 4, 5 and 6 riding days per week and marks which parts come from evidence and which are convention.

Intensity terms used here (approximate, convention): easy or endurance 56 to 75% FTP (RPE 2 to 4), tempo 76 to 90% FTP (RPE 5 to 6), threshold 91 to 105% FTP (RPE 7), VO2max intervals 106 to 120% FTP (RPE 8 to 9), sprints or anaerobic above 120% FTP (RPE 9 to 10).

## Size of the evidence
The pool is small. No trial has compared whole weekly layouts, and recovery timelines come from studies of 10 to 20 people. The best pooled data are on intensity mix (437 athletes in 17 studies; 348 in an individual data analysis).

The biggest datasets are observational runner logs: about 300,000 marathoners on training breaks, 119,000 on intensity mix, 14,000 on volume, and Garmin cohorts of 5,200 to 7,400 runners on load spikes, frequency and injury. They show associations, not cause, and running injuries may not transfer to cycling. No large cyclist log study was found. Rest days, day order and back to back hard days rest on small studies or convention.

## Key findings

### Keep most sessions easy and limit hard sessions to about 2 per week in normal weeks
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 17 studies, about 437 athletes (trained endurance athletes, mixed sports including cyclists)**
**Real world data: 119,452 athletes, marathon training logs from a fitness platform, observational**
Descriptive data from successful endurance athletes show about 80% of sessions at low intensity and about 20% containing hard work, and adding more HIT to already well trained athletes has not shown long term gains (Seiler, 2010). Champion skiers did about 500 sessions per year with roughly 90% of training time below the first lactate threshold and about 23% of sessions classed as HIT (Tønnessen et al., 2014). Trials in trained cyclists that produced good gains used about 2 HIT sessions per week mixed with high volumes of easy riding (Sylta et al., 2016; Rønnestad et al., 2014a). In a 9 week trial of well trained endurance athletes (runners, cyclists, triathletes, skiers), a polarized program (mostly easy plus HIT) improved VO2peak more than threshold focused, HIT only or high volume only programs (Stöggl and Sperlich, 2014), though this was a small, mixed sport study. Larger pooled analyses found polarized only slightly better for VO2peak in short studies and highly trained athletes, no better for time trials (Silva Oliveira et al., 2024), and no different from pyramidal (Rosenblat et al., 2025). In logs from 119,452 marathoners, faster runners did much more volume, mostly easy, while moderate and hard time was similar across levels (Muniz-Pumares et al., 2025). In about 14,000 runners (1.6 million sessions), performance correlated with training volume and intensity (Emig and Peltonen, 2020). These are associations in runners. The figure of 2 hard sessions comes from trial designs, not from a trial testing 1 vs 2 vs 3.

### Recovery after hard sessions takes about 48 hours; easy riding recovers within a day
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: no pooled analysis; 1 narrative review plus single studies of 10 to 17 people (trained runners and cyclists)**
A review of heart rate based recovery found complete cardiac autonomic recovery takes up to 24 h after low intensity exercise, 24 to 48 h after threshold intensity, and at least 48 h after high intensity exercise, with fitter people recovering faster and duration mattering less than intensity (Stanley, Peake and Buchheit, 2013). In highly trained runners, 60 to 120 min below the first ventilatory threshold caused almost no autonomic disturbance, while threshold and interval sessions caused a similar, larger delay, and less trained people recovered more slowly after the same intervals (Seiler, Haugen and Kuffel, 2007). After cycling repeated sprints (10x10 s), neuromuscular fatigue was fully restored after 48 h but not after 24 h (Milioni et al., 2021, n = 10). After 6x30 s cycling intervals, physical performance in trained young and masters cyclists recovered at similar rates, but masters riders felt less recovered and more sore at 48 h (Borges et al., 2018, n = 17). Note that heart rate recovery is not the same as full recovery of energy stores or muscle (Stanley, Peake and Buchheit, 2013). Graded down from Moderate: no meta-analysis of recovery time was found and the cycling data come from groups of 10 and 17. The 48 h rule stays as a sensible default, not a proven threshold.

### Refuelling supports back to back training, but I could not verify a precise glycogen recovery time
**Evidence: Moderate (for the guidance) / Limited (for exact hours)**
**Pool: none pooled here; guidance comes from expert consensus reviews (N not applicable)**
Nutrition reviews recommend matching daily carbohydrate to the training load (in g per kg body mass), starting carbohydrate intake soon after the session when the gap to the next session is under 8 h, and spreading meals more freely when the recovery period is about 24 h (Burke, Kiens and Ivy, 2004; Burke et al., 2011). The abstracts reviewed do not give a fixed number of hours for full glycogen restoration, so the app should not state one as fact. The common claim that hard days need 48 to 72 h for glycogen alone is not supported by what was checked here; the 48 h figure above comes from autonomic and neuromuscular data.

### Alternating hard and easy days reduces "monotony", which is linked to illness
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 1 study, 25 athletes (experienced athletes, mixed sports)**
In 25 experienced athletes, many illnesses came when athletes exceeded individual thresholds of strain, which combines weekly load and monotony (how similar each day's load is) (Foster, 1998). This supports hard days truly hard and easy days truly easy. It is observational and did not test weekly layouts.

### Sudden jumps in session length are linked to overuse injury in runners
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: no pooled analysis; 2 cohorts, about 6,079 runners (Garmin users and novice runners)**
**Real world data: 5,205 athletes, Garmin watch data (Garmin-RUNSAFE), observational; non cyclists**

In 5,205 runners tracked for 18 months (588,071 sessions, 1,820 injuries), a single run more than 10% longer than the longest run of the past 30 days was linked to more overuse injury (hazard ratio about 1.5 to 2.3); weekly ratios such as the acute:chronic workload ratio showed no such link (Frandsen et al., 2025). In 874 novice runners, weekly increases over 30% had a borderline link to some overuse injuries (Nielsen et al., 2014). The acute:chronic workload ratio has no proven causal link to injury (Impellizzeri et al., 2020). Cycling has far less impact loading, so this transfers only loosely, but it supports building the long ride gradually.

### Back to back hard days and "crash" blocks can work in short doses for trained athletes
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 6 pooled block studies, about 107 athletes (cyclists, skiers, hockey players); other trials are single studies of under about 35 athletes**
Trained cyclists who did 5 HIT sessions in one week (then 1 per week for 3 weeks) gained more than those spreading 2 per week (Rønnestad, Hansen and Ellefsen, 2014a). Elite junior alpine skiers did 15 HIT sessions (4x4 min at 90 to 95% HRmax) in 11 days and improved VO2max by 6%, but jump power was still lower 7 days later, suggesting lasting muscle fatigue (Breil et al., 2010). Elite cyclists did 6 consecutive days of HIT then a 5 day taper and improved more than with a taper alone (Rønnestad and Vikmoen, 2019). These blocks were always followed by several easier days. In triathletes, pushing overload far enough to cause a real drop in performance (functional overreaching) removed the benefit and raised infection rates (Aubry et al., 2014). All of these studies are small and mostly in trained to elite athletes. The block meta-analysis pooled only 107 athletes (Mølmen, Øfsteng and Rønnestad, 2019), so this is graded down from Moderate.

### Frequency: at least 3 sessions per week to improve; 2 hard sessions per week can maintain
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 1 pooled review, N not verified (mostly non athletes); 85 studies, 137 runner cohorts, N not reported in abstract (marathon meta-regression, observational)**
**Real world data: about 300,000 athletes, marathon training logs, observational; non cyclists**
A pooled review of mostly non athletes found that 2 sessions per week can improve less fit people, but once VO2max is above about 50 mL/kg/min, at least 3 sessions per week were needed (Wenger and Bell, 1986). In recently trained young adults, VO2max was maintained for 15 weeks when frequency dropped from 6 to 2 or 4 days per week at the same intensity and duration (Hickson and Rosenkoetter, 1981), and when daily duration was cut by up to two thirds (Hickson et al., 1982). Cutting intensity instead lost fitness (Hickson et al., 1985). These are old, small studies in non cyclists, so treat them as directional. Larger observational data favor consistency: across 137 marathon cohorts, more runs and distance per week went with faster marathons (Doherty et al., 2020); in about 300,000 marathoners, breaks of 7 days or more went with 5 to 8% slower finishes (Feely et al., 2022); and in 7,391 Garmin users, running under 2 times per week went with more injuries per 1,000 km (Abrahamson et al., 2025). This is association in runners, so the grade stays Limited.

### Long sessions matter for long event endurance
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 1 small trial in non athletes; plus 85 studies, 137 runner cohorts, N not reported in abstract (observational meta-regression)**
When daily training was cut from 40 to 13 minutes, VO2max and 5 minute endurance were kept, but endurance of 2 h or more fell by about 10% (Hickson et al., 1982). Elite endurance athletes accumulate large volumes of long, easy training (Seiler, 2010; Tønnessen et al., 2014). In marathon runners, longer longest runs and more runs of 32 km or more were linked to faster finishes across 137 cohorts (Doherty et al., 2020), an association in runners. This supports keeping a weekly long ride for riders targeting events longer than about 2 h. Putting it on the weekend is coaching convention based on available time.

### Same total volume: more frequent sessions vs fewer longer ones
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 1 study, N not verified (untrained men, single leg lab model)**
Direct trials in trained cyclists comparing, for example, 5x1 h vs 3x1 h 40 min at matched volume were not found. In untrained men doing one leg knee extension, training twice every second day (second session at low glycogen) beat once daily training at the same work for endurance time and some enzymes (Hansen et al., 2005), a lab model mainly about low glycogen. The practical reasoning for cyclists is: frequency of at least 3 to 4 sessions per week matters for fitter riders (Wenger and Bell, 1986), while one longer ride per week covers long endurance (Hickson et al., 1982).

### Rest days
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: none, coaching convention**
No trial was found that tests the number of full rest days for amateur cyclists. Champion skiers trained almost every day and rarely took rest days even before key races (Tønnessen et al., 2014), but they train full time. Because easy riding causes little autonomic disturbance in trained athletes (Seiler, Haugen and Kuffel, 2007), an easy ride can often replace a rest day for experienced riders. In 209 half Ironman triathletes the fastest group reported fewer rest days (Gilinsky et al., 2014), a cross sectional association, not a cause. One to two rest days per week for time limited amateurs is convention, and is mostly dictated by schedule.

## Rules for the coach
- Weekly intensity mix (a suggestion the rider can change): about 75 to 80% of sessions easy (56 to 75% FTP, RPE 2 to 4). The hard sessions can be VO2max or threshold; a pyramidal mix with some tempo is fine for recreational riders. Hard sessions (threshold or VO2max, RPE 7 to 9): 1 per week for 3 day weeks or beginners, 2 per week by default, 3 maximum in normal weeks for experienced riders.
- Space hard sessions at least 48 h apart in normal weeks (for example Tuesday and Thursday, or Tuesday and Saturday). For riders over about 50, or those reporting high soreness or poor sleep, allow 72 h.
- The day after a hard session is either rest or easy riding only (under 75% FTP, RPE 2 to 4, under 90 min unless it is the long ride).
- Easy rides under about 2 h at RPE 2 to 4 do not need a recovery day after them and can be placed on any day.
- Back to back hard days: allowed only for riders with at least 1 year of structured training, at most 2 consecutive hard days in normal weeks, and always followed by at least 1 rest or easy day.
- HIT blocks ("crash" weeks): at most 1 block every 4 to 8 weeks, 4 to 6 HIT sessions in 5 to 7 days, VO2max sessions at 106 to 120% FTP (for example 5x4 min or 5x5 min, RPE 8 to 9), followed by 3 to 5 easy or rest days. Stop the block early if interval power drops more than about 5% within a session or RPE for the same power rises by 2 or more points.
- Long ride: one per week for riders targeting events over 2 h, 1.5 to 2.5 times the length of the typical weekday ride, mostly at 56 to 75% FTP. Default it to Saturday or Sunday (convention), and do not place a VO2max session the day before. Build it gradually: aim to make the longest ride no more than about 10 to 20% longer than the longest ride of the past month (extrapolated from runner data; convention for cycling).
- Minimum frequency: 3 riding days per week to make progress; 2 sessions per week (with hard efforts kept) can hold fitness for a few weeks during busy periods. Prioritize consistency: a short ride beats a missed week, and avoid gaps of 7 days or more without riding when possible, especially close to a goal event.
- Weekday time limited riders (45 to 75 min): use weekday slots for the hard sessions (intervals fit short sessions) and easy spins; put volume on the weekend.
- Rest days: at least 1 full rest day per week for riders on 3 to 5 days; 6 day riders can use 1 rest day or 1 very easy short day. If the rider reports persistent fatigue, add a rest day before cutting hard sessions.
- Fuel: on days with hard or long sessions, and when the next session is less than about 8 h away, prompt the rider to eat carbohydrate soon after the session. Do not quote a fixed hours figure for glycogen recovery.
- Keep strength or other sports out of the 24 h before a key interval session where possible (convention).
- Do not use the acute:chronic workload ratio as a hard rule for load or injury warnings; its causal link to injury is unproven.
- If the rider loses a day, drop an easy ride first, then shorten the long ride, and keep the hard sessions. If two hard sessions would end up less than 48 h apart, drop or downgrade the second one to tempo or endurance.

### Example templates (Monday to Sunday)
These layouts follow the evidence on intensity mix, 48 h spacing and the long ride. The exact days are convention, built around a Monday to Friday work week. Swap days freely if the 48 h spacing and hard then easy order are kept.

**3 days per week (beginner or very busy, about 3 to 6 h)**
- Mon: rest
- Tue: hard. Threshold or VO2max, 60 to 75 min (for example 3x10 min at 91 to 100% FTP, RPE 7, or 5x3 min at 106 to 115% FTP, RPE 8)
- Wed: rest
- Thu: endurance with some tempo, 60 to 75 min (for example 2x15 min at 76 to 88% FTP, RPE 5 to 6)
- Fri: rest
- Sat: rest or optional short spin
- Sun: long ride, 2 to 3.5 h at 56 to 75% FTP (RPE 2 to 4)
Notes: with only 3 days, one session can be hard and the others moderate. A second true hard session (Thursday) is fine for fitter riders if the long ride stays easy.

**4 days per week (about 5 to 8 h)**
- Mon: rest
- Tue: hard. VO2max or threshold, 60 to 90 min
- Wed: rest or optional 45 min easy
- Thu: hard. Threshold or tempo, 60 to 90 min (for example 2x20 min at 88 to 95% FTP, RPE 6 to 7)
- Fri: rest
- Sat: endurance 1.5 to 2 h at 56 to 75% FTP, optional short sprints
- Sun: long ride 2.5 to 4 h at 56 to 75% FTP
Notes: Tuesday and Thursday are 48 h apart. The weekend is easy volume, so the 2 hard sessions are 20 to 25% of sessions.

**5 days per week (about 7 to 11 h)**
- Mon: rest
- Tue: hard. VO2max, 60 to 90 min (for example 5x4 min at 106 to 120% FTP, RPE 8 to 9)
- Wed: easy 60 to 75 min at 56 to 70% FTP
- Thu: hard. Threshold, 60 to 90 min
- Fri: rest
- Sat: long ride 3 to 4.5 h at 56 to 75% FTP, optionally including 20 to 40 min of tempo
- Sun: endurance 1.5 to 2.5 h at 56 to 75% FTP
Notes: Saturday long then Sunday endurance is a common weekend pairing (convention). If a group ride on Saturday is hard, count it as the second hard session and make Thursday easy.

**6 days per week (experienced, about 9 to 15 h)**
- Mon: rest
- Tue: hard. VO2max, 75 to 90 min
- Wed: easy 60 to 90 min
- Thu: hard. Threshold or race specific, 75 to 90 min
- Fri: easy 45 to 60 min, optional 4 to 6 short sprints or openers
- Sat: long ride 3.5 to 5 h at 56 to 75% FTP, or a hard group ride or race (then make Thursday easy)
- Sun: endurance 2 to 3 h at 56 to 75% FTP
Notes: a third hard session is allowed only for experienced riders who recover well, and never on 3 consecutive days.

**HIT block week (experienced riders only, used as one week in a block plan)**
- Mon: hard (VO2max). Tue: hard (VO2max). Wed: easy 60 min. Thu: hard (VO2max). Fri: hard (VO2max). Sat: easy 1.5 to 2 h. Sun: hard (VO2max) or easy, then 3 to 5 easier days. This follows the shape of the block studies (5 HIT sessions in a week; Rønnestad, Hansen and Ellefsen, 2014a) but the exact day order is convention.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- No trials compare specific weekly layouts (which days get intervals, Saturday vs Sunday long ride). Templates are convention built on general principles.
- Recovery timelines come mostly from heart rate variability data, small samples, runners and sprint protocols. No meta-analysis was found.
- The large training log datasets are runners and observational; none was found for cyclists. Recovery after long threshold or VO2max cycling sessions has not been mapped well.
- A precise glycogen recovery time was not verified from sources in this review; the 48 to 72 h figure often quoted for glycogen is not supported here.
- Evidence on minimum frequency comes from old studies in untrained or recently trained people and pooled data mostly from non athletes.
- Frequency vs session length at matched volume has not been tested in trained cyclists. The one relevant study used single leg knee extension in untrained men.
- The number of rest days needed by amateurs is not studied; it depends heavily on life stress, sleep and age.
- Masters riders may need more time between hard sessions, but the evidence is mainly about feeling less recovered rather than measured performance (Borges et al., 2018).
- The % FTP and RPE ranges are practical translations, not values used in most of the cited studies.
