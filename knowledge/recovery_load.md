# Recovery and Training Load Management

## Summary
Training works by overload followed by recovery. Short dips in performance after a hard block are normal and usually clear within about 2 weeks; dips that last weeks to months are a warning sign (Meeusen et al., 2013). Deliberately driving a rider into functional overreaching before a taper does not seem to give a bigger peak and raises illness risk (Aubry et al., 2014). The most reliable early warnings are falling performance plus how the rider feels: simple daily wellness questions track training load better than most blood or heart markers (Saw et al., 2016).

The fitness and fatigue numbers (CTL, ATL, TSB) are a useful bookkeeping tool built on the Banister model, but the model's parameters are uncertain for any one person and the common targets, such as ramp rates of 5 to 8 CTL per week and race day TSB of +15 to +25, are coaching conventions, not tested limits. The acute to chronic workload ratio has serious statistical problems and should not be used to predict injury. Resting HRV can help time hard sessions, though the pooled performance benefit is small. Sleep is the best recovery tool: 7 hours or more for adults, and sleep loss of 6 hours or less clearly hurts performance. Cold water immersion may blunt strength gains, compression and massage mostly help soreness, and alcohol after training impairs muscle repair.

## Size of the evidence
Most of this topic rests on small studies. Classic overreaching experiments used 8 to 33 athletes, HRV guided training trials 17 to 40, and the Banister model was tested in 9 swimmers. A systematic review found no study that tracked athletes objectively from healthy into overtraining syndrome. The bigger pools are meta-analyses: sleep loss (69 publications, 959 people, 89% men), massage (29 studies, 1,012 people), recovery methods (99 studies), cold water immersion (an umbrella review of 15 meta-analyses covering 161 studies) and the ACWR in team sports (16 studies, 797 athletes).

The largest datasets are observational: 5,205 runners with 588,071 Garmin logged sessions (load spikes and injury), more than 21,000 wearable users with about 2 million nights of HRV, 2,596 women with 1.2 million days of wearable data, and a survey of 376 young athletes on overtraining. None of these are cyclists, and they show associations, not cause and effect. Ramp rate and TSB targets have no study behind them at all.

## Key findings

### Functional overreaching, non-functional overreaching, and overtraining syndrome
**Evidence: Moderate (consensus definitions; diagnosis relies on exclusion)**
**Pool: consensus statement; experiments are small (8 to 33 athletes); 1 systematic review found no study with objective proof of the move into overtraining syndrome**
**Real world data: 376 young athletes, English survey, observational**
The ECSS and ACSM consensus defines overreaching as a short term performance drop from training and other stress, with recovery in days to weeks. If it is followed by a performance gain after recovery, it is functional overreaching (FOR). If recovery takes weeks to months it is non-functional overreaching (NFOR), and long term decline taking months or more is overtraining syndrome (OTS) (Meeusen et al., 2013). No single blood, hormone, or immune marker can diagnose OTS; diagnosis means first ruling out illness, infection, low energy or carbohydrate intake, iron deficiency, and other causes. The 2023 IOC REDs statement adds that underfuelling should be ruled out first (Mountjoy et al., 2023).

In 8 trained cyclists, 2 weeks of intensified training lowered maximal power and time trial performance, raised mood disturbance by 29% and lowered maximal heart rate by about 9%, while lactate and hormone markers did not change; overreaching appeared after about 7 days (Halson et al., 2002). In 33 triathletes, 11 of 23 in an overload group became functionally overreached. The ones who were only acutely fatigued gained more from the taper than those who were overreached, and infections were much more common in the overreached group (70% versus 20% and 10%) (Aubry et al., 2014). A systematic review found no study that objectively tracked performance and mood from healthy into overtraining syndrome (Weakley et al., 2022). In a survey of 376 young athletes, 29% said they had been non-functionally overreached or overtrained at least once; it was more common in girls, individual sports and elite athletes, and training load alone did not predict it (Matos et al., 2011).

### Warning signs that matter most
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: consensus statement plus small experiments (8 to 33 athletes); no large prospective study**
Performance decline at a normal or higher effort, plus raised fatigue and mood disturbance, are the earliest and most useful signs (Halson et al., 2002; Meeusen et al., 2013). A lower maximal heart rate and inability to reach normal interval heart rates are common in overreached cyclists. Other signs include sleep problems, frequent illness, loss of motivation, and heavy legs. The consensus advises performance records, cutting load or resting when performance drops, avoiding monotony, and at least one passive rest day per week (Meeusen et al., 2013).

### Subjective wellness measures beat most objective markers
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 56 studies, N not reported in abstract (mixed sports, mostly small groups)**
A systematic review of 56 studies found subjective and objective measures generally did not agree. Where they differed, 85% of cases favoured subjective measures for sensitivity and consistency. Subjective well being got worse with acute and chronic load increases and improved with load reductions (Saw et al., 2016). Responsive items included fatigue, recovery, well being, non training stress, and feeling "in shape"; the authors advise short daily questions and looking at single items, not just a total.

### The Banister fitness fatigue model and CTL, ATL, TSB
**Evidence: Moderate (as a concept); Limited (as an individual predictor)**
**Pool: no meta-analysis; single group modelling studies (9 swimmers in Hellard et al.; N not reported in abstract for the 2025 reanalysis)**
The Banister impulse response model treats performance as fitness minus fatigue, where each training dose adds to both and fatigue decays faster than fitness (Clarke & Skiba, 2013). In TrainingPeaks, CTL is an exponentially weighted average of daily TSS over 42 days, ATL over 7 days, and TSB is CTL minus ATL. CTL and ATL are load averages, not measured fitness or fatigue. In a study of 9 elite swimmers, the Banister model fit the data reasonably well (R squared about 0.79), but key parameters had wide confidence intervals and were highly correlated (Hellard et al., 2006). A 2025 Bayesian reanalysis with two independent datasets found the model poorly identified and overfitted: adding the fatigue part did not improve prediction (Marchal et al., 2025). The model ignores sleep, illness, life stress, heat, altitude, and fuelling, and TSS depends on an accurate FTP.

### Ramp rate limits are convention, not evidence
**Evidence: Limited (coaching convention)**
**Pool: none, coaching convention**
**Real world data: 5,205 runners, Garmin-RUNSAFE cohort with Garmin device data, observational**
Joe Friel suggests about 5 to 8 CTL points per week suits most athletes, and a few can handle more than 10 for about a week, based on coaching experience, not a cited study (Friel, 2015). No controlled study has tested CTL ramp rates against injury, illness, or performance in cyclists. Team sport research links rapid load spikes with soft tissue injury and suggests a well built chronic load protects (Gabbett, 2016). The largest load and injury cohort, 5,205 recreational runners over 18 months, found that a single run more than 10% longer than the longest run of the past 30 days went with a higher rate of overuse injury (hazard ratio 1.64 for 10% to 30% longer, 2.28 for more than double). A week to week increase showed no link (Frandsen et al., 2025). Cycling has far less impact than running, so this is a hint, not a cycling rule.

### The acute to chronic workload ratio is not a reliable injury predictor
**Evidence: Moderate (for the critique)**
**Pool: 16 studies, about 797 team sport athletes (meta-analysis); statistical critiques use team sport data**
**Real world data: 5,205 runners, Garmin-RUNSAFE cohort, observational**
The ACWR (for example, last 7 days of load divided by last 28) became popular in team sports. The common "coupled" calculation puts the acute week inside the chronic average, creating a built in correlation (Lolli et al., 2019). No studies have properly estimated a causal effect, so changing the ratio to prevent injury is a conjecture, and ratios have known statistical problems (Impellizzeri et al., 2020). A follow up reanalysis found that the chronic load term adds little, and recommended dropping the ACWR and its theory (Impellizzeri et al., 2021). ATL divided by CTL in a cycling app is a version of this ratio and inherits the same problems. A 2026 meta-analysis found a small to moderate link between high ACWR and injury but with extreme differences between studies (I squared about 96%), and concluded it should not be used as a stand alone predictor (Ding et al., 2026). In the 5,205 runner cohort, higher ACWR even went with fewer injuries (Frandsen et al., 2025).

### Taper and race day form
**Evidence: Moderate (taper structure); Limited (TSB targets)**
**Pool: 27 studies (Bosquet et al.) and 14 endurance studies (Wang et al.), N not reported in abstracts; TSB targets: none, coaching convention**
A meta-analysis of 27 studies found the best taper lasted about 2 weeks, with training volume cut by 41% to 60% and intensity and frequency kept the same (Bosquet et al., 2007). A 2023 meta-analysis of 14 endurance studies agreed on the 41% to 60% cut and found tapers of up to 21 days worked. It also found bigger time trial gains when the taper followed a planned overload block (Wang et al., 2023), which sits uneasily with the Aubry finding. TSB race targets are convention. Friel aims for TSB about +15 to +25 for A races (some race best at +5 to +10), -10 to 0 for B races, -10 to -30 during hard training, and warns that below -30 raises illness and injury risk (Friel, n.d.). These ranges are untested.

### HRV guided training can help time hard sessions
**Evidence: Limited (pooled benefit over fixed plans is small and not significant)**
**Pool: 1 meta-analysis of small trials, N not reported in abstract (single trials 17 to 40 athletes)**
**Real world data: more than 21,000 people, about 2 million nights, consumer wearable dataset (device not named in abstract), observational**
In 40 recreational runners, HRV guided training (hard sessions only when morning HRV was in the normal band) used fewer hard sessions yet improved 3000 m performance; the group difference was small (Vesterinen et al., 2016). In 17 well trained cyclists over 8 weeks, the HRV guided group improved peak power (5.1%), second threshold power (13.9%), and 40 minute time trial (7.3%), but the group comparison was not clearly different (Javaloyes et al., 2019). In elite athletes HRV can move in either direction with fatigue, so averaging over several days and comparing to each athlete's own baseline is advised (Plews et al., 2013). The grade was lowered from Moderate because the pooled data are weak: HRV guided training raised HRV itself, but its edge for VO2max, threshold and endurance performance was small and not significant (Manresa-Rocamora et al., 2021). In more than 21,000 wearable users, at least 5 of 7 nights were needed to estimate weekly HRV variability reliably, and night to night swings were larger with more alcohol and shorter, irregular sleep (Grosicki et al., 2026). In women, HRV also drifts with the menstrual cycle (see the women topic).

### Sleep: at least 7 hours, and loss of 6 hours or less hurts performance
**Evidence: Strong (sleep loss harms); Limited (extension helps)**
**Pool: 69 publications, about 959 people (89% men) for sleep loss; extension rests on small single studies**
**Real world data: 2,596 women, 1.2 million days of consumer wearable data, observational**
The AASM and Sleep Research Society recommend at least 7 hours per night for adults (Watson et al., 2015). A meta-analysis of 69 publications found that sleep loss to 6 hours or less lowered exercise performance by about 7.6% on average, with large variation. Full deprivation and cutting sleep at the end of the night (early waking) were consistently harmful, each extra hour awake before exercise cost about 0.4%, and afternoon or evening performance was hurt more than morning performance (Craven et al., 2022). In 11 college basketball players, extending sleep (aiming for 10 hours in bed) added about 111 minutes per night and improved sprint time, shooting accuracy, and mood over 5 to 7 weeks; there was no control group (Mah et al., 2011). The extension grade was lowered to Limited: no larger controlled data were found. Early afternoon naps improved endurance performance in 12 small trials (179 team sport athletes) (Zheng et al., 2026). In 2,596 women, a 10% drop in weekly sleep went with a 1.2% rise in resting heart rate (Gonzalez et al., 2026), and a review of 18 soccer studies linked poor sleep with more injury and illness (Cantón et al., 2026); both are associations. The athlete sleep consensus notes most studies lack women and good controls and advises individual sleep targets (Walsh et al., 2021).

### Cold water immersion may blunt strength gains; endurance effects unclear
**Evidence: Moderate (strength); Limited (endurance)**
**Pool: umbrella review of 15 meta-analyses covering 161 studies (mostly low quality); 8 studies for the strength blunting meta-analysis**
In 21 active men doing 12 weeks of strength training, 10 minutes of cold water immersion after each session led to smaller gains in strength and muscle mass than active recovery (Roberts et al., 2015). A meta-analysis found regular cold water immersion reduced resistance training adaptations, while endurance performance gains did not seem to be harmed, based on few studies (Malta et al., 2021). A meta-analysis of 99 recovery studies found immersion, cryotherapy, compression, active recovery, and massage all reduced soreness to some degree after exercise (Dupuy et al., 2018). An umbrella review of 15 meta-analyses (161 studies) found cold water helped power and soreness at 24 to 96 hours but impaired strength and muscle gains with regular use, and gave no later benefit for endurance adaptation (Berjisian et al., 2026).

### Compression and massage mainly help soreness and short term recovery
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 29 studies, about 1,012 people (massage); 23 studies, N not reported in abstract (compression)**
A meta-analysis of 23 studies found compression garments gave a small benefit to recovery (effect size 0.38), mostly for strength recovery (Brown et al., 2017). A meta-analysis of 29 studies (1,012 people) found massage did not improve sprint, jump, strength, endurance, or fatigue, but gave small benefits for soreness and flexibility (Davis et al., 2020). Massage was ranked the most effective method for soreness and perceived fatigue in another meta-analysis (Dupuy et al., 2018). Placebo effects are likely for soreness.

### Alcohol impairs recovery
**Evidence: Limited (small mechanistic trials)**
**Pool: 1 trial of 8 men for muscle protein synthesis, plus a narrative review**
**Real world data: more than 21,000 people, consumer wearable dataset, observational (alcohol linked with less stable night HRV)**
In 8 active men, drinking alcohol after a combined strength and cycling session lowered muscle protein synthesis, even when protein was eaten with it (Parr et al., 2014). A review concluded that alcohol at levels athletes often drink may slow recovery from muscle damage. Effects depend on dose and timing (Barnes, 2014). The grade was lowered from Moderate because the direct evidence is one 8 person trial; large wearable data showing less stable night HRV after drinking agree in direction but are observational (Grosicki et al., 2026). The advice stays the same.

## Rules for the coach
- Daily check in: ask 4 to 5 questions on a 1 to 5 scale (fatigue, muscle soreness, sleep quality, stress, mood or motivation). Use a rolling 7 day baseline. If 2 or more items are 2 or more points worse than baseline, or the total is more than 20% below baseline, replace that day's intervals with easy riding (zone 1 to 2, 60 minutes or less) or rest. Items from Saw et al. (2016); cutoffs are practical choices.
- Performance flag: if a rider cannot hit target power in 2 straight key sessions at normal or higher effort, or max heart rate is clearly lower than usual, schedule 2 to 3 easy or rest days, then retest.
- Overreaching flag: if performance is down and fatigue is high for more than 7 days despite easy days, cut weekly load by at least 50% for a week. If it persists past 2 to 3 weeks, tell the rider to see a doctor to rule out illness, iron deficiency, or low energy availability. Never diagnose overtraining.
- Include at least 1 full rest day or very easy day per week, and a lighter week (about 30% to 50% less volume) every 3 to 4 weeks. This is convention.
- Avoid monotony: never repeat the same load every day. Make easy days truly easy.
- Ramp rate (convention): default cap of 5 CTL points per week sustained. Allow up to 8 per week for 1 to 2 weeks in experienced riders with good wellness scores. Never plan above 8 per week for more than 1 week. For beginners or riders returning from a break, cap at 3 to 5. Stop ramping when wellness flags appear. Also grow the longest ride gradually rather than jumping far past the longest ride of the last 30 days; this is borrowed from running injury data, not tested in cyclists (Frandsen et al., 2025).
- TSB (convention): during build blocks keep TSB between about -10 and -30. If TSB falls below -30, insert recovery days. For A races, aim for TSB of about +5 to +25 on race day, using the rider's own history of best races to narrow the range. Do not let TSB stay above +25 for more than about a week outside of a planned break.
- Taper: about 2 weeks for main events, cut volume 40% to 60%, keep intensity and session frequency. See the periodization_taper topic.
- Do not use ATL divided by CTL (or any acute to chronic ratio) to predict or warn about injury. Use it only as a description of how fast load is changing.
- Treat CTL, ATL, and TSB as load bookkeeping. If they disagree with the rider's wellness and performance, trust the rider.
- HRV: only use HRV if the rider measures every morning the same way. Build a baseline over at least 2 to 4 weeks. Use a 7 day rolling average of ln RMSSD, and only trust it when at least 5 of the 7 days have readings (borrowed from a study of weekly HRV variability; Grosicki et al., 2026). If it falls below the normal band (for example, more than 0.5 standard deviations below the baseline mean), swap hard sessions for easy. If within the band, hard sessions are allowed. Do not react to a single low day alone. For riders who log a menstrual cycle, read a late cycle HRV dip against the same phase of earlier cycles (see the women topic).
- Sleep: target at least 7 hours per night, and 8 or more during heavy blocks if the rider can manage it. If the rider slept 6 hours or less, avoid maximal efforts that day, especially in the afternoon or evening, or move the key session to the next morning. Before big events, suggest extra sleep in the week before. After a short night, an early afternoon nap is a reasonable option (Zheng et al., 2026).
- After strength sessions, do not suggest ice baths within a few hours if strength or muscle gain is a goal. Cold water immersion is acceptable for multi day race recovery or heat, when performance tomorrow matters more than adaptation.
- Compression and massage: optional, mainly for soreness and stage races. Do not expect them to speed fitness gains.
- Alcohol: advise none on the evening after key sessions or races, and avoid it entirely the night before hard sessions or events. If the rider drinks, recommend eating protein and carbohydrate and rehydrating first.
- Illness: no intensity with fever, chest symptoms, or body aches. Resume with easy riding after symptoms clear, and refer to a doctor for chest pain, breathlessness, or palpitations.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- Safe CTL ramp rates and TSB targets have never been tested in controlled studies. All numbers here are coaching convention.
- The biggest load and injury dataset is in runners, not cyclists, and is observational.
- Whether a deliberate overload before a taper helps (Wang et al., 2023) or backfires when it tips into overreaching (Aubry et al., 2014).
- Whether the Banister model, or its 42 and 7 day time constants, fit an individual cyclist.
- The ACWR for injury prediction. Strongly criticised; defenders argue the coupling effect is small in real data.
- HRV guided training trials are small and short; the 0.5 standard deviation band in the rules is a practical choice.
- Whether cold water immersion harms endurance adaptations.
- Sleep extension studies are small and often lack control groups. Most athlete sleep research has few women.
- The line between NFOR and OTS cannot be drawn with any test; it is known only after the fact.
- Most recovery and load research is in men and in non cycling sports.
