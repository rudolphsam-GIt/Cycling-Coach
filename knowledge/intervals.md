# Interval Training for Cyclists (VO2max, Threshold, Sweet Spot, Sprint Intervals)

## Summary

Intervals are repeated hard efforts separated by easier recovery. They are the main tool for raising the ceiling of a rider's aerobic fitness (VO2max) and the power they can hold for long efforts. There are three broad families. **VO2max intervals** are efforts of about 30 seconds to 8 minutes at or above FTP that drive breathing and heart rate close to maximum. **Threshold and sweet spot intervals** are longer efforts of about 8 to 20 minutes or more just below or at FTP. **Sprint interval training (SIT)** is short all out efforts, typically 30 seconds, with long rests.

In trained cyclists, the best supported options are 2 interval sessions per week on top of mostly easy riding, using either short intervals (such as 30 seconds hard and 15 seconds easy, repeated in sets) or longer efforts of 4 to 8 minutes. Accumulating around 30 minutes of hard work per session at a pace you can just sustain seems to work better than doing fewer, harder minutes. Fitness gains from a new interval block show up within 3 to 6 weeks and typically level off after 8 to 12 weeks, so blocks should change every few weeks. Beginners and less trained riders gain much more, and from less, than already well trained riders.

## Key findings

### Short intervals (30/15) are at least as good as, and often better than, long intervals in trained cyclists
**Evidence: Moderate**
In 16 trained cyclists over 10 weeks, two weekly sessions of effort matched short intervals improved VO2max more than long intervals (8.7% vs 2.6%), with moderate to large advantages in 30 second, 5 minute and 40 minute all out power (Rønnestad et al., 2015). In 18 elite cyclists (VO2max about 73 mL/kg/min) over 3 weeks of 3 weekly sessions, short intervals (3 sets of 13 x 30 s work with 15 s recovery, 3 min between sets) improved 20 minute power by 4.7% while 4 x 5 min intervals did not improve it (Rønnestad et al., 2020). During a single session in elite cyclists, short intervals produced 14% higher mean power and more time above 90% of VO2max than long intervals at similar RPE (Almquist et al., 2020). In a 1 week block of 5 sessions, short intervals beat 6 x 5 min for VO2max and power at 4 mmol lactate (Rønnestad et al., 2021). In male and female well trained cyclists, a 30/15 format at 118% of 40 minute max power (recovery at 60%) gave more time at or above 90% VO2max than steady efforts or 60/60 at the same average power (Urianstad et al., 2024). Most of this evidence comes from one research group with samples of 8 to 20 riders.

### Longer HIIT work bouts (4 minutes or more) are strongly supported for time trial performance
**Evidence: Moderate**
A meta-analysis of 6 trials in active and trained people found HIIT and SIT improved time trial performance similarly overall, but long HIIT intervals (4 minutes or more) gave about 2% greater time trial improvement than SIT, and HIIT gave a moderate advantage for maximal aerobic power (Rosenblat et al., 2020). In 35 recreational cyclists (VO2peak about 52) over 7 weeks with 2 interval sessions per week, 4 x 8 min at maximal sustainable effort (about 90% of peak heart rate) improved VO2peak, peak power and power at 4 mmol lactate about 11%, roughly twice as much as 4 x 4 min (about 94% heart rate) or 4 x 16 min (about 88% heart rate). The authors concluded that accumulating 32 minutes of work at about 90% of max heart rate produced bigger gains than 16 minutes at about 95%, despite lower RPE (Seiler et al., 2013). In 38 highly trained cyclists and triathletes, two weekly sessions of 8 reps at peak aerobic power, each lasting 60% of the rider's time to exhaustion at that power, improved 40 km time trial by about 4 to 6% in 4 weeks (Laursen et al., 2002).

### Time at or near VO2max is a useful target, and session design changes it
**Evidence: Limited**
Reviews propose that spending several minutes per session above 90% of VO2max is an important stimulus for central and peripheral adaptations (Buchheit & Laursen, 2013). Intermittent formats generally keep athletes near VO2max longer than continuous efforts (Midgley & McNaughton, 2006, runners). This is a mechanistic idea that fits the 30/15 findings above, but no trial has proven that time above 90% VO2max itself is what causes the larger gains. When work is matched, changing the power pattern within each 60 second interval did not change time near VO2max (Wommer et al., 2022, 10 healthy men).

### Recovery between 4 minute efforts can be about 2 minutes
**Evidence: Limited**
In 9 well trained runners doing 6 x 4 min, extending recovery from 1 to 2 minutes slightly raised running speed, but going to 4 minutes gave no further benefit. When allowed to choose, athletes took about 2 minutes (118 s on average) (Seiler & Hetlelid, 2005). This is runners, small sample, single session.

### Two interval sessions per week is the standard dose; more is not clearly better
**Evidence: Moderate**
Most positive trials in trained cyclists used 2 interval sessions per week with the rest of training easy (Laursen et al., 2002; Rønnestad et al., 2015; Seiler et al., 2013; Sylta et al., 2016). A meta-analysis of 29 interval studies found a dose response for HIIT with more sessions, weeks and total work, but the authors also concluded that going beyond minimal requirements may not add much, and SIT showed no dose response (Rosenblat et al., 2021). Trained riders improved time trial performance by about 2%, while less trained people improved up to 6% (Rosenblat et al., 2021).

### Block periodization of intervals works at least as well as spreading them out
**Evidence: Moderate**
In trained cyclists, doing 5 interval sessions in 1 week followed by 3 weeks of 1 session per week improved VO2max (4.6%) and peak power more than 2 sessions per week for 4 weeks at the same total load (Rønnestad, Hansen et al., 2014). Over 12 weeks, repeating that pattern every 4 weeks also gave larger VO2max gains (8.8% vs 3.7%) than steady 2 per week (Rønnestad, Ellefsen et al., 2014). A meta-analysis found small benefits of block over traditional organization for VO2max (SMD 0.40) and peak power (SMD 0.28) in trained athletes, from a small number of studies (Mølmen et al., 2019). Most of these studies are from one group.

### The order of interval types across a block matters little
**Evidence: Moderate**
In 63 cyclists over 12 weeks with 24 interval sessions, going from 4 x 16 to 4 x 8 to 4 x 4 min, the reverse order, or a mixed order all produced similar 5 to 10% gains in 40 minute power, peak power and VO2peak, with similar rates of high, moderate and non responders (Sylta et al., 2016). This was the largest trial in this area and suggests the total amount of quality work matters more than its exact sequence.

### Sprint interval training improves aerobic fitness, mostly in less trained people
**Evidence: Moderate**
A meta-analysis of 16 RCTs (318 young, mostly untrained or recreationally active people) found repeated 30 second all out efforts raised VO2max by about 8% versus no exercise, with similar gains to moderate continuous training at a much lower time cost (Gist et al., 2014). Low volume HIT with 30 to 60 second near maximal efforts gave moderate VO2max gains in sedentary and active non athletes (6 to 10% in men), but the effect in athletic men was unclear (2.7% with wide uncertainty) (Weston et al., 2014). In highly trained cyclists, 12 x 30 s at 175% of peak aerobic power with 4.5 min recovery, twice weekly, improved 40 km time trial as much as longer HIIT, but did not improve VO2peak significantly (Laursen et al., 2002). In trained people, long HIIT appears better than SIT for time trial performance (Rosenblat et al., 2020).

### Intervals raise VO2max more than steady riding, especially in less fit people
**Evidence: Moderate**
A meta-analysis of 28 controlled trials in healthy adults (average starting VO2max about 41) found both endurance training and HIT gave large VO2max gains, with HIT about 1.2 mL/kg/min better than endurance training. Compared with endurance training, the HIT advantage was larger with longer HIT repetitions and longer programs (Milanović et al., 2015). In 40 moderately trained men over 8 weeks with total work matched, 4 x 4 min at 90 to 95% max heart rate (7.2%) and 15/15 intervals (5.5%) raised VO2max more than steady running at 70% or 85% heart rate, mainly through bigger stroke volume (Helgerud et al., 2007, runners). A pooled analysis of interval programs lasting 6 to 13 weeks in sedentary or recreationally active people under 45 found an average VO2max gain of 0.51 L/min (Bacon et al., 2013).

### How fast adaptations appear
**Evidence: Moderate**
Measurable gains appear within 3 to 4 weeks in trained cyclists when interval load is focused (Laursen et al., 2002; Rønnestad, Hansen et al., 2014; Rønnestad et al., 2020). Typical trials of 7 to 12 weeks show gains of about 5 to 10% in VO2max or sustained power in trained riders (Seiler et al., 2013; Sylta et al., 2016). Gains are larger in longer programs and in less fit people (Milanović et al., 2015; Rosenblat et al., 2021).

### Threshold and sweet spot intervals
**Evidence: Limited**
There are few controlled trials of threshold or sweet spot intervals as a primary method in cyclists. The 4 x 16 min group in Seiler et al. (2013), at about 88% of peak heart rate (close to threshold effort), improved less than 4 x 8 min. Threshold heavy training improved less than polarized training in trained cyclists (Neal et al., 2013; Stöggl & Sperlich, 2014, mixed sports). Running at about 85% max heart rate improved VO2max less than 4 x 4 min (Helgerud et al., 2007). In highly trained runners, threshold work delayed short term autonomic recovery about as much as high intensity intervals, both returning to baseline within about 30 minutes (Seiler et al., 2007). Threshold work still has a place for building sustained power and race specific tolerance, but the evidence does not show it beats VO2max intervals for raising fitness.

## Rules for the coach

Intensity conversions to % FTP below are practical approximations. The studies prescribed by heart rate, % of peak aerobic power, or maximal session effort, not by FTP. Treat them as starting points and let RPE and the ability to complete the session adjust them.

**Session types (warm up 10 to 20 min easy plus a few short openers before every interval session)**

- **Short VO2max intervals (30/15).** 30 s at about 115 to 120% FTP, 15 s at about 50 to 60% FTP. Sets of 10 to 13 reps, 3 sets, 3 min easy between sets. RPE 8 to 9 by the end of each set (Rønnestad et al., 2015, 2020; Urianstad et al., 2024).
- **Long VO2max intervals.** 4 to 6 x 4 to 5 min at about 106 to 120% FTP, recovery 2 to 2.5 min easy. RPE 8 to 9 (Rønnestad et al., 2020; Seiler & Hetlelid, 2005).
- **Aerobic intervals (Seiler 4 x 8).** 4 x 8 min at the highest power you can hold evenly for all 4 reps, roughly 100 to 108% FTP, recovery 2 min easy. RPE 7 to 8, not all out (Seiler et al., 2013).
- **Threshold intervals.** 2 to 4 x 10 to 20 min at 95 to 100% FTP, recovery 3 to 5 min. RPE 7.
- **Sweet spot intervals.** 2 to 3 x 15 to 30 min at 88 to 94% FTP, recovery 5 min. RPE 6 to 7. Use as a time efficient load builder, not as a VO2max tool.
- **Sprint intervals (SIT).** 4 to 8 x 30 s all out (RPE 10), with 4 to 4.5 min easy recovery (Gist et al., 2014; Laursen et al., 2002). Best for riders with very little time, or as an occasional top end stimulus. Do not use SIT as the main interval type for trained riders aiming at time trial or long events (Rosenblat et al., 2020).

**Dose and frequency**

- Target total hard work per VO2max session of about 20 to 40 min (for example 4 x 8 min, 3 x 13 x 30 s, or 5 to 6 x 5 min) (Seiler et al., 2013).
- Default is 2 interval sessions per week. Use 1 per week for riders under 3 h per week or returning from a break, and never more than 3 per week except in a planned block (Rosenblat et al., 2021; Sylta et al., 2016).
- Leave at least 48 h between hard sessions, with easy riding or rest between. This is a practical rule; the cited recovery data (Seiler et al., 2007) only measured the first few hours.
- Optional block for experienced riders with at least 6 h per week. 1 week with 4 to 5 interval sessions, then 3 weeks of 1 interval session per week plus easy volume (Rønnestad, Hansen et al., 2014). Not for beginners. Only use when sleep, life stress and health are good.

**Adjustments by hours available**

- Under 4 h per week. 2 sessions of 45 to 60 min (1 VO2max session, 1 threshold or sweet spot session), plus 1 easy ride if possible. SIT is acceptable as one of the two.
- 4 to 8 h per week. 2 interval sessions (at least 1 VO2max type) plus easy rides including 1 longer ride.
- Over 8 h per week. 2 interval sessions, extra time added as easy riding. Consider a 1 week block every 4 to 6 weeks for experienced riders.

**Progression**

- Run each interval block for 3 to 6 weeks, then change the stimulus (different work duration) or take a lighter week. Gains often level off after about 8 to 12 weeks of the same format.
- Within a block, progress in this order. First add reps or time (for example 4 x 4 min to 5 x 4 min, or 2 to 3 sets of 30/15), then raise power 2 to 3% once all reps can be completed at target RPE.
- If the rider fails to complete the last rep at target power twice in a row, hold or reduce power 3 to 5% rather than adding work.
- Do not obsess over block order. Long to short, short to long, or mixed all work similarly (Sylta et al., 2016).
- Retest FTP or a 20 to 40 min effort every 4 to 8 weeks and update targets.

**Experience level**

- Riders new to structure (under 1 year) gain from almost any interval type. Start with 1 session per week at RPE 7 to 8 for 2 to 3 weeks, then move to 2.
- Trained riders should expect smaller gains, about 2 to 5% over a block (Rosenblat et al., 2021), and benefit most from short intervals or 4 to 8 min efforts held at a sustainable maximum.

**Red flags to reduce intervals**

- Two consecutive sessions where target power cannot be reached at expected RPE, unusual fatigue, poor sleep, illness or a rising resting heart rate. Replace the next interval session with easy riding.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed

- Most short interval (30/15) and block periodization evidence comes from one Norwegian research group with samples of 8 to 20 riders.
- No cycling trial was found that prescribes intervals directly by % FTP; FTP conversions in this file are approximations.
- Whether time above 90% VO2max is the cause of better adaptations is an assumption, not a tested fact (Buchheit & Laursen, 2013).
- Very few controlled trials test threshold or sweet spot intervals as the main training method in cyclists. No peer reviewed trial of "sweet spot" training by name was found.
- Data on women and masters riders over 50 is very limited. One study found the 30/15 advantage in time near VO2max held for both sexes (Urianstad et al., 2024), but long term training studies in women are scarce.
- The 48 h spacing rule between hard sessions is common practice, not tested directly in these trials.
- SIT meta-analyses are mostly in young, untrained or recreationally active people; benefits in trained cyclists are less clear (Weston et al., 2014).
- Most trials last 3 to 12 weeks, so long term progression rules are based on reasoning rather than direct evidence.
