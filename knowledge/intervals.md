# Interval Training for Cyclists (VO2max, Threshold, Sweet Spot, Sprint Intervals)

## Summary

Intervals are repeated hard efforts separated by easier recovery. They are the main tool for raising the ceiling of a rider's aerobic fitness (VO2max) and the power they can hold for long efforts. There are three broad families. **VO2max intervals** are efforts of about 30 seconds to 8 minutes at or above FTP that drive breathing and heart rate close to maximum. **Threshold and sweet spot intervals** are longer efforts of about 8 to 20 minutes or more just below or at FTP. **Sprint interval training (SIT)** is short all out efforts, typically 30 seconds, with long rests.

That intervals raise VO2max more than steady training is well supported by pooled data from thousands of mostly non athlete adults. In trained cyclists, the best supported options are 2 interval sessions per week on top of mostly easy riding, using either short intervals (such as 30 seconds hard and 15 seconds easy, repeated in sets) or longer efforts of 3 to 8 minutes. Longer work bouts of about 4 minutes or more beat all out sprints for time trial performance. Gains from a new block show up within 3 to 6 weeks and level off after 8 to 12 weeks, so blocks should change every few weeks. Beginners and less trained riders gain much more, and from less, than already well trained riders.

## Size of the evidence

The general evidence for interval training is large. A 2026 dose response meta-analysis pooled 69 randomised trials with 2,387 participants, a 2026 sprint interval meta-analysis pooled 78 studies with 1,188 participants, and a 2025 network meta-analysis pooled 51 studies with 1,261 athletes. Most participants are not cyclists and are untrained or recreationally active, where gains are largest.

Evidence on how to design intervals for trained cyclists is much smaller. The 30/15 and block periodization findings come mainly from one Norwegian group, with under about 100 riders in total. The largest cycling trial on interval order had 63 cyclists. The best synthesis of programming for time trial performance pooled 29 studies with 491 people, 258 of them trained. Pooled data on time near VO2max cover about 240 athletes and are inconsistent. No peer reviewed analysis of large training platform datasets (such as Strava, TrainingPeaks, Garmin, Zwift, intervals.icu or Golden Cheetah) linking interval types to cyclists' performance was found. Women and masters riders are under represented throughout.

## Key findings

### Short intervals (30/15) look at least as good as long intervals in trained cyclists
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 5 studies from one research group, 8 to 20 riders each, under about 100 trained and elite cyclists in total (no meta-analysis)**
In 16 trained cyclists over 10 weeks, effort matched short intervals improved VO2max more than long intervals (8.7% vs 2.6%), with better 30 s, 5 min and 40 min power (Rønnestad et al., 2015). In 18 elite cyclists over 3 weeks, 3 x 13 x 30/15 improved 20 minute power by 4.7% while 4 x 5 min did not (Rønnestad et al., 2020). In single sessions, short intervals gave higher mean power and more time above 90% VO2max than long intervals (Almquist et al., 2020), including in women (Urianstad et al., 2024), and a 1 week block of short intervals beat 6 x 5 min (Rønnestad et al., 2021). Larger pooled data do not single out short intervals: in 1,261 athletes the HIIT benefit peaked at work bouts of about 140 s (Yang et al., 2025), and across 86 studies bouts of 2 minutes or more gave more time near VO2max than bouts of 30 s or less (Schoenmakers et al., 2026). Neither isolated the 30/15 format with short recoveries. **Grade change: Moderate to Limited**, because the advantage rests on under 100 riders from one group and the wider pooled data do not support it. **Rule change:** 30/15 and 3 to 8 minute efforts are now equal first choices.

### Longer HIIT work bouts (4 minutes or more) beat sprint intervals for time trial performance
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 12 studies, about 460 participants (moderately trained to trained, mixed sports)**
A meta-analysis of 6 trials in active and trained people found long HIIT (4 minutes or more) gave about 2% greater time trial improvement than SIT (Rosenblat et al., 2020). A 2026 meta-analysis of 12 trials (460 participants) found HIIT beat SIT for VO2max and maximal aerobic power, most clearly with work bouts over 4 minutes and programs over 8 weeks, though most outcomes were low or very low certainty (Xiao et al., 2026). In 35 recreational cyclists over 7 weeks, 4 x 8 min at maximal sustainable effort improved VO2peak and power about 11%, roughly twice as much as 4 x 4 min or 4 x 16 min. Accumulating 32 minutes at about 90% max heart rate beat 16 minutes at about 95% (Seiler et al., 2013). In 38 highly trained cyclists and triathletes, 8 reps at peak aerobic power twice weekly improved 40 km time trial by about 4 to 6% in 4 weeks (Laursen et al., 2002).

### Time at or near VO2max is a useful target, and session design changes it
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 19 studies, about 240 athletes (healthy trained athletes, mixed sports, average VO2max about 60)**
Reviews propose that several minutes per session above 90% of VO2max is an important stimulus (Buchheit & Laursen, 2013). Intermittent formats generally keep athletes near VO2max longer than continuous efforts (Midgley & McNaughton, 2006, runners). A 2026 meta-analysis of 86 studies (239 protocols; N not reported in abstract) found longer work bouts (2 minutes or more), more total interval time and variable intensity bouts increased time near VO2max; recovery type made no difference (Schoenmakers et al., 2026). A 2026 network meta-analysis of 19 studies (240 athletes) found no interval structure clearly better than even paced long intervals, with large inconsistency between studies (Held et al., 2026), and changing the power pattern within matched 60 s intervals did not change it (Wommer et al., 2022, 10 healthy men). No trial has proven that time above 90% VO2max itself causes the larger gains.

### Recovery between 4 minute efforts can be about 2 minutes
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 1 study, 9 well trained runners (direct test); indirect support from 9 studies, about 176 elite athletes (mixed sports)**
In 9 well trained runners doing 6 x 4 min, 2 minutes of recovery beat 1 minute and 4 minutes added nothing; given a choice, they took about 2 minutes (Seiler & Hetlelid, 2005). In a meta-analysis of 176 elite athletes, HIIT raised VO2max more than conventional training, and the benefit held when recoveries were 2 minutes or more at low intensity (Ma et al., 2023). A meta-regression in 1,261 athletes found the best VO2max response at a work to recovery ratio of about 0.85, for example 140 s work and 165 s recovery (Yang et al., 2025, mostly running based HIIT). Neither tested recovery length directly.

### Two interval sessions per week is the standard dose; more is not clearly better
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 29 studies, about 491 participants (258 trained, 146 active, 75 inactive; mixed endurance)**
Most positive trials in trained cyclists used 2 interval sessions per week (Laursen et al., 2002; Seiler et al., 2013; Sylta et al., 2016). A meta-analysis of 29 interval studies found a dose response for HIIT with more sessions, weeks and total work, but the authors also concluded that going beyond minimal requirements may not add much, and SIT showed no dose response (Rosenblat et al., 2021). Trained riders improved time trial performance by about 2%, while less trained people improved up to 6% (Rosenblat et al., 2021). A 2026 meta-analysis of 12 trials suggested 2 to 3 sessions per week of 3 to 6 x 4 to 5 min (Xiao et al., 2026).

### Block periodization of intervals works at least as well as spreading them out
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 6 studies pooled, N not reported in abstract (trained to well trained athletes; mean PEDro 3.7 out of 10)**
In trained cyclists, 5 interval sessions in 1 week then 3 weeks of 1 per week improved VO2max (4.6%) more than 2 per week at the same load (Rønnestad, Hansen et al., 2014). Over 12 weeks the pattern also won (8.8% vs 3.7%) (Rønnestad, Ellefsen et al., 2014). A meta-analysis found small benefits of block over traditional organization for VO2max (SMD 0.40) and peak power (SMD 0.28), from small studies of generally low quality (Mølmen et al., 2019). A systematic review of 7 studies in trained road cyclists found no evidence favoring any specific periodization model over 8 to 12 weeks (Galán-Rioja et al., 2023). **Grade change: Moderate to Limited.** The pooled studies are few, small, low quality and mostly from one group, and a cycling review found no clear advantage.

### The order of interval types across a block matters little
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 1 study, 63 cyclists**
In 63 cyclists over 12 weeks, going from 4 x 16 to 4 x 8 to 4 x 4 min, the reverse, or mixed order all gave similar 5 to 10% gains in 40 minute power, peak power and VO2peak (Sylta et al., 2016). Total quality work seems to matter more than sequence. **Grade change: Moderate to Limited**, because the finding rests on a single trial.

### Sprint interval training improves aerobic fitness, mostly in less trained people
**Evidence: Strong** (for untrained and recreationally active people; Moderate at best for trained cyclists)
**Pool: 78 studies, about 1,188 participants (mostly untrained or recreationally active adults, all out cycling sprints)**
A 2026 meta-analysis of 93 trials across 78 studies (1,188 people) found repeated all out cycling sprints raised VO2max by about 8.3%. The number of sprints per session made almost no difference, while longer programs helped (about 0.7% per extra week) (Hutchinson et al., 2026). Earlier meta-analyses agree: 16 RCTs (318 mostly untrained young people) found about an 8% gain, similar to moderate continuous training at much lower time cost (Gist et al., 2014), and 13 studies found gains of 4.2 to 13.4% (Sloth et al., 2013). Low volume HIT gave 6 to 10% VO2max gains in non athletic men but an unclear effect in athletic men (Weston et al., 2014). In athletes, a network meta-analysis of 1,261 athletes found SIT raised VO2max versus conventional training, though less than HIIT on point estimates (Yang et al., 2025). In highly trained cyclists, 12 x 30 s at 175% of peak aerobic power improved 40 km time trial as much as longer HIIT but not VO2peak (Laursen et al., 2002). In trained people, long HIIT appears better than SIT for time trial performance (Rosenblat et al., 2020; Xiao et al., 2026). **Grade change: Moderate to Strong** for less trained people, based on more than 1,000 participants across many independent trials with consistent results.

### Intervals raise VO2max more than steady riding, especially in less fit people
**Evidence: Strong** (for healthy adults in general; less certain in trained cyclists)
**Pool: 69 studies, about 2,387 participants (adults in randomised trials, mixed populations, mostly non cyclists)**
A 2026 dose response meta-analysis of 69 randomised trials (2,387 participants) found HIIT improved VO2max more than moderate continuous training (d 0.38). About 80% of the maximal HIIT effect came from roughly 11 minutes per session, versus about 52 minutes for continuous training (Mattioni Maturana et al., 2026). A meta-analysis of 28 controlled trials in 723 healthy adults (starting VO2max about 41) found HIT about 1.2 mL/kg/min better than endurance training, more so with longer repetitions and programs (Milanović et al., 2015). In athletes, a network meta-analysis of 51 studies (1,261 athletes) found HIIT raised VO2max more than continuous or conventional training (Yang et al., 2025), as did meta-analyses of 176 elite athletes (Ma et al., 2023) and of 18 studies in trained athletes (Qi et al., 2026). In 40 moderately trained men with work matched, 4 x 4 min (7.2%) and 15/15 intervals (5.5%) raised VO2max more than steady running (Helgerud et al., 2007, runners). A pooled analysis of 37 studies (334 untrained or recreational people) found an average gain of 0.51 L/min (Bacon et al., 2013). **Grade change: Moderate to Strong**, based on several large, independent pooled analyses of randomised trials with consistent results.

### How fast adaptations appear
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 29 studies, about 491 participants (mixed endurance; 258 trained)**
Measurable gains appear within 3 to 4 weeks in trained cyclists when interval load is focused (Laursen et al., 2002; Rønnestad, Hansen et al., 2014; Rønnestad et al., 2020). Typical trials of 7 to 12 weeks show gains of about 5 to 10% in VO2max or sustained power in trained riders (Seiler et al., 2013; Sylta et al., 2016). Gains are larger in longer programs and less fit people (Milanović et al., 2015; Rosenblat et al., 2021; Hutchinson et al., 2026).

### Threshold and sweet spot intervals
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 0 studies of sweet spot by name; a few small trials with threshold type arms, for example 1 study, 35 recreational cyclists (Seiler et al., 2013)**
The 4 x 16 min group in Seiler et al. (2013), at about 88% of peak heart rate (close to threshold effort), improved less than 4 x 8 min. Threshold heavy training improved less than polarized training in trained cyclists (Neal et al., 2013; Stöggl & Sperlich, 2014, mixed sports). Running at about 85% max heart rate improved VO2max less than 4 x 4 min (Helgerud et al., 2007). But a 2026 network meta-analysis of intensity distribution models found no clear winner and ranked threshold focused training most likely best for VO2max, without a definite difference (Li et al., 2026). Threshold work delayed short term autonomic recovery about as much as high intensity intervals in highly trained runners (Seiler et al., 2007). Threshold work still has a place for sustained power and race specific tolerance; the evidence neither shows it beats VO2max intervals for fitness nor clearly loses to them.

## Rules for the coach

Intensity conversions to % FTP below are practical approximations. The studies prescribed by heart rate, % of peak aerobic power, or maximal session effort, not by FTP. Treat them as starting points and let RPE and the ability to complete the session adjust them.

**Session types (warm up 10 to 20 min easy plus a few short openers before every interval session)**

- **Short VO2max intervals (30/15).** 30 s at about 115 to 120% FTP, 15 s at about 50 to 60% FTP. Sets of 10 to 13 reps, 3 sets, 3 min easy between sets. RPE 8 to 9 by the end of each set (Rønnestad et al., 2015, 2020; Urianstad et al., 2024).
- **Long VO2max intervals.** 4 to 6 x 3 to 5 min at about 106 to 120% FTP, recovery 2 to 2.5 min easy. RPE 8 to 9 (Rønnestad et al., 2020; Seiler & Hetlelid, 2005; Xiao et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025).
- **Aerobic intervals (Seiler 4 x 8).** 4 x 8 min at the highest power you can hold evenly for all 4 reps, roughly 100 to 108% FTP, recovery 2 min easy. RPE 7 to 8, not all out (Seiler et al., 2013).
- **Threshold intervals.** 2 to 4 x 10 to 20 min at 95 to 100% FTP, recovery 3 to 5 min. RPE 7.
- **Sweet spot intervals.** 2 to 3 x 15 to 30 min at 88 to 94% FTP, recovery 5 min. RPE 6 to 7. Use as a time efficient load builder, not as a VO2max tool.
- **Sprint intervals (SIT).** 2 to 6 x 30 s all out (RPE 10), with 4 to 4.5 min easy recovery (Gist et al., 2014; Laursen et al., 2002). For untrained riders, fewer sprints (2 to 4) gave about the same VO2max gain as more (Hutchinson et al., 2026), so do not add reps for their own sake. Best for riders with very little time, or as an occasional top end stimulus. Do not use SIT as the main interval type for trained riders aiming at time trial or long events (Rosenblat et al., 2020; Xiao et al., 2026).

**Choosing between short and long VO2max intervals**

- Treat 30/15 sets and 3 to 8 min efforts as equal first choices. Pick by rider preference, terrain and which one the rider can complete at target effort. Rotate between them across blocks rather than relying on one (Yang et al., 2025; Schoenmakers et al., 2026; Sylta et al., 2016).

**Dose and frequency**

- For trained riders, target total hard work per VO2max session of about 20 to 40 min (for example 4 x 8 min, 3 x 13 x 30 s, or 5 to 6 x 5 min) (Seiler et al., 2013).
- For novice or very time limited riders, 10 to 20 min of hard work per session is enough to get most of the VO2max benefit (Mattioni Maturana et al., 2026, mixed adult populations, not trained cyclists).
- Default is 2 interval sessions per week. Use 1 per week for riders under 3 h per week or returning from a break, and never more than 3 per week except in a planned block (Rosenblat et al., 2021; Sylta et al., 2016).
- Leave at least 48 h between hard sessions. This is a practical rule; the recovery data (Seiler et al., 2007) only covered the first few hours.
- Optional block for experienced riders with at least 6 h per week. 1 week with 4 to 5 interval sessions, then 3 weeks of 1 interval session per week plus easy volume (Rønnestad, Hansen et al., 2014). It is a variation, not a proven upgrade (Galán-Rioja et al., 2023). Not for beginners, and only when sleep, stress and health are good.

**Adjustments by hours available**

- Under 4 h per week. 2 sessions of 45 to 60 min (1 VO2max session, 1 threshold or sweet spot session), plus 1 easy ride if possible. SIT is acceptable as one of the two.
- 4 to 8 h per week. 2 interval sessions (at least 1 VO2max type) plus easy rides including 1 longer ride.
- Over 8 h per week. 2 interval sessions, extra time added as easy riding. Consider a 1 week block every 4 to 6 weeks for experienced riders.

**Progression**

- Run each interval block for 3 to 6 weeks, then change the stimulus (different work duration) or take a lighter week. Gains often level off after about 8 to 12 weeks of the same format.
- Within a block, progress in this order. First add reps or time (for example 4 x 4 min to 5 x 4 min, or 2 to 3 sets of 30/15), then raise power 2 to 3% once all reps can be completed at target RPE.
- If the rider fails to complete the last rep at target power twice in a row, hold or reduce power 3 to 5% rather than adding work.
- Do not obsess over block order. Long to short, short to long, or mixed all work similarly (Sylta et al., 2016).
- Retest FTP or a 20 to 40 min effort every 4 to 8 weeks and update targets.

**Experience level**

- Riders new to structure (under 1 year) gain from almost any interval type. Start with 1 session per week at RPE 7 to 8 for 2 to 3 weeks, then move to 2.
- Trained riders should expect smaller gains, about 2 to 5% over a block (Rosenblat et al., 2021), and benefit most from short intervals or 3 to 8 min efforts held at a sustainable maximum.

**Red flags to reduce intervals**

- Two consecutive sessions where target power cannot be reached at expected RPE, unusual fatigue, poor sleep, illness or a rising resting heart rate. Replace the next interval session with easy riding.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed

- Most short interval (30/15) and block periodization evidence comes from one Norwegian research group with samples of 8 to 20 riders. Larger pooled data do not single out 30/15 as best (Yang et al., 2025; Schoenmakers et al., 2026).
- The largest interval meta-analyses (1,000 to 2,400 participants) are mostly in untrained or recreationally active people and mostly not cyclists. Effects in trained cyclists are smaller and less certain.
- No cycling trial was found that prescribes intervals directly by % FTP; FTP conversions in this file are approximations.
- Whether time above 90% VO2max is the cause of better adaptations is an assumption, not a tested fact (Buchheit & Laursen, 2013), and pooled data on which formats maximise it are inconsistent (Held et al., 2026; Schoenmakers et al., 2026).
- Very few controlled trials test threshold or sweet spot intervals as the main training method in cyclists. No peer reviewed trial of "sweet spot" training by name was found.
- No peer reviewed analysis of large training platform datasets linking interval types to cyclists' performance was found.
- Data on women and masters riders over 50 is very limited (one acute 30/15 study included women; Urianstad et al., 2024).
- The 48 h spacing rule between hard sessions is common practice, not tested directly in these trials.
- Most trials last 3 to 12 weeks, so long term progression rules are based on reasoning rather than direct evidence.
