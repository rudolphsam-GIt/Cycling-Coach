# Heat, Cold, Altitude and Air Quality for Cyclists

## Summary
Heat is the environmental stress with the best evidence base for cyclists. Repeated training in the heat for about 1 to 2 weeks, with sessions of at least 60 minutes that raise core temperature and cause sweating, reduces heat strain and improves performance in hot conditions. Whether heat training also improves performance in cool conditions is mixed: some studies in trained cyclists show gains, others show none. Longer heat training blocks (around 5 weeks) can raise hemoglobin mass in elite cyclists. When racing in heat, arriving acclimatized and hydrated and using cooling before and during the event help limit the drop in performance.

Altitude lowers aerobic power right away, roughly in a straight line from low altitudes upward. Living at moderate altitude (about 2,000 to 2,500 m) while training lower ("live high, train low") can raise red cell mass and sea level performance, but individual responses vary widely and the dose has to be large: weeks of exposure and many hours per day. A well controlled study of simulated altitude in cyclists found no benefit, so the evidence is not uniform. Cold weather is mainly a safety and clothing issue. Air pollution and wildfire smoke are health issues, and the coach should follow the official Air Quality Index (AQI) rather than invent thresholds.

## Key findings

### Heat acclimation reduces strain and improves performance in the heat
**Evidence: Strong**
Exercise heat acclimation improves sweating and skin blood flow, lowers body temperature and heart rate, expands plasma volume, improves fluid balance, and lowers the risk of serious heat illness; the size of the adaptation depends on the intensity, duration, frequency and number of heat exposures, and on whether the heat is dry or humid (Périard et al., 2015). Controlled hyperthermia protocols that hold a target core temperature appear to give faster and more complete adaptation than fixed workload protocols, and acclimatizing outdoors in conditions like the competition gives the most specific adaptations (Périard et al., 2015). The 2015 consensus states that heat acclimatization is the single most important step to reduce strain and optimize performance in the heat, recommends repeated exercise heat exposures over 1 to 2 weeks (at least 1 week, ideally 2), sessions of at least 60 minutes per day (75 to 90 minutes in its training camp example), and notes that holding core temperature at about 38.5 °C or more can optimize adaptation (Racinais et al., 2015). The adaptations are largely kept for about 2 to 4 weeks without exposure, and re-acclimatization is faster than the first time (Racinais et al., 2015). A large review in Physiological Reviews covers the same countermeasures (heat acclimation, cooling, hydration) in more depth (Périard et al., 2021), and the 2023 IOC consensus repeats heat acclimation, hydration management, and adapting warm up and clothing as athlete level protections (Racinais et al., 2023).

### Heat training as a tool for performance in temperate conditions
**Evidence: Limited to Moderate (mixed results, small samples)**
In 12 trained cyclists, 10 days of heat acclimation (about 50% VO2max in 40 °C) raised VO2max by 5% and time trial work by 6% in cool conditions (13 °C), with a 6.5% rise in plasma volume; a control group training in the cool did not change (Lorenzo et al., 2010). Later studies did not replicate the cool condition gain. In 18 trained cyclists, 2 weeks of outdoor training at about 35 °C improved time trial performance in the heat by 16% but did not change VO2max, peak power or a 43 km outdoor time trial in cool conditions (Karlsen et al., 2015). In a crossover study of 7 trained men, 10 days of 90 minute sessions at 38 °C improved VO2max and time trial performance in the heat but not at 18 °C (Keiser et al., 2015). The consensus describes temperate benefits as possible but more circumstantial in highly trained athletes (Racinais et al., 2015).

### Longer heat training can raise hemoglobin mass
**Evidence: Moderate (randomized but few studies)**
In elite female (n = 20) and male (n = 27) cyclists, 5 weeks of heat training (five 50 minute sessions per week added to normal training) increased hemoglobin mass by about 4% in women and 3.5% in men and improved lactate threshold, VO2max and 15 minute all out power in thermoneutral conditions compared with controls (Lundby et al., 2023). After a 3 week altitude camp in 18 elite male cyclists, three weekly heat sessions over 3.5 weeks maintained the altitude gain in hemoglobin mass, while the control group lost about 3.3% (Rønnestad et al., 2025). Both studies are from the same research group and used elite riders.

### Racing in the heat: hydration and cooling
**Evidence: Strong (hydration basics), Moderate (cooling)**
Athletes should start in a euhydrated state, for example by drinking about 6 mL per kg body mass every 2 to 3 hours before exercise, minimize dehydration during exercise without gaining weight, use drinks with about 0.5 to 0.7 g/L sodium for sessions longer than 1 hour, and take 30 to 60 g/h carbohydrate (up to 90 g/h beyond 2.5 hours) (Racinais et al., 2015). Body mass losses of about 2% or more can impair performance in the heat (Racinais et al., 2015). Precooling (for example drinking about 1 L of ice slurry, or using ice vests and fans) may benefit sustained events like cycling (Racinais et al., 2015). A 2024 meta-analysis of 59 studies (563 male athletes, average conditions 34 °C) found cooling before or during exercise reduced the performance loss in the heat, with an effect size of about 0.30 for self paced exercise like time trials and larger effects for fixed workload tests; no women were included (van de Kerkhof et al., 2024). Caffeine at 3 to 6 mg/kg remains effective for endurance work in the heat (Guest et al., 2021). Wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) is an environmental index, not a measure of an individual's heat strain, and may underestimate risk when sweat cannot evaporate (Racinais et al., 2015).

### Cold weather riding
**Evidence: Moderate (position stand, few performance studies)**
The ACSM position stand states that exercise can be done safely in most cold environments with proper risk management: know the signs of hypothermia, frostbite and non freezing cold injury, check current and forecast weather, choose clothing to the individual rather than a fixed outfit, use the wind chill index to judge frostbite risk with extra watchfulness below a wind chill of -27 °C (-18 °F), and monitor people with asthma or heart disease closely (Castellani et al., 2006). Aerobic performance appears to be reduced in the cold, and strength and power fall as muscle temperature drops, but this has not been well studied; exhaustive exercise can also blunt shivering and vasoconstriction ("thermoregulatory fatigue") (Castellani & Tipton, 2015). Cycling adds self generated wind, so wind chill on the bike is worse than the forecast at rest; this is a practical inference, not a measured guideline.

### Acute altitude lowers aerobic power and pacing must change
**Evidence: Strong (consistent lab data, small samples)**
In 8 endurance trained athletes in a hypobaric chamber, VO2max fell linearly by about 6.3% per 1,000 m from 300 to 2,800 m (range 4.6 to 7.5%), and the decline was already measurable at 800 m (Wehrlin & Hallén, 2006). In 10 well trained cyclists and triathletes, mean power in a 5 minute time trial fell by about 5.8%, 10.3% and 19.8% at simulated 1,200, 2,200 and 3,200 m compared with 200 m; efficiency did not change, and riders whose first trial was at higher altitude tended to mis-pace (Clark et al., 2007). Moderate altitude (about 2,000 to 3,000 m) lowers VO2max by roughly 15 to 20% in endurance athletes (Saunders et al., 2009). For events where aerodynamic drag dominates, thinner air partly offsets the loss: a hour record model predicted the best elevation is about 2,500 m for acclimatized riders and 2,000 m for unacclimatized riders (Bassett et al., 1999). For climbing, where drag matters less, the aerobic loss dominates (inference from the same physics).

### Live high, train low can improve sea level performance, with wide individual variation
**Evidence: Moderate**
In 39 competitive runners, 4 weeks of living at 2,500 m and training at 1,250 m increased VO2max by 5% and red cell mass by 9% and improved 5,000 m time (by about 13 s), while sea level controls did not improve (Levine & Stray-Gundersen, 1997). Responses vary: responders had a larger early rise in erythropoietin and could keep their interval training speeds, while non-responders did not increase red cell volume (Chapman et al., 1998). A meta-analysis of 51 studies found natural live high train low to be the best protocol for both elite and sub elite athletes, with possible gains of about 4% in power in elite athletes, but also substantial placebo, nocebo and training camp effects (Bonetti & Hopkins, 2009). A meta-analysis of raw data from 17 studies found hemoglobin mass rose about 1.1% per 100 hours of altitude exposure and stayed about 3.3% higher for up to 20 days after, and that camps as short as 2 weeks will quite likely increase hemoglobin mass (Gore et al., 2013). Altitudes above about 2,000 m for more than 3 weeks, plus adequate iron stores, are needed for the red cell response; training at altitude should include short (1 to 2 minute) high intensity efforts with long recoveries to protect race specific fitness (Saunders et al., 2009).

### Hypoxic dose: hours per day and weeks matter
**Evidence: Moderate (reviews), Limited (exact thresholds)**
A review concluded that the best living altitude for red cell gains is about 2,200 to 2,500 m, the best duration is about 4 weeks, the minimum daily dose to stimulate red cell production seems to be about 12 hours per day, and more benefit appears beyond 16 hours per day; natural altitude for 20 to 22 hours per day appears sufficient (Millet et al., 2010). Non hematological changes (economy, buffering) may need less (Millet et al., 2010). Wilber (2007) reviews how elite athletes apply natural altitude, nitrogen tents and other devices. Importantly, a double blind, placebo controlled study in 16 endurance cyclists found that 4 weeks of 16 hours per day in normobaric hypoxia (equivalent to 3,000 m) did not change hemoglobin mass, VO2max or 26 km time trial power compared with placebo rooms (Siebenmann et al., 2012). This raises doubt about home tent protocols at typical doses.

### Air pollution and wildfire smoke
**Evidence: Moderate (health effects), Limited (exercise specific thresholds)**
Exercising raises breathing volume and therefore the dose of inhaled pollutants. Ozone is particularly harmful to exercising athletes and peaks on hot, bright afternoons, often in rural areas; carbon monoxide impairs performance; and exercisers should avoid riding right beside busy roads (Carlisle & Sharp, 2001). A later review summarizes lung, heart, cognitive and whole body effects of particulate matter, ozone and carbon monoxide during exercise, and ways to reduce exposure (Giles & Koehle, 2014). The U.S. AQI runs from Good (0 to 50, green) through Moderate (51 to 100, yellow), Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups (101 to 150, orange), Unhealthy (151 to 200, red), Very Unhealthy (201 to 300, purple) and Hazardous (301 and above, maroon) (U.S. EPA AirNow, n.d.). EPA guidance for smoke events advises reducing inhaled dose by being active when air quality is better, lowering effort, and shortening time outdoors; avoiding strenuous activities such as going for a run when it is smoky; and treating coughing, wheezing or shortness of breath as a sign to immediately reduce exposure (U.S. EPA, n.d.).

## Rules for the coach
- Heat acclimation before a hot race or hot season: plan 7 to 14 consecutive or near consecutive days of heat sessions of 60 to 90 minutes, at easy to moderate intensity, in conditions that cause heavy sweating and a clear rise in core temperature (hot room, outdoor heat, or extra clothing indoors). Most of the benefit comes in the first week.
- Schedule the block to finish in the final 1 to 2 weeks before the hot event; if done earlier, add 1 to 2 heat sessions per week to maintain it, since adaptation fades over about 2 to 4 weeks.
- During the first days of heat training, reduce planned intensity or volume; heat sessions count as training stress.
- Heat training for temperate racing: describe it as possibly helpful, not guaranteed. For riders who want to try it, a block of about 5 sessions of 50 minutes per week for 5 weeks is the protocol with published gains in hemoglobin mass in elite cyclists.
- Heat safety: stop a heat session and cool down at once if the rider reports dizziness, confusion, nausea, headache, chills or stops sweating. Advise urgent medical care for confusion or collapse in heat, which can be heat stroke. Riders with heart disease, prior heat stroke, or on medications that affect heat tolerance should get medical clearance before heat training.
- Hot race day: drink about 6 mL/kg every 2 to 3 hours before, use 0.5 to 0.7 g/L sodium drinks for rides over 1 hour, keep body mass loss under about 2%, and never finish heavier than you started.
- Precooling for time trials and hot races: about 1 L of ice slurry or cold drink in the 30 to 60 minutes before the start, plus ice vest or cold towels during warm up; shorten the warm up in severe heat. Practice it in training first.
- Lower target power in heat: expect a lower sustainable power until acclimatized and pace by heart rate and feel as well as watts.
- Cold weather: dress in layers chosen for the rider, protect hands, feet, ears and face, and plan for the descent and stops. Advise extra caution or an indoor ride when the wind chill is below about -27 °C (-18 °F), on icy roads, or for riders with asthma or heart conditions. Warm up longer before hard efforts in the cold.
- Riding at altitude: for each 1,000 m above about 500 to 800 m, lower threshold based power targets by roughly 5 to 7% for an unacclimatized rider, and more above about 2,500 m. Pace the first efforts conservatively.
- Racing at altitude without time to acclimatize: start conservatively, use the lower power targets above, and keep carbohydrate and fluid intake up.
- Altitude camps: to target red cell gains, live at about 2,000 to 2,500 m for at least 3 to 4 weeks; train hard sessions lower if possible; keep high intensity efforts short with long recoveries; increase sleep and fueling in the first days.
- Iron before altitude: advise riders to have iron status checked by their doctor before an altitude block and not to start iron supplements without medical advice.
- Hypoxic tents: tell riders that red cell gains appear to need at least about 12 hours per day (16 or more is better) for at least 3 to 4 weeks, and that a placebo controlled study in cyclists found no benefit at 16 hours per day for 4 weeks. Do not promise results.
- Altitude illness: if a rider at altitude reports severe headache, vomiting, unusual breathlessness at rest, confusion or poor coordination, tell them to stop, descend and seek medical care.
- Air quality: check the local AQI (or the AirNow Fire and Smoke Map in the U.S.) before outdoor rides.
  - AQI 0 to 50: no change.
  - AQI 51 to 100: no change for most riders; riders who are unusually sensitive to air pollution should consider shorter or easier rides.
  - AQI 101 to 150: riders with asthma, heart or lung disease, older riders and children should move hard sessions indoors or cut them short; others can ride but should avoid long, hard efforts if symptoms occur.
  - AQI 151 to 200: move hard or long sessions indoors for everyone.
  - AQI above 200: indoor training only.
  - Any visible or smelled smoke, or any coughing, wheezing or chest tightness: shorten or stop the ride and move indoors.
- Ozone: on high ozone days, ride in the morning rather than the afternoon. On any day, prefer routes away from heavy traffic.
- These AQI rules are the app's conservative interpretation of EPA categories, not an exercise specific research threshold. Riders with asthma or heart or lung disease should follow their doctor's advice on exercising in poor air.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- Whether heat acclimation improves performance in cool conditions for trained cyclists is disputed; one positive study (Lorenzo et al., 2010) has been followed by null results (Karlsen et al., 2015; Keiser et al., 2015).
- Hemoglobin mass gains from heat training come mainly from one research group in elite cyclists (Lundby et al., 2023; Rønnestad et al., 2025).
- Cooling studies are almost all in men, and the effect on self paced performance is modest (van de Kerkhof et al., 2024).
- Live high train low results vary widely between individuals, and placebo and training camp effects are hard to remove (Chapman et al., 1998; Bonetti & Hopkins, 2009).
- Normobaric (tent) protocols have mixed results; a placebo controlled cyclist study at 16 hours per day found no effect (Siebenmann et al., 2012).
- The 12 hours per day minimum hypoxic dose comes from a review, not a dose finding trial (Millet et al., 2010).
- The roughly 6% VO2max drop per 1,000 m comes from a small sample of runners in a chamber (Wehrlin & Hallén, 2006), and the power data come from 10 riders doing 5 minute efforts (Clark et al., 2007); the loss for longer efforts and well acclimatized riders may differ.
- Cold effects on cycling performance are poorly studied (Castellani & Tipton, 2015).
- There are no validated AQI thresholds specific to training intensity or duration; the coach rules for air quality are precautionary.
