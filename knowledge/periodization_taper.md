# Periodization, Tapering, Peaking and the Off Season

## Summary
Periodization means planning training in phases so that fitness rises over months and peaks for the races that matter. The classic ("traditional" or "linear") model builds a broad base of easy volume first, then adds more race specific intensity as the season nears, and trains several abilities at once in each phase. Block periodization instead packs a lot of one type of work (often hard intervals) into a short block, then switches focus. Reverse periodization puts the intensity early and the volume later.

For a rider new to structured training, the most useful message is this: how hard and how much you train in total, done consistently, matters more than which named model you follow. Block periodization shows small benefits over traditional planning in a handful of small studies, but when total load is matched, the order of the work often makes little difference. The best supported tool in this whole area is the taper: cutting training volume by roughly 40 to 60% over about two weeks while keeping intensity and keeping most of your sessions, which reliably improves race performance by a few percent. After the season, a short break is fine, but stopping completely for more than a few weeks loses a lot of fitness, so reduced training beats total rest.

## Size of the evidence
The pool is modest. Most trials have 10 to 60 athletes. The biggest pooled analyses hold a few hundred: 437 athletes on intensity distribution, about 350 on detraining, 174 endurance athletes on tapering (a larger 27 study taper meta-analysis does not report its total). Block periodization rests on 107 pooled athletes, mostly from one research group.

The biggest datasets are observational marathon training logs, not cyclists: about 158,000 runners on tapering, 300,000 on training breaks and 119,000 on intensity mix. They show what goes with faster races, not what causes it. No large cyclist training log study was found. Recovery week ratios, multiple peaks and off season length still rest on small studies or convention.

## Key findings

### Block periodization gives small advantages over traditional organization in short studies
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 6 studies, about 107 athletes (trained cyclists, cross country and alpine skiers, hockey players)**
In trained cyclists (VO2max about 62 mL/kg/min), a 4 week block with 5 high intensity interval (HIT) sessions in week 1 then 1 HIT session per week for 3 weeks improved VO2max by 4.6%, peak power by 2.1% and power at 2 mmol/L lactate by 10%, while a group doing 2 HIT sessions per week every week did not change, despite matched total HIT and low intensity volume (Rønnestad, Hansen and Ellefsen, 2014a). A 12 week version (one 5 HIT week followed by three 1 HIT weeks, repeated) gave a larger VO2max gain than 2 HIT per week (8.8% vs 3.7%) with moderate effect sizes on 40 minute all out power (Rønnestad et al., 2014b). Similar results were seen in elite cross country skiers over 5 weeks (Rønnestad et al., 2016). The meta analysis by Mølmen, Øfsteng and Rønnestad (2019) pooled only 6 studies and found small effects favoring block over traditional for VO2max (SMD 0.40, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.79) and peak power (SMD 0.28, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.54). The authors stress caution because studies were small and of low methodological quality (mean PEDro 3.7 out of 10). Most trials come from one research group, with 15 to 20 riders each, and the pooled analysis held 107 athletes in studies averaging about 5 weeks. Graded down from Moderate: too few athletes, low quality, one main group.

### The theory behind block periodization is argued more than tested
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: none, expert argument (no athletes tested)**
Issurin (2010) argued that traditional mixed training causes conflicting adaptations, excess fatigue and too little stimulus per ability, and cannot produce multiple peaks, and proposed concentrated blocks on few abilities. These are reasoned arguments, not trial results. Kiely (2018) argues that periodization theory rests on an outdated view of stress and that planning models have not been critically tested, which supports treating any named model as a flexible framework rather than a law.

### When total load is matched, the order of interval types matters little
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 1 study, 63 athletes (trained cyclists)**
In 63 trained cyclists, 12 weeks of 24 interval sessions plus high volume low intensity riding were arranged as long to short intervals (4x16 min, then 4x8, then 4x4 min), short to long (the reverse), or mixed every block. All groups improved 5 to 10% in 40 minute all out power, peak power and VO2peak, with no differences between groups and similar rates of responders and non responders (Sylta et al., 2016). This is the strongest direct evidence in cyclists that load, not sequence, drives most of the gain. It is one trial, so it is graded down from Moderate, though it fits pooled data showing intensity models differ little at similar load (Rosenblat et al., 2025).

### Reverse periodization is not better than other models
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 11 studies, about 200 athletes (swimmers, runners, strength measures; no cyclists)**
A systematic review of 11 studies (200 athletes) found reverse periodization gave no better performance or VO2max gains than traditional or block models (González-Ravé et al., 2022). It held no cycling trials, so this is inferred from other sports.

### Most of the training should stay easy, with a smaller amount of hard work across all phases
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 17 studies, about 437 athletes (trained endurance athletes, mixed sports including cyclists)**
**Real world data: 119,452 athletes, marathon training logs from a fitness platform, observational**
Descriptive data from successful endurance athletes show roughly 80% of sessions at low intensity and about 20% containing hard work, and intensification studies in well trained athletes do not show that adding more HIT gives long term gains (Seiler, 2010). Olympic and world champion skiers and biathletes trained about 800 h per year with about 90% of time below the first lactate threshold, and their absolute HIT volume stayed stable across all phases while total volume followed a traditional pattern (Tønnessen et al., 2014). These are elite skiers, and the data are descriptive. In logs from 119,452 marathon runners, faster runners did far more volume, mainly as easy running, and most used a pyramidal split (Muniz-Pumares et al., 2025), an association in runners. In pooled trials, polarized gave a small VO2peak edge only in short studies and highly trained athletes, with no time trial advantage (Silva Oliveira et al., 2024), and individual data from 13 studies (348 athletes) found no difference between polarized and pyramidal, with recreational athletes possibly doing better on pyramidal (Rosenblat et al., 2025).

### A taper of about 2 weeks with 41 to 60% less volume, same intensity and frequency, works best
**Evidence: Strong (that a taper improves performance) / Moderate (for the exact numbers)**
**Pool: 27 studies, N not reported in abstract (competitive swimmers, runners, cyclists, triathletes); endurance only: 14 studies, about 174 athletes (mainly runners and cyclists)**
**Real world data: about 158,000 athletes, marathon training logs from a fitness app, observational; non cyclists**
A meta analysis of 27 studies in competitive athletes found the best performance gains with a 2 week taper (overall effect 0.59), volume reduced exponentially by 41 to 60% (effect 0.72), and no change to intensity or frequency (Bosquet et al., 2007). A later meta analysis restricted to endurance athletes (14 studies, 174 athletes, including cyclists) found 8 to 14 day tapers had the largest effect on time trial performance, 41 to 60% volume reduction was best, keeping intensity and frequency improved time trials while cutting them did not, and progressive and step tapers both worked with no significant difference between them (Wang et al., 2023). Mujika and Padilla (2003) summarize that performance usually improves by about 3% (range 0.5 to 6%), that intensity should be maintained, that volume can drop 60 to 90% and frequency by no more than about 20%, and that progressive non linear tapers beat step tapers. In cyclists specifically, a 7 day step taper with 50% volume reduction at maintained intensity improved 20 km time trial by 5.4%, while 30% and 80% reductions did not significantly help (Neary, Bhambhani and McKenzie, 2003, n = 11, very small groups). In more than 158,000 recreational marathoners, strict tapers (volume dropping steadily each week) and tapers up to 3 weeks went with faster races; a strict 3 week taper was linked to a median 2.6% faster finish than a minimal taper (Smyth and Lawlor, 2021). This is association, in runners. In 209 half Ironman triathletes, self reported taper details did not differ between fast and slow groups (Gilinsky et al., 2014). "Taper helps" is consistent across two meta-analyses and huge observational data, so it stays Strong; the exact numbers rest on subgroups of small trials, so they are Moderate.

### Intensity is the element to protect during a taper and during reduced training
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: taper subgroups of 27 studies (N not reported in abstract) and 14 studies, about 174 athletes; plus 3 small trials in non athletes**
Reviews conclude that high intensity work before and during the taper maintains or enhances adaptations, and that volume, not intensity, should carry the load reduction (Mujika, 2010). In recently trained non athletes, VO2max was maintained for 15 weeks when frequency was cut from 6 to 2 or 4 days per week, or duration cut by up to two thirds, as long as intensity was kept (Hickson and Rosenkoetter, 1981; Hickson et al., 1982), but cutting intensity by one third or two thirds lost VO2max and endurance (Hickson et al., 1985). Long endurance (2 h or more) dropped when duration was cut to 13 minutes per day, so long rides matter for long event fitness.

### A harder block right before the taper can help, but true overreaching backfires
**Evidence: Moderate (overload then taper helps) / Limited (overreaching removes the gain)**
**Pool: subgroup of 14 studies, about 174 athletes, N per subgroup not reported in abstract (endurance athletes); overreaching harm rests on 1 study of 33 triathletes**
In 33 trained triathletes, 3 weeks of overload followed by a taper gave about 2.6% greater peak cycling performance in those who were only acutely fatigued, but those who became functionally overreached (performance actually dropped with high perceived fatigue) did not get the extra gain and had more infections (70% vs 10 to 20%) (Aubry et al., 2014). The endurance taper meta-analysis found that tapers after a pre taper overload improved time trials more than standard tapers (Wang et al., 2023). Most peak performances came within the first 2 weeks of taper. Modelling studies suggest a prior overload makes a slightly longer, deeper taper optimal (Thomas, Mujika and Busso, 2008) and that a 20 to 30% training bump in the final 3 days might add tiny gains (Thomas, Mujika and Busso, 2009); treat these as theoretical.

### Short peaking cycles for tight race calendars
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 1 study, 17 athletes (elite cyclists)**
Elite cyclists who did 6 days of daily HIT followed by a 5 day step taper improved VO2peak (4.0% vs 0.8%) and 1 minute peak power more than cyclists doing an 11 day taper only (Rønnestad and Vikmoen, 2019, n = 17). This suggests a compressed overload plus taper can be used between races a few weeks apart, but it is a single small study in elite riders.

### Peaking for multiple races in a season is poorly studied
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: none, apart from descriptive data on 11 champion skiers and a review of world class runners**
Taper research focuses on a single peak; multiple peaking is a key gap (Pyne, Mujika and Reilly, 2009). Issurin (2010) proposes block sequences partly to allow several peaks per season, but this is theory. Champion skiers kept training volume, frequency and intensity largely unchanged into their key race, and only 3 of 11 took a rest day in the final 5 days (Tønnessen et al., 2014), which shows real world peaking often uses milder tapers than the meta analyses suggest. World class distance runners race 6 to 9 times a year and start tapering 7 to 10 days before the main event (Haugen et al., 2022).

### Detraining: fitness drops quickly once training stops
**Evidence: Strong (that fitness falls) / Limited (exact rates for amateur cyclists)**
**Pool: 21 studies, about 350 athletes (mixed sports and training levels)**
**Real world data: about 300,000 athletes, marathon training logs, observational; non cyclists**
In trained athletes, less than 4 weeks of insufficient training causes a rapid fall in VO2max, blood volume, cardiac output, capillary density and oxidative enzymes, and a lower lactate threshold (Mujika and Padilla, 2000a). Beyond 4 weeks, VO2max keeps falling but stays above untrained levels in long trained athletes, while recently gained VO2max is completely lost (Mujika and Padilla, 2000b). Metabolic changes can start within about 10 days and capillary density may fall within 2 to 3 weeks (Mujika and Padilla, 2001a; 2001b). These losses can be limited by reduced training that keeps intensity and cuts volume a lot and frequency only moderately (Mujika and Padilla, 2000b). These are narrative reviews. A meta-analysis of 21 studies (about 350 athletes, counted from its study table) found VO2max fell after short (4 weeks or less) and more after longer stops, less so when athletes kept some exercise (Zheng et al., 2022). In about 300,000 recreational marathoners, breaks of 7 days or more in the 16 weeks before the race were linked to 5 to 8% slower finishes than the same runners' unbroken races, with late breaks costing more (Feely et al., 2022), an association only. Graded up from Moderate because the direction is consistent across 21 studies and several hundred athletes.

### Off season: reduced training beats complete rest
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 2 studies, 14 athletes each (kayakers); supported by a detraining meta-analysis subgroup (21 studies, about 350 athletes, mixed sports)**
In top level kayakers after the season, 5 weeks of complete rest cut VO2max by 10.1% versus 4.8% with reduced training (García-Pallarés et al., 2009). In world class kayakers, 5 weeks of stopping after a taper produced larger losses in strength than reduced training (García-Pallarés et al., 2010). These are small studies (n = 14) in kayakers, not cyclists. The detraining meta-analysis also found that some exercise during a break limits VO2max loss (Zheng et al., 2022), but no trial has compared off season plans in cyclists.

### Recovery weeks every 3rd or 4th week are coaching convention, not tested
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: none, coaching convention**
No controlled study was found that compares 2:1, 3:1 or no recovery week patterns in endurance athletes. Block studies used one hard week then three easier weeks (Rønnestad et al., 2014b), and a 6 week cyclist mesocycle with 2 loading weeks then 2 recovery weeks found time trial power rose in the recovery weeks while 15 second sprint power stayed lower, and riders reporting more stress performed worse (Wallett et al., 2018). The overtraining consensus says overload needs enough recovery but gives no ratio (Meeusen et al., 2013). Illness is linked to high "monotony" (similar hard load every day) and strain (Foster, 1998), which supports lighter periods. The exact 3:1 or 2:1 ratio is coaching convention.

## Rules for the coach
- Do not sell any periodization model as clearly superior. Prioritize consistent total load (hours per week and hard sessions per week) over model choice.
- Default plan: traditional structure with about 80% of sessions easy (below about 75% FTP, RPE 2 to 4) and 1 to 3 hard sessions per week year round. Keep some HIT in every phase rather than removing it in base. The non easy work can be polarized (mostly hard intervals) or pyramidal (some tempo and threshold too); for recreational riders a pyramidal mix is fine, since pooled trials show no clear difference.
- Present block periodization as a reasonable option, not a proven upgrade (pooled evidence is about 107 athletes). Offer it for experienced riders (at least 1 to 2 years of structured training) who have plateaued: 1 week with 4 to 5 HIT sessions (for example 5x4 to 6x5 min at 106 to 120% FTP, RPE 8 to 9), then 2 to 3 weeks with 1 HIT per week plus easy volume. Do not schedule HIT on more than 2 consecutive days for riders new to blocks, and add at least 1 easy day after every 2 HIT days.
- Do not prescribe reverse periodization as a performance advantage. It is acceptable if it suits the rider's calendar or weather.
- Taper for A races: 10 to 14 days. Reduce weekly volume by 40 to 60% from the normal training level, progressively (for example 30% less in week 1 and 50 to 60% less in week 2). Keep interval intensity the same. Keep session frequency at 80% or more of normal (no more than about 1 fewer session per week). Make the drop steady each week; do not let volume drift back up (runner data link relaxed tapers to slower races). After a big overload block, up to 3 weeks is acceptable (Wang et al., 2023, found tapers up to 21 days effective).
- Keep 1 to 2 short HIT sessions in the taper (for example 3 to 5x2 to 3 min at 106 to 120% FTP, RPE 8), with the last one 2 to 4 days before the race. Include short openers (a few 30 to 60 s efforts) the day before if the rider likes them; this is convention.
- B races: 3 to 7 day mini taper with 30 to 50% volume reduction. C races: no taper, treat as a hard training session.
- Plan an overload block of 2 to 3 weeks (volume or HIT up about 10 to 20%) before an A race taper only if the rider is recovering well. If performance drops while RPE is high, fatigue persists beyond 3 to 5 days, or the rider gets ill, stop the overload and start recovery or taper early.
- For races 3 to 5 weeks apart: 1 to 2 weeks of normal training, optionally a short HIT emphasis of up to 5 to 6 days for experienced riders, then a 5 to 7 day taper. Limit true A peaks to about 2 to 3 per season; this limit is convention.
- Recovery week: schedule one every 3rd or 4th week by default (every 3rd week for riders over about 50, beginners, or those with high life stress; this is convention). Cut volume by about 30 to 50%, keep 1 short intensity session, keep frequency.
- Skip or move a planned recovery week if the rider just had an unplanned easy week (illness, travel). Insert an extra one if fatigue markers persist.
- Off season: 1 to 3 weeks of unstructured or very light riding and other activity, then reduced training. Avoid more than 3 to 4 weeks with no endurance exercise. During transition keep at least 2 to 3 sessions per week with some efforts above threshold to limit VO2max loss.
- Treat unplanned breaks of 7 days or more close to an A race as a real cost (runner data link them to 5 to 8% slower races). If the rider is ill or injured, rest is still right; then lower the race goal rather than cramming lost training.
- After a break of more than 2 weeks, restart at about 50 to 70% of previous weekly volume and build back over 2 to 4 weeks; this restart ramp is convention.
- When a rider must cut training (busy period, travel), cut duration and number of easy rides first, keep at least 2 sessions per week with hard efforts, and keep at least one ride of 2 h or more every 1 to 2 weeks if their event is long.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- Block periodization evidence comes mostly from one research group, with 15 to 20 riders per trial and interventions of 4 to 12 weeks. Long term (full season or multi year) comparisons do not exist.
- It is unclear whether block benefits come from the block structure itself or from the higher concentration of HIT; the 12 week study acknowledges this.
- No trials compare 2:1 vs 3:1 vs 4:1 loading patterns in endurance athletes. Recovery week frequency is coaching practice.
- Multiple peaks per season have almost no direct research. Guidance here is extrapolated.
- Taper meta analyses include swimmers and runners; cycling specific taper trials are few and very small. The biggest taper dataset is observational marathon data; none was found for cyclists. Elite athletes in practice often taper less than the meta analyses recommend.
- The total sample of the largest taper meta-analysis (Bosquet et al., 2007) could not be checked from its abstract.
- Detraining data mix highly trained athletes, recently trained people and other sports; exact rates of loss for amateur cyclists are not well known.
- The FTP and RPE ranges in the rules are practical translations of the intensities used in studies (which mostly used % VO2max, % HRmax or lactate), not values tested directly.
- Off season length, and how much intensity to keep in transition, is not well studied in cyclists.
