# Strength Training for Cyclists (Heavy Lifting, Concurrent Training, Maintenance, Bone Health)

## Summary

Adding heavy lower body strength training to normal bike training improves cycling performance in trained cyclists. Riders who lifted 2 times per week for roughly 10 to 25 weeks improved 40 to 45 minute power, maximal aerobic power and sprint power more than riders who only rode. The gains come from better efficiency, better short power and holding a higher share of VO2max, not from a higher VO2max. Heavy loads (about 4 to 10 reps near the heaviest load possible) have the best evidence; explosive and plyometric work has weaker support. The effect is moderate, cycling studies are small and many come from one Norwegian group, but larger meta-analyses in runners and mixed endurance athletes point the same way.

Lifting and riding together causes little loss of maximal strength or muscle size, though explosive strength can suffer when both are in one session, men may lose a little leg strength gain, and very high riding volume reduces gains. Once built, strength is kept in season with 1 heavy session per week; stopping loses the benefit within about 8 weeks. Road cyclists, especially masters riders, often have low bone density. Heavy lifting plus jumps is the most practical protection; large trial pools in non cyclists show small but real bone gains.

## Size of the evidence

The cycling pool is small. The biggest cycling synthesis covers 17 studies and about 262 cyclists, rated low certainty, and most trials have 6 to 12 riders per group. Other endurance sports add more: about 652 runners in a running economy meta-analysis, 28 studies across running, cycling, skiing and swimming, and an umbrella review of 17 systematic reviews, all pointing the same way.

Concurrent training data are large but mostly in general adults: 43 studies with about 1,090 people and 59 studies with about 1,346. Exercise order has 19 trials with about 482 adults.

Bone data are the largest, but almost all in non cyclists: 162 trials with about 10,475 adults and 80 studies with about 5,581 postmenopausal women show small gains from exercise. Bone data in cyclists are mostly cross sectional (groups of up to 93 riders) and show associations, not cause and effect. One trial (36 elite cyclists) has tested bone loading in cyclists.

Still small: in season maintenance (2 trials, 26 cyclists), explosive vs heavy work in cyclists (3 trials, about 47 riders), exercise selection, and anything beyond one season.

## Key findings

### Heavy strength training improves time trial and long effort performance in trained cyclists
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 17 studies, about 262 athletes (trained endurance cyclists, 60 women)**
A 2026 meta-analysis of 17 studies (262 cyclists, 60 female, mean VO2max about 61 mL/kg/min; 145 lifters vs 117 controls) found heavy strength training (at or above 80% of 1 RM, 1 to 3 sessions per week, 5 to 25 weeks) improved cycling performance (time to exhaustion and time trial combined, effect size 0.46), efficiency (0.35) and anaerobic power (0.56) vs riding only, with no change in VO2max, power at VO2max or maximal metabolic steady state. Certainty was rated low (Llanos-Lagos et al., 2026). Rønnestad and Mujika (2014) concluded heavy, not explosive, training has the best evidence. A 2010 review of 5 trials found the 3 positive studies replaced some riding with lifting, while the 2 that added lifting on top of full volume found no gain (Yamamoto et al., 2010). Across endurance sports, a meta-analysis of 28 studies found moderate performance gains (effect size 0.52), largest with maximal force training and consistent across levels (Berryman et al., 2018; N not reported in abstract). An umbrella review of 17 systematic reviews agreed, though most reviews were of low quality (Ramos-Campo et al., 2025).

Key single trials: 12 weeks of 2 weekly sessions (4 exercises, 3 sets of 4 to 10 RM) improved 40 minute power and thigh muscle size without raising body mass (Rønnestad et al., 2010a, n = 11 vs 9); 25 weeks in young elite riders improved maximal aerobic power and 40 minute power (Rønnestad et al., 2015, n = 9 vs 7); 8 weeks of half squats improved economy by 4.8% (Sunde et al., 2010, n = 8 vs 5).

### Cycling economy and holding a higher share of VO2max are the likely mechanisms
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 17 studies, about 262 athletes (trained cyclists) for cycling efficiency; about 652 athletes (moderately to highly trained runners) for the parallel economy finding**
In 19 well trained female cyclists, 11 weeks of heavy lifting improved 40 minute power, economy and the fraction of VO2max sustained, with no VO2max change; gains tracked quadriceps size (r = 0.73) and a shift from IIX toward IIA fibers (Vikmoen et al., 2016). The benefit appears similar in men and women, and muscle growth seems to be part of how it works (Vikmoen & Rønnestad, 2021). Proposed mechanisms include delayed recruitment of less efficient fibers, fiber shifts, and faster force development (Rønnestad & Mujika, 2014; Mujika et al., 2016). In runners, a meta-analysis of about 652 athletes found heavy loads gave a small economy gain (effect size 0.27) while submaximal loads did not (Llanos-Lagos et al., 2024). Mechanism data inside cycling still come from small trials.

### Heavy vs explosive vs plyometric training
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 3 small trials, about 47 athletes (trained and competitive cyclists); in runners, 22 studies (N not reported in abstract)**
Direct comparisons in cyclists are scarce. Combined maximal and explosive training for 20 weeks improved strength, sprint power and power at VO2max (Beattie et al., 2017, n = 6 vs 9). Replacing 37% of riding with explosive work kept sprint power but did not improve time trials (Bastiaans et al., 2001, n = 6 vs 8). Explosive jumps plus low cadence bike sprints improved 1 and 4 km power by about 8%, but the bike sprints confound the result (Paton & Hopkins, 2005, n = 9 vs 9). In runners, a meta-analysis of 22 studies found heavy training (especially at or above 90% of 1 RM) beat plyometrics, whose effects were trivial (Eihara et al., 2022). Practical reading: heavy lifting is the base; explosive work is an optional add on for sprint and bone goals.

### Concurrent training interference is small for maximal strength and muscle size
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 59 studies, about 1,346 athletes (healthy adults 18 to 50, untrained to highly trained); also 43 studies, about 1,090 (healthy adults)**
An early meta-analysis of 21 studies found endurance training reduced strength, power and muscle gains, more so with frequent and long sessions and with running than cycling (Wilson et al., 2012). An update of 43 studies (1,090 participants) found no meaningful loss of maximal strength (standardized mean difference -0.06) or muscle size (-0.01), but a small loss of explosive strength (-0.28) when both were done in one session, not when separated by 3 or more hours (Schumann et al., 2022). A 2024 meta-analysis of 59 studies (1,346 participants) found a small loss of lower body strength gains in men (-0.43) but not women, and no VO2max blunting in trained endurance athletes (Huiberts et al., 2024). In well trained cyclists, lifting on top of high riding volume gave smaller strength and muscle gains than in non endurance people (Rønnestad et al., 2012). Interference is small and does not cancel the performance benefit.

### Order and spacing of lifting and riding on the same day
**Evidence: Moderate (lifting first gives slightly more leg strength in general adults), Limited (effect on cycling performance)**
**Pool: 19 trials, about 482 athletes (general adults, 285 men and 197 women)**
A meta-analysis of 19 randomized trials found lifting before endurance gave a small extra gain in leg strength (standardized mean difference 0.19) with no VO2max difference (Gao & Yu, 2023), matching an earlier 10 study meta-analysis (about 7% more dynamic strength; Eddens et al., 2018). Separating sessions by 3 or more hours removed the explosive strength penalty (Schumann et al., 2022). A 2026 cyclist review stresses intensity, distance from failure and spacing from endurance sessions (de Pablos et al., 2026). No cycling trial has tested lifting the day before key intervals; keeping them apart is convention.

### One session per week maintains gains in season; stopping loses them
**Evidence: Limited (in cyclists), Moderate (that stopping loses strength, general populations)**
**Pool: 2 trials, about 26 athletes (well trained and elite cyclists); for training cessation in general, 103 studies (N not reported in abstract)**
Well trained cyclists who switched to 1 heavy session per week for 13 weeks of the race season kept muscle size and strength and improved 40 minute power more than riders who only rode (Rønnestad et al., 2010b, n = 6 vs 6). Elite cyclists who stopped lifting for 8 weeks lost squat jump, maximal aerobic power and 30 second sprint power (Rønnestad et al., 2016, n = 7 vs 7). Outside cycling, a meta-analysis of 103 studies found stopping resistance training reduces strength and power, more with longer breaks (Bosquet et al., 2013), and a narrative review found 1 heavy session per week can keep strength for up to 32 weeks (Spiering et al., 2021). The rule rests on small cycling trials plus consistent general data.

### Program design that worked in trials
**Evidence: Moderate (for the general recipe), Limited (for exact numbers)**
**Pool: 17 studies, about 262 athletes (trained cyclists); no moderator effect of frequency or length was found**
The best studied protocol uses half squat, single leg press, one legged hip flexion and toe raise, 3 sets of 4 to 10 RM, 2 times per week for 10 to 12 weeks (Rønnestad et al., 2010a). Rønnestad and Mujika (2014) advise 2 to 3 sets of 4 to 10 RM with 2 to 3 minutes rest, fast lifting intent, technique with light loads first, and less riding load in the first 2 to 3 weeks. A 2026 review suggests 1 to 2 weekly sessions of 3 to 5 compound lifts at 60 to 85% of 1 RM, 2 to 4 sets, stopping sets at 10 to 20% velocity loss (de Pablos et al., 2026). The meta-analysis found no effect of program length or frequency, so 1 vs 3 sessions per week is unresolved (Llanos-Lagos et al., 2026).

### Exercise selection
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: none for head to head exercise choice; core work rests on 1 small trial (young female mountain bikers)**
Exercises should train the main cycling muscles in movements similar to pedaling (Rønnestad & Mujika, 2014). Squat variants and leg press, including single leg versions, are the most studied. Deadlifts and hip hinge movements are reasonable compound choices but have not been tested on their own in cycling trials. For core training, 8 weeks of trunk strength work (3 sessions per week) in young female mountain bikers improved trunk strength and reduced side to side bike movement, but did not change physiological cycling economy (Blechschmied et al., 2026). Core work is reasonable for trunk strength and bike control, not as a direct economy tool.

### Body mass worries are mostly unfounded with this kind of program
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 2 small trials, about 33 athletes (well trained cyclists)**
The main trials report increased thigh muscle size without an increase in body mass (Rønnestad et al., 2010a) or no change in body weight (Sunde et al., 2010). A 2026 review concludes that properly programmed resistance training does not appear to hurt performance through unwanted muscle growth (de Pablos et al., 2026). Higher lower body lean mass is linked to higher power per kg of lean mass in female cyclists (Mujika et al., 2016).

### Cyclists, especially masters road cyclists, often have low bone density
**Evidence: Moderate (that it is low), Limited (for what fixes it in cyclists)**
**Pool: 31 studies (N not reported in abstract; road, track and mountain cyclists, mostly cross sectional)**
**Real world data: 93 athletes, elite cyclists at different career stages, observational**
A systematic review of 31 studies concluded road cycling gives no meaningful bone benefit and adult road cyclists have low spine bone density; mountain biking or other sports may help (Olmedillas et al., 2012). Two more reviews agree but note the data are mostly cross sectional and inconsistent: cyclists usually had lower spine density than runners or active people but often similar to sedentary controls (Nagle & Brooks, 2011, 13 studies), and 9 of 10 cycling studies linked professional cycling with low density (Abrahin et al., 2016). Recreational male cyclists had 63% osteopenia vs 19% in runners (Rector et al., 2008). Master cyclists had lower spine and hip density than non athletes, 15% at osteoporosis level (Nichols et al., 2003), and lost more over 7 years, with less loss in those who lifted or did impact work (Nichols & Rauh, 2011). In 93 elite cyclists, low spine density (Z score below -1) affected 27% of early career, 64% of advanced career and 50% of retired men and 45% of active women, associated with low body mass, past fractures, little bone loading and signs of low energy availability (Hilkens et al., 2023). In 40 male racers, weight training hours were associated with higher spine and hip density (Mathis & Caputo, 2018). These are associations, not cause and effect.

### Heavy loading and impact build bone, even in older adults with low bone mass
**Evidence: Strong (small benefit in non cyclists), Limited (in cyclists)**
**Pool: 162 trials, about 10,475 athletes (adults aged 18 to 82, mostly non athletes); also 80 studies, about 5,581 (postmenopausal women)**
In postmenopausal women with low bone mass, 8 months of twice weekly heavy lifting (5 sets of 5 above 85% of 1 RM) plus impact raised spine density by 2.9% vs a 1.2% loss in a light program (Watson et al., 2018); in men 45 and older the same program raised spine density by 4.1% and hip by 2.8% (Harding et al., 2020). Large pools confirm a small effect: 162 trials (10,475 adults) found effect sizes of 0.22 at the spine and 0.28 at the femoral neck (Feng et al., 2026), and 80 studies (5,581 postmenopausal women) found 0.27 to 0.41, similar with or without osteopenia or supervision (Mohebbi et al., 2023). Men have less data: 10 trials (555 men) found a mild benefit (Lu et al., 2023), another meta-analysis a hip but not spine benefit (Hamilton et al., 2022). In adults 65 and older, resistance training raised hip density by about 2.8% (O'Bryan et al., 2022). The only cyclist trial found five 5 minute jump bouts per week for 18 weeks (with collagen) preserved femoral neck density in 36 elite road cyclists while controls lost some (Hilkens et al., 2024).

## Rules for the coach

- Recommend lower body heavy strength training to every road and time trial cyclist who has the time, framed as a performance tool, not just injury prevention. Expect a moderate gain in 40 to 60 minute power and sprint power, not a VO2max gain.
- Base phase (build strength): 2 sessions per week for 10 to 12 weeks minimum. Use 3 to 4 compound lower body exercises (for example half squat or back squat, single leg leg press or split squat, hip hinge such as Romanian deadlift, plus a calf raise or hip flexion exercise). 2 to 4 sets per exercise, 2 to 3 minutes rest between sets.
- Load progression in the base phase: weeks 1 to 3 at about 10 to 12 reps with a load the rider could lift 2 to 3 more times (RPE about 7 out of 10), technique first. Weeks 4 to 8 at 6 to 8 RM loads (RPE 8). Weeks 9 to 12 at 4 to 6 RM loads (RPE 8 to 9). Do not take sets to failure; stop when bar speed clearly slows.
- Cue every rep: lower under control, push up as fast as possible.
- In the first 2 to 3 weeks of a new lifting block, cut planned bike intensity (fewer or shorter sessions above 90% FTP) because legs will be heavy and sore.
- Schedule the lifting base phase in the off season or early base, when bike intensity is lowest.
- In season maintenance: 1 session per week, 2 to 3 sets of 3 to 5 exercises at heavy loads (4 to 6 RM, RPE 8), short session (about 30 to 45 minutes). Never drop lifting entirely for more than 4 weeks during the season unless the rider wants to accept losing the gains within about 8 weeks.
- Same day order: if strength is the priority that day, lift first, then ride easy (below 75% FTP). If a key bike session is the priority, ride first and lift at least 3 hours later, or lift on a different day.
- Do not place a heavy leg session within about 24 hours before a key interval session, VO2max test or race (convention, not tested).
- Pair heavy lifting days with hard bike days where possible (hard day after hard day, then an easy day), so easy days stay easy. This is convention.
- Riders with very high riding volume (for example more than 15 hours per week) should expect smaller strength gains; keep lifting volume low and intensity high. Male riders may see slightly smaller leg strength gains from combined training than women; this is not a reason to skip lifting.
- Taper: keep 1 short heavy session in race weeks, at least 3 to 4 days before the target event; drop lifting in the final 3 days (convention).
- Core work: 2 to 3 short sets of trunk exercises 2 to 3 times per week is fine for trunk strength and bike control. Do not promise it improves cycling economy.
- Do not hold back strength training from female cyclists; the benefit is at least as large as in men.
- Bone health: for masters riders (40 or older), riders with low bone density or past fractures, and riders who only cycle, include impact and heavy loading year round. Add 2 to 3 sets of 5 to 10 jumps 2 to 3 times per week once the rider has lifted for 4 weeks without problems (short jump bouts of about 5 minutes on up to 5 days per week are a tested alternative), plus heavy lifts (5 reps at RPE 8 or higher) at least weekly. Expect small gains or less loss, a few percent at most.
- Watch for low energy availability (low body mass, missed periods, frequent fractures) in riders with low bone density; it is linked to low bone density in elite cyclists. Refer to a doctor or sports dietitian.
- Suggest masters road cyclists ask their doctor about a bone density (DXA) scan. Riders with known osteoporosis should start bone loading under supervision.
- Weight concern: reassure that the trial programs increased leg muscle size with no gain in body mass. Monitor body mass if the rider cares; if it rises more than 1 to 2% with no performance gain, reduce sets.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed

- Most cycling strength trials are small (6 to 12 riders per group) and many come from the same research group in Norway. The 2026 meta-analysis (about 262 cyclists) rated the certainty of evidence as low. Larger pools in runners and mixed endurance athletes support the same direction but are not cyclist data.
- No consensus exists on the best weekly frequency (1 vs 2 vs 3 sessions) or program length; the meta-analysis found no moderating effect of either.
- The 2010 systematic review found that adding lifting on top of full riding volume sometimes failed to help, while replacing some riding with lifting worked better. It is unclear how much riding should be cut.
- The value of explosive and plyometric training for endurance cycling performance (as opposed to sprinting) is unclear.
- Effects on power at lactate threshold are mixed across studies.
- Same day order evidence comes mostly from general populations, not cyclists, and looks at strength outcomes rather than cycling performance.
- Specific exercise choices beyond squat and leg press (deadlift, single leg work, core) have not been tested head to head in cyclists.
- The sets, reps and RPE numbers in the rules are a practical translation of the trial protocols, not tested dose response values.
- The two large concurrent training meta-analyses disagree slightly on maximal strength (no loss vs a small loss in men), and both are mostly in general adults.
- Bone density data in cyclists are mostly cross sectional; they cannot show that cycling causes low bone density or that lifting fixes it.
- Bone loading trials (LIFTMOR and the large meta-analyses) were in older adults with low bone mass, not cyclists. Whether lifting fully offsets the bone loss from high volume cycling is unknown.
- Long term data (more than 1 season) on strength training in cyclists are almost absent.
