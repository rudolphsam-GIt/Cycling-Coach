# Training by Time Available

## Summary
How much time a rider has each week changes what kind of training makes sense. When time is short, intensity has to carry more of the load, because hard intervals produce a lot of adaptation per minute. Classic lab studies show that a few minutes of very hard work can match early gains from several hours of steady riding in untrained or recreationally active people. In already trained cyclists, adding two hard interval sessions a week to normal easy riding improves time trial power within a few weeks. But these gains are fast and early. Over months and years, extra easy volume is what most well trained riders use to keep improving, and adding more intensity on top of a small base does not keep paying off forever.

Fitness is also cheaper to keep than to build. Studies of reduced training show that VO2max and short endurance can be held for about 15 weeks with as few as two hard sessions a week, or with sessions cut to a third of their length, as long as intensity stays high. Long endurance (rides of 2 hours or more) is the first thing to slip when volume drops. The coach should match the weekly structure to the hours, protect intensity when hours fall, and treat any extra hours as mostly easy riding.

## Key findings

### A few minutes of very hard work can match early gains from much longer steady training
**Evidence: Moderate**
In 16 active young men, six sessions of 4 to 6 x 30 s all out sprints over 2 weeks (about 2.5 h total time) improved 50 kJ and 750 kJ time trials and muscle oxidative capacity as much as six sessions of 90 to 120 min steady riding (about 10.5 h) (Gibala et al., 2006). A 6 week follow up in 20 active but untrained adults found similar mitochondrial and fat oxidation changes with about 1.5 h per week of sprint intervals versus about 4.5 h per week of steady riding (Burgomaster et al., 2008). Over 12 weeks in sedentary men, 3 x 20 s all out sprints within a 10 minute session, three times a week, raised VO2peak by 19%, the same as 45 min of moderate cycling (Gillen et al., 2016). Limits: all three studies used small groups of untrained or recreationally active people, and lasted 2 to 12 weeks. They show the minimum dose works for beginners. They do not show that sprints alone can build a race ready cyclist.

### In trained cyclists, two interval sessions a week added to normal easy riding improve performance quickly
**Evidence: Moderate**
In 38 highly trained cyclists and triathletes (VO2peak about 64 mL/kg/min), adding two HIIT sessions a week for 4 weeks on top of normal easy training improved 40 km time trial performance by 4.4 to 5.8%, while controls did not change (Laursen et al., 2002). Several formats worked, including 8 intervals at the power reached at VO2peak and 12 x 30 s at 175% of peak power. In 20 trained male cyclists, six interval sessions over 3 weeks improved 40 km time trial most with 8 x 4 min at 85% of peak aerobic power, and also with 12 x 30 s at 175% of peak power (Stepto et al., 1999). Short intervals (effort matched) beat long intervals for VO2max gains over 10 weeks in a small cyclist study (Ronnestad et al., 2015). The review by Laursen and Jenkins (2002) argues that in already highly trained athletes, more submaximal volume does not seem to add further gains, and improvement comes mainly from HIIT. Limits: the trials were short (3 to 10 weeks) and the review is narrative.

### Over the long run, top endurance athletes use high volume with a small share of hard work
**Evidence: Moderate (descriptive)**
Descriptive data from national and international endurance athletes training 10 to 13 times a week converge on about 80% of sessions at low intensity and about 20% containing hard work. Intensification studies in well trained athletes do not give convincing evidence that more HIIT gives long term gains (Seiler, 2010). This is mostly observational data from elite athletes with lots of time. It tells us what works at high volume, not what is best at 3 to 5 hours.

### Polarized is not clearly better than other distributions, and recreational riders may do as well or better with pyramidal
**Evidence: Moderate**
A 6 week crossover in 12 trained cyclists found a polarized plan (80% easy, 20% hard, about 6.4 h per week) gave larger gains in peak power and lactate threshold than a threshold heavy plan (Neal et al., 2013). An early meta analysis of 3 small trials favored polarized over threshold training for time trial performance (Rosenblat et al., 2019). A larger 2024 meta analysis (17 studies, 437 people) found polarized slightly better for VO2peak, but only in interventions under 12 weeks and in highly trained athletes, with no difference for time trial, time to exhaustion, or threshold power (Silva Oliveira et al., 2024). A 2025 individual participant network meta analysis (13 studies, 348 athletes) found no overall difference between polarized and pyramidal, with a signal that competitive athletes gain more VO2max from polarized and recreational athletes from pyramidal (Rosenblat et al., 2025). In recreational runners, a "focused" plan with more moderate work produced the same gains as polarized with 17% less training time (Festa et al., 2019). Practical meaning: at low hours, the coach does not need to force a strict 80/20 split. A pyramidal split (most time easy, a solid block of tempo and sweet spot, a small dose of VO2max work) is a reasonable default for a time crunched amateur.

### Fitness is cheaper to maintain than to build, and intensity is the variable to protect
**Evidence: Moderate**
Hickson's classic reduced training studies took untrained young adults through 10 weeks of 40 min a day, 6 days a week, at high intensity, then cut one variable for 15 weeks. Cutting frequency to 2 or 4 days a week kept VO2max at trained levels (Hickson & Rosenkoetter, 1981). Cutting session length from 40 to 26 or 13 min kept VO2max and 5 min endurance, but the 13 min group lost about 10% of long endurance (2 h or more) (Hickson et al., 1982). Cutting intensity by one third or two thirds caused VO2max to fall and long endurance to drop by 21% and 30% (Hickson et al., 1985). A 2021 narrative review concluded that endurance can be kept for up to 15 weeks with as few as 2 sessions per week or a 33 to 66% cut in volume (as low as 13 to 26 min per session), provided intensity is kept; strength and muscle size can be kept with 1 strength session per week and 1 set per exercise in younger people, while older people may need 2 sessions per week and 2 to 3 sets (Spiering et al., 2021). In a 32 week trial, a maintenance strength dose of one third or one ninth of the build dose kept muscle size in young adults (20 to 35 years) but not in older adults (60 to 75 years) (Bickel et al., 2011). Limits: the Hickson subjects were not trained cyclists, and the reviewers say there is not enough data for specific athlete recommendations.

### HIIT and steady training both raise VO2max, but steady training benefits grow with longer programs
**Evidence: Moderate**
A meta analysis of 28 controlled trials in healthy adults aged 18 to 45 found both endurance training and HIIT raise VO2max, with HIIT giving a small extra benefit on average (Milanovic et al., 2015). This was mostly in untrained or moderately fit people (mean starting VO2max about 41 mL/kg/min).

### Commuting and accumulated easy riding count
**Evidence: Limited to Moderate (untrained people)**
In 122 employees who began commuting by bike (mean one way trip 8.5 km, more than 3 times a week), maximal power per kg rose 13% in 6 months. Gains were larger in those who started less fit and in those who rode more kilometers (Hendriksen et al., 2000). A 1 year cycling to work trial in untrained adults also improved cholesterol and blood pressure markers (de Geus et al., 2008). These were untrained people. For a trained rider, commuting mainly adds easy volume, which helps long endurance and fat oxidation but will not replace structured intervals.

### Indoor riding tends to produce lower power at the same perceived effort
**Evidence: Limited**
In 12 recreationally trained cyclists told to give the same effort indoors and outdoors over 40 km, outdoor power was about 208 W versus 163 W in the lab, with heart rate also higher outdoors and similar RPE (Mieras et al., 2014). Skin temperature was higher indoors. One small study, and modern smart trainers and fans may close some of this gap. It supports good cooling indoors and using power targets rather than feel alone.

### How much intensity a time crunched rider can tolerate
**Evidence: Limited**
Most successful trials in trained cyclists used 2 hard sessions per week, on top of easy riding (Laursen et al., 2002; Ronnestad et al., 2015). The Stepto study used about 2 per week for 3 weeks. There is little controlled evidence on 3 or more hard sessions per week for months in amateurs. Coaching practice, not trial data, sets the common ceiling of 2 to 3 hard sessions per week. Treat a third hard session as an option only when sleep, mood and performance are good.

## Rules for the coach
- Ask for real weekly hours, not ideal hours. Plan for the hours the rider hits in a normal week, then treat extra hours as easy bonus riding.
- Hard sessions mean intervals at or above 88% FTP (sweet spot and up), or RPE 7 or higher. Easy means below about 75% FTP, RPE 2 to 4.
- Hard session cap by hours: under 4 h per week, at most 2 hard sessions; 4 to 10 h, 2 to 3 hard sessions; over 10 h, usually 2 to 3, rarely 4. Never schedule hard sessions on back to back days for a rider under 5 h unless one is a short sprint session.
- Under 5 h per week, intensity carries the plan. At least 2 of the 3 to 4 sessions should contain intervals. Use a pyramidal mix; strict polarized is not required.
- Example interval doses that have trial support: 4 to 6 x 30 s all out with 4 min easy (novices and short sessions); 8 x 4 min at about 105 to 110% FTP (Stepto's 85% of peak aerobic power); 8 intervals at about 120 to 130% FTP (Laursen's intervals at peak aerobic power, each lasting 60% of the rider's time to exhaustion at that power; in practice often 2 to 3 min, which is an estimate); 12 x 30 s near all out (about 200 to 230% FTP) with 4 to 4.5 min easy (175% of peak aerobic power in both trials). Threshold work such as 2 x 20 min at 88 to 95% FTP is standard coaching practice. All % FTP targets are coaching translations of study intensities that were set as % of ramp test peak power, so treat them as approximate.
- Keep at least one session of 2 h or more every 1 to 2 weeks when the rider's goals include events longer than 2 h. Long endurance is the first ability lost when sessions get short.
- When a rider's time drops for a busy period (work, family, travel), switch to maintenance: 2 to 3 sessions per week, keep 1 to 2 of them hard, cut duration before cutting intensity. Expect VO2max and short power to hold for about 8 to 15 weeks this way; warn that long ride endurance may drop.
- Strength maintenance during busy periods: 1 session per week, 1 to 2 hard sets per exercise for riders under about 50; 2 sessions per week and 2 to 3 sets for older riders.
- Count commuting as easy volume (RPE 2 to 4) in the weekly load. Do not let commutes turn into daily threshold efforts. If the rider commutes on a hard interval day, the commute stays easy.
- Indoors, set targets by power, use a fan, and expect RPE to feel higher at the same power. Keep indoor endurance rides shorter (60 to 90 min) and more structured than outdoor ones.
- Above about 10 to 12 h per week, add new hours as easy riding first. Do not add a fourth hard day just because hours went up.
- Progress weekly hours by no more than about 10 to 15% for 2 to 3 weeks, then take a lighter week (about 60 to 70% of normal hours) every 3 to 4 weeks. This is coaching practice, not tested in trials.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- The classic time efficient HIIT studies (Gibala, Burgomaster, Gillen) used untrained or recreationally active people for 2 to 12 weeks. Long term results in trained cyclists at 3 to 5 h per week are not well studied.
- Whether polarized beats pyramidal is unsettled. The newest analyses suggest no overall difference and possibly a level dependent effect, with recreational athletes doing at least as well on pyramidal.
- There is almost no controlled research on the best intensity split at very low volume (under 4 h per week) in trained cyclists.
- The safe number of hard sessions per week for amateurs over months is based on coaching practice, not trials.
- Maintenance research (Hickson) used young untrained people and heart rate based intensity, not trained cyclists with power data.
- Indoor versus outdoor evidence is one small study and may not reflect modern smart trainers.
- The table above is a coaching synthesis of the findings, not a tested prescription.
