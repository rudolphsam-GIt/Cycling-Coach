# Training Female Cyclists

## Summary
Most cycling training science was built on men: in six major journals from 2014 to 2020, only 34% of participants were female (Cowley et al., 2021). So many "rules" in this app (zone models, ramp rates, recovery times) are extrapolated to women, not tested in them. Women tend to fatigue a bit less in sustained tasks, use a bit more fat at moderate intensity (mainly when untrained), and may gain slightly less VO2max from the same dose. None of this justifies a separate training system.

The menstrual cycle has, on average, a trivial effect on performance, and the evidence for planning training around cycle phase is weak (McNulty et al., 2020; Colenso-Semple et al., 2023). Symptoms matter more than phase, so the coach should track symptoms and adjust day to day. The oral contraceptive pill has at most a trivial effect (Elliott-Sale et al., 2020). Three health issues matter far more than phase: low energy availability (REDs), iron deficiency, and muscle and bone loss around menopause. In pregnancy and postpartum, the coach supports and a clinician decides.

## Size of the evidence
The evidence pool is uneven. A few questions rest on big numbers: iron deficiency prevalence (a meta-analysis of 17,519 athletes), disordered eating in athletes (70,957 athletes in 177 studies), exercise in pregnancy (meta-analyses of more than 260,000 women, with randomised trials of several thousand), and menstrual symptoms in everyday exercisers (Strava surveys of 6,812, 7,766 and 8,060 women). Wearable data cover thousands more (2,596 women, 42,759 cycles). These big datasets are surveys and observational records: they show how common something is and what goes with what, not cause and effect.

The performance questions are much smaller. The menstrual cycle meta-analysis pools 1,193 women across 78 studies, the pill meta-analysis 590 women across 42 studies, and the VO2max trainability meta-analysis only 175 people. Many studies had 10 to 30 women and did not confirm cycle phase with hormone tests. Cycle phase based training has one randomised trial (24 women, resistance training) and no endurance trial. Data on female cyclists, masters women, postpartum return and menopause in athletes remain small.

## Key findings

### Most evidence comes from men
**Evidence: Strong**
**Pool: 1 audit of 5,261 papers in six journals (total people not reported in the sources checked here)**
Across 5,261 papers, about 66% of participants were male and 31% of studies were male only (Cowley et al., 2021). Many studies did not verify cycle phase or contraceptive status, which makes them hard to interpret (Elliott-Sale et al., 2021). Treat any training rule in other topic files as extrapolated to women unless it says otherwise. Women in midlife and older are even less studied.

### Women are often less fatigable, but it depends on the task
**Evidence: Moderate (single muscle lab tasks); Limited (cycling)**
**Pool: no meta-analysis found; 1 narrative review of many small lab studies (N not reported in abstract), plus 1 cycling study of 32 trained cyclists**
For matched isometric contractions at the same relative intensity, women usually fatigue more slowly than men. The gap shrinks or disappears with fast, high power, or dynamic tasks, and changes with muscle group and environment (Hunter, 2014). Most of this work is on single muscle groups in the lab. In one cycling study (16 men, 16 women, all trained), 90 minutes of heavy intensity riding lowered power at the first and second thresholds more in men than in women, so the women were somewhat more "durable" (Pastorio et al., 2026). This is one small study. It does not justify longer intervals by default; use her own test data.

### Substrate use differs a little, mostly in untrained people
**Evidence: Moderate**
**Pool: 35 studies (21 sedentary or recreational, 14 athletic), N not reported in abstract**
A meta-analysis of 35 studies found that during moderate intensity aerobic exercise, men had a higher respiratory exchange ratio than women in both sedentary and athletic groups, so men burned relatively more carbohydrate. But in athletes, fat oxidation did not differ by sex (Cano et al., 2022). Fuelling advice for female cyclists should not be lowered on the idea that women "run on fat". Carbohydrate needs for hard and long rides still apply.

### Training response: similar direction, possibly slightly smaller VO2max gains
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 8 studies, about 175 people (mostly untrained adults)**
A meta-analysis of 8 studies (175 people) found that men gained more VO2max than women from the same endurance training dose, about 1.95 mL/kg/min more in relative terms (Diaz-Canestro & Montero, 2019). There is no good evidence that women need a different intensity distribution, interval types, or periodisation, and sex differences in recovery time are barely studied.

### Menstrual cycle phase has a trivial average effect on performance
**Evidence: Moderate (for the small average effect); Limited (for individual effects)**
**Pool: 78 studies, about 1,193 women (mostly recreationally active, small lab studies)**
**Real world data: 6,812 women, Strava user survey, observational**
A meta-analysis of 78 studies (1,193 women) found performance might be trivially lower in the early follicular phase (around menstruation) than in other phases, with an effect size of about minus 0.06. Large differences between studies and poor study quality (42% rated low and 26% very low) meant no general guidelines could be made. (McNulty et al., 2020). Smaller meta-analyses agree: perceived exertion (17 studies, 160 women; Prado et al., 2024) and VO2max in trained women (5 studies; Britton & Duncan, 2026) did not differ by phase.

Symptoms are a different story. In 6,812 exercising women recruited through Strava (not on combined hormonal contraception), mood changes, fatigue, cramps and breast pain were each reported by more than 80%. A higher symptom score went with greater odds of missing or changing training and missing events (Bruinvels et al., 2021). This is self report and shows association only, but it supports a symptom led, not phase led, approach.

Wearable data also shift with the cycle. A review of 16 wearable studies found HRV was higher early in the cycle and lower toward the end, by about 3% to 9% (de Jager et al., 2026). In 2,596 women with 42,759 logged cycles, short sleep shifted these metrics whatever the phase (Gonzalez et al., 2026). A late cycle HRV dip may therefore be hormonal, not fatigue.

### Cycle phase based training is not supported by current evidence
**Evidence: Limited (the evidence base itself is weak)**
**Pool: 1 umbrella review of systematic reviews (N not reported in abstract) and 1 randomised trial of 24 women (resistance training); no endurance trial found**
An umbrella review found highly variable, weak findings and concluded it is premature to say short term hormone changes affect strength performance or adaptation (Colenso-Semple et al., 2023). A later randomised trial in 24 women over three cycles found that loading training into the follicular or the luteal phase gave no more muscle or strength gain than spreading it evenly; total training volume mattered (D'Souza et al., 2026). Endurance specific data are even fewer. Putting all hard work in the follicular phase is a hypothesis, not a finding. Tracking is still useful to spot missing periods and link symptoms to days.

### Hormonal contraception: at most a trivial effect on performance
**Evidence: Moderate (oral pill); Limited (other forms)**
**Pool: 42 studies, about 590 women (83% of studies rated moderate, low or very low quality)**
**Real world data: 8,060 women, Strava user survey, observational**
A meta-analysis of 42 studies (590 women) found pill users might perform slightly worse than naturally menstruating women, but any effect was most likely trivial, and performance was steady across the pill cycle. The evidence does not support advice to use or avoid the pill for performance (Elliott-Sale et al., 2020). In 430 elite athletes, about half used hormonal contraception, and side effects were reported more often with progestin only methods (Martin et al., 2018). In large Strava surveys, about 40% of 7,766 exercising women used hormonal contraception and users reported fewer cycle symptoms (Freemas et al., 2026), and among 8,060 women users were less likely to miss or change training (Bowring et al., 2026). These are self reported associations, not a reason to recommend it. Contraception is a medical decision. The pill causes a withdrawal bleed, not a true period, so it can hide missing periods caused by low energy availability.

### Low energy availability and REDs affect women and men
**Evidence: Strong (that it is common and linked with harm); Moderate (cause and effect, thresholds and screening)**
**Pool: IOC consensus summarising many studies (total N not reported); largest single synthesis is disordered eating in 177 studies, about 70,957 athletes**
**Real world data: 411 female athletes from 34 sports, pooled bone scan data from seven projects, observational**
The IOC defined Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport in 2014 as a broader replacement for the Female Athlete Triad that also affects men (Mountjoy et al., 2014); the 2018 update listed harms to metabolism, menstrual function, bone, immunity, protein synthesis and heart health (Mountjoy et al., 2018). The 2023 update treats it as a spectrum from adaptable to problematic and reports estimated prevalence of LEA or REDs indicators of 23% to 79.5% in females and 15% to 70% in males. It no longer treats 30 kcal per kg fat free mass per day as a firm clinical cutoff, because it came from short lab studies in a few sedentary women (Mountjoy et al., 2023). Low carbohydrate availability magnifies the harm. Low energy availability should be ruled out before diagnosing overtraining.

The REDs CAT2 tool uses indicators such as missing 3 or more periods in a row, cycles longer than 35 days, low testosterone in men, low T3, bone stress injuries, low bone density and disordered eating (Mountjoy et al., 2023). Cycling is a risk sport: long rides, weight sensitive climbing, little bone loading. In 50 competitive male road cyclists, 28% had low energy availability on a questionnaire and interview, and 44% had low lumbar spine bone density. (Keay et al., 2018). In 411 women from 34 sports, about a third of those not on hormonal contraception had severe menstrual disturbance, in every sport type. Endurance athletes had lower spine bone density than other athletes (17% versus 2% low), and endurance athletes with severe disturbance had lower hip bone density (Øistuen et al., 2026). Across 70,957 athletes, about 19% screened positive for disordered eating, higher in women (Ghazzawi et al., 2024). Most of this is cross sectional; cause and effect data come from short, small lab studies, so that part stays Moderate.

### Iron deficiency is common in female endurance athletes
**Evidence: Strong (that it is common); Moderate (causes and treatment)**
**Pool: 122 reports, about 17,519 student athletes in 23 countries (prevalence); 11 trials, about 1,014 people (daily versus alternate day iron, general population)**
**Real world data: 1,190 athletes, German pre-participation screening; 1,862 exercising women, online and London Marathon survey; both observational**
Causes include menstrual losses, low intake, altitude, and a post exercise rise in the iron regulating hormone hepcidin (Sim et al., 2019). A meta-analysis of 17,519 student athletes found about 54% had ferritin below 50 µg/L and 23% below 20 µg/L (Thompson et al., 2025). In 1,190 screened athletes, 20% had ferritin below 20 µg/L; female sex raised the odds about 4 times, and iron deficiency went with lower VO2peak (Keller et al., 2024). Heavy menstrual bleeding, a major risk factor, was reported by 54% of 789 women online and 36% of 1,073 London Marathon runners (Bruinvels et al., 2016). Low stores (ferritin) fall first, anaemia comes last. There is no agreed ferritin cutoff (studies use 7 to 40 µg/L), and illness can falsely raise ferritin (Pedlar et al., 2018). In iron depleted women (ferritin 25 µg/L or less), a single 60 mg iron dose on alternate days was absorbed better than daily dosing, because daily doses raise hepcidin (Stoffel et al., 2017). That was a small study, not in athletes; a meta-analysis of 11 trials (1,014 people with anaemia) found both schedules raised haemoglobin about equally, with fewer side effects on alternate days, at very low certainty (Dhanvijay et al., 2025). Test before supplementing, since overload is possible.

### Pregnancy: exercise is safe and helpful for most, under medical guidance
**Evidence: Strong (moderate exercise is safe and lowers some complications); Limited (elite level training loads)**
**Pool: 106 studies, about 273,182 pregnant women (complications), including randomised trials of about 3,300 to 6,900 women per outcome; 11 studies, about 2,256 women (elite athletes)**
Raised from Moderate because of very large pooled data. In randomised trials, exercise only programmes cut the odds of gestational diabetes, gestational high blood pressure and pre-eclampsia by about 40%; about 140 minutes a week of moderate exercise such as stationary cycling gave at least a 25% cut (Davenport et al., 2018). Across 46 studies (266,778 women), exercise was not linked to miscarriage or perinatal death, though that evidence was very low quality and mostly covered sessions up to 60 minutes (Davenport et al., 2019). Without obstetric or medical complications, physical activity in pregnancy is safe and desirable. Active women can usually continue, aiming for at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity; stationary cycling suits, and fall or abdominal trauma risks should be avoided (ACOG, 2020). The Canadian guideline agrees, over at least 3 days, with aerobic and resistance training (Mottola et al., 2018). Elite level data are limited: in 11 studies of elite athletes (2,256 women), outcomes were mostly similar to controls, but two tiny studies (7 women) saw brief fetal heart rate drops after high intensity exercise (Wowdzia et al., 2021).

### Postpartum return: gradual, symptom led, clinician guided
**Evidence: Limited**
**Pool: 11 studies, about 482 women (372 elite athletes), very low certainty; return timelines rest on expert guidance**
Elite athletes returned before 6 weeks far more often than non-elite women and may have more postpartum injuries, including stress fractures (Kimber et al., 2021). ACOG (2020) supports resuming activity after birth as medically appropriate. UK physiotherapy guidance on returning to running, the closest high impact model, suggests waiting until around 12 weeks postnatal and passing a set of load and pelvic floor tests first, and stresses it guides clinical reasoning, not a protocol (Goom et al., 2019). Cycling has less pelvic floor impact than running but adds saddle pressure and fall risk. Breastfeeding and sleep loss raise REDs risk.

### Perimenopause and menopause: protect muscle and bone, expect symptom variability
**Evidence: Moderate (muscle and bone); Moderate (exercise does not reduce hot flash frequency); Limited (performance and recovery)**
**Pool: 40 trials, about 2,230 postmenopausal women (exercise and bone); 21 trials, about 2,884 women (exercise and hot flashes); 1 cohort of 234 women (muscle loss)**
In 234 Finnish women followed from perimenopause to early postmenopause, lean and muscle mass fell, and menopause status predicted the loss. Activity was linked to keeping more (Juppi et al., 2020). Road cycling alone does not seem to build bone, and adult road cyclists often have low spine bone density (Olmedillas et al., 2012). In postmenopausal women with low bone mass, 8 months of supervised high intensity resistance and impact training twice a week (5 sets of 5 at above 85% of 1RM) raised spine bone density by about 2.9% versus a drop in controls, without excess harm (Watson et al., 2018). A meta-analysis of 40 trials (2,230 women aged 50 to 60) found aerobic, resistance and combined training all raised bone density slightly at the spine and hip, with combined training looking best (Hejazi et al., 2025). The gains are small, so the aim is slowing loss, not reversing it.

Hot flashes affect about 50% to 80% of women around menopause and come from changes in the brain's temperature control as oestradiol falls (Gombert-Labedens et al., 2025). A randomised trial of 261 women found exercise did not reduce hot flashes or night sweats (Daley et al., 2015), and a Cochrane review found no difference in 3 trials (454 women) (Daley et al., 2014). The largest meta-analysis (21 trials, 2,884 women) found no change in how often hot flashes happen (high certainty) and a small drop in severity that disappeared when high risk of bias trials were removed (Liu et al., 2022). Sudden jumps in intensity may make them feel worse (Witkowski et al., 2023). Research on menopause and recovery or heat tolerance in sport is scarce. Hormone therapy is a medical decision.

## Rules for the coach
- Never diagnose. For any health flag below, tell the rider to see a doctor, and lower training stress until they do.
- Do not plan training by cycle phase by default. Offer optional cycle and symptom tracking. If a rider logs symptoms (cramps, heavy bleeding, poor sleep, gut upset) rated moderate or worse, allow swapping that day's hard session for an easy ride or rest, then move the hard session 1 to 3 days later.
- If a rider sees a repeatable pattern over at least 3 cycles, let her choose to plan lighter days there. This is preference, not science.
- Refer to a doctor if a rider not on hormonal contraception, not pregnant, and not postmenopausal reports: no period for 3 months or more, cycles regularly longer than 35 days, or a big change from her usual pattern. Treat this as a possible REDs sign, not as normal for athletes.
- Refer if a rider describes her periods as very heavy (lay rules of thumb, not from the sources below: soaking through protection every 1 to 2 hours, or bleeding more than 7 days), since heavy bleeding is common in exercising women and raises iron deficiency risk (Bruinvels et al., 2016; Pedlar et al., 2018).
- Never advise starting, stopping, or changing contraception or hormone therapy.
- REDs screening triggers, for any sex: unplanned weight loss, a stress fracture, repeated illness, falling performance with high fatigue, low libido, mood changes, or strong anxiety about food or weight. On any trigger, stop planned increases in load, hold or reduce volume, prompt a fuelling review, and recommend a doctor or sports dietitian.
- Never set weight loss goals with a large calorie deficit during heavy training blocks. Cap weight loss at about 0.5 kg per week (a convention, not a tested threshold) and keep carbohydrate around hard sessions (Mountjoy et al., 2023).
- For female cyclists who report fatigue, breathlessness, or falling performance without a training reason, suggest asking a doctor about iron status (ferritin and haemoglobin). Do not suggest iron supplements without a blood test. If the doctor prescribes iron, the rider can ask about alternate day morning dosing. Because about 1 in 4 athletes in pooled data had ferritin below 20 µg/L (Thompson et al., 2025), suggest women in regular endurance training ask their doctor about a routine ferritin check, such as yearly (the interval is a convention).
- Pregnancy: plan nothing beyond what the rider's clinician has approved. Default to indoor riding at moderate intensity (able to talk), at least 150 minutes per week if approved. Tell the rider to stop and contact her clinician for any warning sign they have given her or any new symptom such as bleeding, fluid loss, chest pain, dizziness, or painful contractions (a general list; the guidelines give the full warning signs).
- Postpartum: require clinician clearance before structured training. Start with easy riding, add intensity only after several weeks of pain free easy volume. Flag leakage, pelvic heaviness, or pain for pelvic health physiotherapy. Do not copy elite athletes' early returns (Kimber et al., 2021).
- Perimenopause and later: include 2 strength sessions per week, progressing toward heavy loads (sets of about 5) where safe, plus some impact work (jumps or brisk walking). Riders with known osteoporosis need clinician approval for heavy or impact work.
- For riders reporting hot flashes or night sweats, check sleep and recovery scores more closely, allow cooler, indoor, or earlier sessions, and avoid sudden big jumps in intensity. Do not present exercise as a treatment for hot flashes; suggest a doctor if they disrupt sleep or life.
- HRV and resting heart rate in naturally menstruating riders: if she logs her cycle, compare with the same part of earlier cycles, and do not treat a modest late cycle HRV dip alone as a fatigue flag; act when it comes with poor wellness or falling performance (de Jager et al., 2026).
- Use each rider's own test data and response to training. Do not apply sex based multipliers to zones, ramp rates, or recovery days.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- Nearly all load, recovery and ramp rate guidance is extrapolated from men.
- Whether any woman benefits from phase based training; endurance trials are missing.
- Effects of contraceptives other than the combined pill.
- The right ferritin cutoff for athletes (7 to 40 µg/L in studies).
- The energy availability level at which harm starts.
- Whether exercise eases hot flash severity (at most a small, fragile effect).
- Most large datasets (Strava surveys, wearable records, screening data) are self selected and observational.
- Sex differences in fatigability and durability during real cycling rest on one study of 32 riders.
- How menopause changes recovery, heat tolerance and training response in trained cyclists.
- Safe elite training loads in pregnancy and timelines for postpartum return to hard cycling.
