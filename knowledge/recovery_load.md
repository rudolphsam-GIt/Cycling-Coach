# Recovery and Training Load Management

## Summary
Training works by overload followed by recovery. The aim is to push hard enough to adapt without sliding into a state where performance stays down for weeks. Short dips in performance after a hard block are normal and usually clear within about 2 weeks; dips that last weeks to months are a warning sign (Meeusen et al., 2013). Deliberately driving a rider into functional overreaching before a taper does not seem to give a bigger peak and raises illness risk (Aubry et al., 2014). The most reliable early warnings are falling performance plus how the rider feels: simple daily wellness questions track training load better than most blood or heart markers (Saw et al., 2016).

The fitness and fatigue numbers (CTL, ATL, TSB) are a useful bookkeeping tool built on the Banister model, but the model's parameters are uncertain for any one person and the common targets, such as ramp rates of 5 to 8 CTL per week and race day TSB of +15 to +25, are coaching conventions, not tested limits. The acute to chronic workload ratio has serious statistical problems and should not be used to predict injury. Resting HRV can help time hard sessions. Sleep is the best recovery tool: 7 hours or more for adults, and sleep loss of 6 hours or less clearly hurts performance. Cold water immersion may blunt strength gains, compression and massage mostly help soreness, and alcohol after training impairs muscle repair.

## Key findings

### Functional overreaching, non-functional overreaching, and overtraining syndrome
**Evidence: Moderate (consensus definitions; diagnosis relies on exclusion)**
The ECSS and ACSM consensus defines overreaching as a short term drop in performance from training and non training stress, where recovery takes days to weeks. If it is followed by a performance gain after recovery, it is functional overreaching (FOR). If recovery takes weeks to months it is non-functional overreaching (NFOR), and long term decline taking months or more is overtraining syndrome (OTS) (Meeusen et al., 2013). Recovery from overreaching within about 2 weeks is possible, while OTS may take months or years. No single blood, hormone, or immune marker can diagnose OTS; diagnosis means first ruling out illness, infection, low energy or carbohydrate intake, iron deficiency, and other causes. Common training errors listed include big jumps in volume or intensity, training monotony, many competitions, and outside stressors like travel, work, and family stress. The 2023 IOC REDs statement adds that many published "overtraining" cases showed signs of low energy availability, so underfuelling should be ruled out first (Mountjoy et al., 2023).

In 8 trained cyclists, 2 weeks of intensified training lowered maximal power and time trial performance, raised mood disturbance by 29%, lowered maximal heart rate by about 9%, and raised perceived effort, while lactate and hormone markers did not change. Overreaching appeared after about 7 days of intensified training with limited recovery (Halson et al., 2002). In 33 triathletes, 11 of 23 in an overload group became functionally overreached. The ones who were only acutely fatigued gained more from the taper than those who were overreached, and infections were much more common in the overreached group (70% versus 20% and 10%) (Aubry et al., 2014).

### Warning signs that matter most
**Evidence: Moderate**
Performance decline at a normal or higher effort, plus raised fatigue and mood disturbance, are the earliest and most useful signs (Halson et al., 2002; Meeusen et al., 2013). A lower maximal heart rate and inability to reach normal interval heart rates are common in overreached cyclists. Other signs listed in the consensus include sleep problems, frequent illness, loss of motivation, and heavy legs. The consensus advises keeping accurate performance records, cutting intensity or volume or giving a full rest day when performance drops or the athlete reports excess fatigue, avoiding monotony, and including at least one passive rest day per week, especially during hard blocks (Meeusen et al., 2013).

### Subjective wellness measures beat most objective markers
**Evidence: Moderate**
A systematic review of 56 studies found subjective and objective measures generally did not agree. Where they differed, 85% of cases favoured subjective measures for sensitivity and consistency. Subjective well being got worse with acute and chronic load increases and improved with load reductions (Saw et al., 2016). Responsive items included fatigue, physical recovery, general well being, non training stress, and feeling "in shape". The authors suggest daily short questions, a longer questionnaire weekly, and objective tests about once per training cycle, and recommend looking at individual items rather than a single total.

### The Banister fitness fatigue model and CTL, ATL, TSB
**Evidence: Moderate (as a concept); Limited (as an individual predictor)**
The Banister impulse response model treats performance as fitness minus fatigue, where each training dose adds to both and fatigue decays faster than fitness (Clarke & Skiba, 2013). The TrainingPeaks Performance Management Chart is a simplified version: CTL ("fitness") is an exponentially weighted average of daily TSS with a default 42 day time constant, ATL ("fatigue") uses 7 days, and TSB ("form") is CTL minus ATL. Note that CTL and ATL are load averages, not measured fitness or fatigue. In a study of 9 elite swimmers, the Banister model fit the data reasonably well (R squared about 0.79), but confidence intervals for the most useful parameters were wide and some parameters were highly correlated, so their meaning for an individual was questionable (Hellard et al., 2006). The model ignores sleep, illness, life stress, heat, altitude, and fuelling, and TSS depends on an accurate FTP.

### Ramp rate limits are convention, not evidence
**Evidence: Limited (coaching convention)**
TrainingPeaks author Joe Friel suggests that an increase of about 5 to 8 CTL points per week suits most athletes, with higher rates leading into "crash" territory, and that a few athletes can handle more than 10 per week for about a week. This is based on his coaching experience, not on a cited study (Friel, 2015). Other coaching sources suggest 3 to 5 for steady progress. No controlled study has tested CTL ramp rates against injury, illness, or performance in cyclists. Team sport research does link rapid load spikes with soft tissue injury, and also suggests that a high, well built chronic load is protective (Gabbett, 2016), but those data are from running based team sports, not cycling.

### The acute to chronic workload ratio is not a reliable injury predictor
**Evidence: Moderate (for the critique)**
The ACWR (for example, last 7 days of load divided by last 28 days) became popular in team sports. Critics showed that the common "coupled" calculation includes the acute week inside the chronic average, which creates a built in correlation between the two (Lolli et al., 2019). No studies have properly estimated a causal effect, so changing the ratio to prevent injury is a conjecture, and ratios have known statistical problems (Impellizzeri et al., 2020). A follow up reanalysis found that the chronic load term adds little, and recommended dropping the ACWR and its theory (Impellizzeri et al., 2021). ATL divided by CTL in a cycling app is a version of this ratio and inherits the same problems. It was never validated in cyclists.

### Taper and race day form
**Evidence: Moderate (taper structure); Limited (TSB targets)**
A meta-analysis of 27 studies found the best taper lasted about 2 weeks, with training volume cut by 41% to 60% and intensity and frequency kept the same (Bosquet et al., 2007). See the periodization_taper topic for details. TSB race targets are convention. Friel aims for TSB about +15 to +25 for A races, notes some athletes race best at +5 to +10, suggests -10 to 0 for B races, calls -10 to -30 a productive range during hard training, warns that below -30 raises illness and injury risk, and says above +25 means fitness is being lost (Friel, n.d.). These ranges come from experience with TrainingPeaks users and have not been tested in studies.

### HRV guided training can help time hard sessions
**Evidence: Moderate (small RCTs)**
In 40 recreational runners, HRV guided training (hard sessions only when morning HRV was within the athlete's smallest worthwhile change, otherwise easy) did fewer hard sessions (13.2 versus 17.7) yet improved 3000 m performance, while the fixed plan group did not; the between group difference was small (Vesterinen et al., 2016). In 17 well trained cyclists over 8 weeks, the HRV guided group improved peak power (5.1%), second threshold power (13.9%), and 40 minute time trial (7.3%), while the traditional group did not improve significantly, but the direct group comparison was not clearly different (Javaloyes et al., 2019). In elite athletes HRV can move in either direction with fatigue, so averaging over several days and comparing to each athlete's own baseline is advised (Plews et al., 2013).

### Sleep: at least 7 hours, and loss of 6 hours or less hurts performance
**Evidence: Strong (sleep loss harms); Moderate (extension helps)**
The AASM and Sleep Research Society recommend at least 7 hours per night for adults, and note that more than 9 may suit young adults and people recovering from sleep debt (Watson et al., 2015). A meta-analysis of 69 publications found that sleep loss to 6 hours or less lowered exercise performance by about 7.6% on average, with large variation. Full deprivation and cutting sleep at the end of the night (early waking) were consistently harmful, each extra hour awake before exercise cost about 0.4%, and afternoon or evening performance was hurt more than morning performance (Craven et al., 2022). In 11 college basketball players, extending sleep (aiming for 10 hours in bed) added about 111 minutes per night and improved sprint time, shooting accuracy, and mood over 5 to 7 weeks; there was no control group (Mah et al., 2011). The athlete sleep consensus notes that athletes often sleep less than 7 hours, that most studies lack women and good controls, and advises individual sleep needs rather than a fixed target (Walsh et al., 2021).

### Cold water immersion may blunt strength gains; endurance effects unclear
**Evidence: Moderate (strength); Limited (endurance)**
In 21 active men doing 12 weeks of strength training, 10 minutes of cold water immersion after each session led to smaller gains in strength and muscle mass than active recovery, and blunted muscle signalling for up to 2 days (Roberts et al., 2015). A meta-analysis found regular cold water immersion reduced resistance training adaptations, while endurance performance gains did not seem to be harmed, based on few studies (Malta et al., 2021). A meta-analysis of 99 recovery studies found immersion, cryotherapy, compression, active recovery, and massage all reduced soreness to some degree after exercise (Dupuy et al., 2018).

### Compression and massage mainly help soreness and short term recovery
**Evidence: Moderate**
A meta-analysis of 23 studies found compression garments gave a small benefit to recovery (effect size 0.38), mostly for strength recovery. The one large effect was cycling performance 24 hours later, based on few studies (Brown et al., 2017). A meta-analysis of randomised studies found massage did not improve sprint, jump, strength, endurance, or fatigue, but gave small benefits for soreness and flexibility (Davis et al., 2020). Massage was ranked the most effective method for soreness and perceived fatigue in another meta-analysis (Dupuy et al., 2018). Placebo effects are likely for soreness.

### Alcohol impairs recovery
**Evidence: Moderate (mechanistic, small samples)**
In 8 active men, drinking alcohol after a combined strength and cycling session lowered muscle protein synthesis, even when protein was eaten with it (Parr et al., 2014). A review concluded that alcohol at the levels athletes often drink may impair immune and hormone function, blood flow, and protein synthesis, slowing recovery from muscle damage, with smaller effects on rehydration and glycogen refilling. Effects depend on dose, timing, and recovery time available (Barnes, 2014). Most data are from men.

## Rules for the coach
- Daily check in: ask 4 to 5 questions on a 1 to 5 scale (fatigue, muscle soreness, sleep quality, stress, mood or motivation). Use a rolling 7 day baseline. If 2 or more items are 2 or more points worse than baseline, or the total is more than 20% below baseline, replace that day's intervals with easy riding (zone 1 to 2, 60 minutes or less) or rest. The items come from Saw et al. (2016); the cutoffs are practical choices, not tested thresholds.
- Performance flag: if a rider cannot hit target power in 2 straight key sessions at normal or higher effort, or max heart rate is clearly lower than usual, schedule 2 to 3 easy or rest days, then retest.
- Overreaching flag: if performance is down and fatigue is high for more than 7 days despite easy days, cut weekly load by at least 50% for a week. If it persists past 2 to 3 weeks, tell the rider to see a doctor to rule out illness, iron deficiency, or low energy availability. Never diagnose overtraining.
- Include at least 1 full rest day or very easy day per week, and a lighter week (about 30% to 50% less volume) every 3 to 4 weeks. This structure is convention, supported by the consensus advice on rest days.
- Avoid monotony: never repeat the same load every day. Make easy days truly easy.
- Ramp rate (convention): default cap of 5 CTL points per week sustained. Allow up to 8 per week for 1 to 2 weeks in experienced riders with good wellness scores. Never plan above 8 per week for more than 1 week. For beginners or riders returning from a break, cap at 3 to 5. Stop ramping when wellness flags appear.
- TSB (convention): during build blocks keep TSB between about -10 and -30. If TSB falls below -30, insert recovery days. For A races, aim for TSB of about +5 to +25 on race day, using the rider's own history of best races to narrow the range. Do not let TSB stay above +25 for more than about a week outside of a planned break.
- Taper: about 2 weeks for main events, cut volume 40% to 60%, keep intensity and session frequency. See the periodization_taper topic.
- Do not use ATL divided by CTL (or any acute to chronic ratio) to predict or warn about injury. Use it only as a description of how fast load is changing.
- Treat CTL, ATL, and TSB as load bookkeeping. If they disagree with the rider's wellness and performance, trust the rider.
- HRV: only use HRV if the rider measures every morning the same way. Build a baseline over at least 2 to 4 weeks. Use a 7 day rolling average of ln RMSSD. If it falls below the normal band (for example, more than 0.5 standard deviations below the baseline mean), swap hard sessions for easy. If within the band, hard sessions are allowed. Do not react to a single low day alone.
- Sleep: target at least 7 hours per night, and 8 or more during heavy blocks if the rider can manage it. If the rider slept 6 hours or less, avoid maximal efforts that day, especially in the afternoon or evening, or move the key session to the next morning. Before big events, suggest extra sleep in the week before.
- After strength sessions, do not suggest ice baths within a few hours if strength or muscle gain is a goal. Cold water immersion is acceptable for multi day race recovery or heat, when performance tomorrow matters more than adaptation.
- Compression and massage: optional, mainly for soreness and stage races. Do not expect them to speed fitness gains.
- Alcohol: advise none on the evening after key sessions or races, and avoid it entirely the night before hard sessions or events. If the rider drinks, recommend eating protein and carbohydrate and rehydrating first.
- Illness: no intensity with fever, chest symptoms, or body aches. Resume with easy riding after symptoms clear, and refer to a doctor for chest pain, breathlessness, or palpitations.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- Safe CTL ramp rates and TSB targets have never been tested in controlled studies. All numbers here are coaching convention.
- Whether the Banister model, or its CTL and ATL time constants of 42 and 7 days, fit an individual cyclist well. Individual parameters are uncertain.
- The ACWR for injury prediction. Strongly criticised; defenders argue the coupling effect is small in real data.
- HRV guided training trials are small, short, and mostly in recreational or well trained athletes. Best thresholds and averaging windows are not settled; the 0.5 standard deviation band in the rules is a practical choice.
- Whether cold water immersion harms endurance adaptations. Very few studies.
- Sleep extension studies are small and often lack control groups. Most athlete sleep research has few women.
- The line between NFOR and OTS cannot be drawn with any test; it is known only after the fact.
- Most recovery and load research is in men and in non cycling sports.
