# Training Female Cyclists

## Summary
Most of the sports science used to plan cycling training was built on men. In a review of six major sport and exercise science journals from 2014 to 2020, only 34% of participants were female and only 6% of studies included women alone (Cowley et al., 2021). So many "rules" in this app (zone models, ramp rates, recovery times) are extrapolated to women, not tested in them. The main real differences are that women tend to fatigue a bit less in many sustained tasks, rely a bit more on fat at moderate intensity (mainly in untrained people), and may gain slightly less VO2max from the same training dose. None of these justify a separate training system. Good training for women looks like good training for men, adjusted to the person.

The menstrual cycle has, on average, a trivial effect on performance, and the evidence for planning training around cycle phase is weak (McNulty et al., 2020; Colenso-Semple et al., 2023). Symptoms matter more than phase, so the coach should track symptoms and adjust day to day. The oral contraceptive pill has at most a trivial effect (Elliott-Sale et al., 2020). Three health issues matter far more than cycle phase: low energy availability (REDs), iron deficiency, and, later in life, the loss of muscle and bone around menopause. Pregnancy and postpartum return are medical territory: the coach supports, a clinician decides.

## Key findings

### Most evidence comes from men
**Evidence: Strong**
Across 5,261 papers, about 66% of participants were male and 31% of studies were male only (Cowley et al., 2021). Women's hormone profiles change across the cycle, with hormonal contraception, and through menopause, and many past studies did not verify cycle phase or contraceptive status, which makes them hard to interpret (Elliott-Sale et al., 2021). Treat any training rule in other topic files as extrapolated to women unless it says otherwise. Women in midlife and older are even less studied.

### Women are often less fatigable, but it depends on the task
**Evidence: Moderate**
For matched isometric contractions at the same relative intensity, women usually fatigue more slowly than men. The gap shrinks or disappears with fast, high power, or dynamic tasks, and changes with muscle group and environment (Hunter, 2014). Proposed reasons include muscle fibre type, blood flow, and metabolism. Most of this work is on single muscle groups in the lab, not on cycling. It does not mean women should do longer intervals by default; it means a woman may hold a given percent of her threshold a little longer than a man, and the coach should use her own test data.

### Substrate use differs a little, mostly in untrained people
**Evidence: Moderate**
A meta-analysis of 35 studies found that during moderate intensity aerobic exercise, men had a higher respiratory exchange ratio than women in both sedentary and athletic groups, so men burned relatively more carbohydrate. But in athletes, fat oxidation did not differ by sex (Cano et al., 2022). Sex hormones and differences in adrenergic response were the main proposed reasons. Fuelling advice for female cyclists should not be lowered on the idea that women "run on fat". Carbohydrate needs for hard and long rides still apply.

### Training response: similar direction, possibly slightly smaller VO2max gains
**Evidence: Limited**
A meta-analysis of 8 studies (175 people) found that men gained more VO2max than women from the same endurance training dose, about 1.95 mL/kg/min more in relative terms (Diaz-Canestro & Montero, 2019). The sample is small and mostly untrained people. There is no good evidence that women need a different intensity distribution, different interval types, or different periodisation. Evidence on sex differences in recovery time between hard sessions is thin, so the app should use each rider's own response data rather than sex based defaults.

### Menstrual cycle phase has a trivial average effect on performance
**Evidence: Moderate (for the small average effect); Limited (for individual effects)**
A meta-analysis of 78 studies (1,193 women) found performance might be trivially lower in the early follicular phase (around menstruation) than in other phases, with an effect size of about minus 0.06. Large differences between studies and poor study quality (42% rated low and 26% very low) meant no general guidelines could be made. The authors recommend a personalised approach (McNulty et al., 2020). Individual women may still have real symptom driven dips, such as cramps, heavy bleeding, poor sleep, or gut upset.

### Cycle phase based training is not supported by current evidence
**Evidence: Limited (the evidence base itself is weak)**
An umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses found highly variable, methodologically weak findings and concluded it is premature to say short term hormone changes meaningfully affect performance or adaptation to resistance training. Programming training by cycle phase is not evidence based (Colenso-Semple et al., 2023). Endurance specific data are even fewer. Popular advice to put all hard work in the follicular phase is a hypothesis, not a finding. Tracking the cycle is still useful for spotting missing or irregular periods (a health flag) and for linking symptoms to days.

### Hormonal contraception: at most a trivial effect on performance
**Evidence: Moderate (oral pill); Limited (other forms)**
A meta-analysis of 42 studies found pill users might perform slightly worse on average than naturally menstruating women, but any effect was most likely trivial and varied between studies. Performance was consistent across the pill cycle, including the pill free days. The authors say the evidence does not support general advice to use or avoid the pill for performance (Elliott-Sale et al., 2020). In 430 elite athletes, about half were using hormonal contraception, mostly the combined pill. Negative side effects were reported more often with progestin only methods, especially the implant (Martin et al., 2018). Contraception choice is a medical decision; the coach should never advise starting, stopping, or changing it. Note that the pill causes a withdrawal bleed, not a true period, so it can hide missing periods caused by low energy availability.

### Low energy availability and REDs affect women and men
**Evidence: Strong (that it causes harm); Moderate (thresholds and screening)**
The IOC first defined Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport in 2014 as a broader replacement for the Female Athlete Triad, noting that it affects many body systems and also male athletes (Mountjoy et al., 2014). The 2018 update listed impairments in metabolic rate, menstrual function, bone health, immunity, protein synthesis, and heart health (Mountjoy et al., 2018). The 2023 update treats low energy availability as a spectrum from adaptable (short, mild) to problematic (long or severe). It reports estimated prevalence of LEA or REDs indicators of 23% to 79.5% in females and 15% to 70% in males. It no longer treats 30 kcal per kg fat free mass per day as a firm clinical cutoff, because that figure came from short lab studies in a few sedentary women and real thresholds vary a lot between people (Mountjoy et al., 2023). Low carbohydrate availability magnifies the harm. REDs and overtraining syndrome share many symptoms, and the 2023 statement says low energy availability should be ruled out before diagnosing overtraining.

The 2023 clinical tool (REDs CAT2) uses indicators such as: missing 3 to 11 periods in a row (primary indicator), missing 12 or more (severe), cycles longer than 35 days (secondary), low testosterone in men, low T3, bone stress injuries, low bone density, and disordered eating scores (Mountjoy et al., 2023). Endurance cycling is a risk sport because of long rides, weight sensitive climbing, and the bike's lack of bone loading. In 50 competitive male road cyclists, 28% had low energy availability on a questionnaire and interview, and 44% had low lumbar spine bone density. Energy availability was the strongest predictor of spine bone density (Keay et al., 2018).

### Iron deficiency is common in female endurance athletes
**Evidence: Moderate**
Female and endurance athletes are commonly diagnosed with iron deficiency. Causes include menstrual losses, low intake, altitude, and a rise in the iron regulating hormone hepcidin after exercise (Sim et al., 2019). Heavy menstrual bleeding is a major risk factor and was reported by about 1 in 3 athletes in one survey cited by Pedlar et al. (2018). Iron deficiency runs in stages: low stores (low ferritin) first, then early changes in red cell markers, then anaemia. There is no agreed ferritin cutoff; studies have used values from 7 to 40 µg/L, and ferritin can be falsely raised by illness or inflammation (Pedlar et al., 2018). In iron depleted women (ferritin 25 µg/L or less), a single 60 mg iron dose on alternate days was absorbed better than daily dosing, because daily doses raise hepcidin (Stoffel et al., 2017). This was a small study, not in athletes. Iron overload is possible with unneeded supplements, so testing should come before supplementing.

### Pregnancy: exercise is safe and helpful for most, under medical guidance
**Evidence: Moderate (guideline level, mostly observational data)**
Without obstetric or medical complications, physical activity in pregnancy is safe and desirable. Women who were active or did vigorous exercise before pregnancy can usually continue, with a target of at least 150 minutes per week of moderate activity. Stationary cycling is listed as a suitable activity, and activities with a risk of abdominal trauma or falls should be avoided (ACOG, 2020). The Canadian guideline also recommends at least 150 minutes per week of moderate activity over at least 3 days and supports combining aerobic and resistance training (Mottola et al., 2018). Data on elite level training loads during pregnancy are limited.

### Postpartum return: gradual, symptom led, clinician guided
**Evidence: Limited**
ACOG (2020) supports resuming activity after birth as medically appropriate. UK physiotherapy guidance on returning to running, the closest high impact model, suggests waiting until around 12 weeks postnatal and passing a set of load and pelvic floor tests first, and warns about urinary incontinence, abdominal separation, pelvic organ prolapse, and pain. The authors stress it is a guide for clinical reasoning, not a protocol (Goom et al., 2019). Cycling puts less impact on the pelvic floor than running but involves saddle pressure and, outdoors, fall risk. Breastfeeding and sleep loss raise energy and recovery needs, which raises REDs risk.

### Perimenopause and menopause: protect muscle and bone, expect symptom variability
**Evidence: Moderate (muscle and bone); Limited (performance and recovery)**
In 234 Finnish women followed from perimenopause to early postmenopause, lean and muscle mass fell, and menopause status predicted the loss. Physical activity was linked to keeping more muscle (Juppi et al., 2020). Road cycling alone does not seem to build bone, and adult road cyclists often have low spine bone density (Olmedillas et al., 2012). In postmenopausal women with low bone mass, 8 months of supervised high intensity resistance and impact training twice a week (5 sets of 5 at above 85% of 1RM) raised spine bone density by about 2.9% versus a drop in controls, without excess harm (Watson et al., 2018). This was supervised training in one trial.

Hot flashes affect about 50% to 80% of women around menopause and come from changes in the brain's temperature control as oestradiol falls (Gombert-Labedens et al., 2025). Whether exercise reduces hot flashes is disputed. A randomised trial of 261 women found exercise did not reduce hot flashes or night sweats (Daley et al., 2015), while a later narrative review found most trials showed some reduction in self reported hot flashes and warned that sudden jumps in intensity may make them feel worse (Witkowski et al., 2023). Direct research on how menopause changes recovery, heat tolerance in sport, or the best training mix for female masters cyclists is scarce. Hormone therapy is a medical decision.

## Rules for the coach
- Never diagnose. For any health flag below, tell the rider to see a doctor, and lower training stress until they do.
- Do not plan training by cycle phase by default. Offer optional cycle and symptom tracking. If a rider logs symptoms (cramps, heavy bleeding, poor sleep, gut upset) rated moderate or worse, allow swapping that day's hard session for an easy ride or rest, then move the hard session 1 to 3 days later.
- If a rider sees a repeatable pattern over at least 3 cycles (for example, worse interval quality on days 1 to 2), let her choose to plan lighter days there. This is personal preference, not science.
- Refer to a doctor if a rider not on hormonal contraception, not pregnant, and not postmenopausal reports: no period for 3 months or more, cycles regularly longer than 35 days, or a big change from her usual pattern. Treat this as a possible REDs sign, not as normal for athletes.
- Refer if a rider describes her periods as very heavy (common lay examples are soaking through protection every 1 to 2 hours, or bleeding for more than 7 days; these examples are general clinical rules of thumb, not from the sources below), since heavy bleeding raises iron deficiency risk (Pedlar et al., 2018).
- Never advise starting, stopping, or changing contraception or hormone therapy.
- REDs screening triggers, for any sex: unplanned weight loss, a stress fracture, repeated illness, falling performance with high fatigue, low libido, mood changes, or strong anxiety about food or weight. On any trigger, stop planned increases in load, hold or reduce volume, prompt a fuelling review, and recommend a doctor or sports dietitian.
- Never set weight loss goals with a large calorie deficit during heavy training blocks. If a rider wants to lose weight, cap the target at a slow rate (about 0.5 kg per week at most, a coaching convention rather than a tested threshold) and keep carbohydrate around hard sessions, since low carbohydrate availability adds to REDs risk (Mountjoy et al., 2023).
- For female cyclists who report fatigue, breathlessness, or falling performance without a training reason, suggest asking a doctor about iron status (ferritin and haemoglobin). Do not suggest iron supplements without a blood test. If the doctor prescribes iron, the rider can ask about alternate day morning dosing.
- Pregnancy: plan nothing beyond what the rider's clinician has approved. Default to indoor riding at moderate intensity (able to talk), at least 150 minutes per week if approved. Tell the rider to stop and contact her clinician for any warning sign they have given her or any new symptom such as bleeding, fluid loss, chest pain, dizziness, or painful contractions (a general safety list, not quoted from the guidelines; the guidelines themselves list the full warning signs).
- Postpartum: require clinician clearance before structured training. Start with easy riding, add intensity only after several weeks of pain free easy volume. Flag leakage, pelvic heaviness, or pain for pelvic health physiotherapy.
- Perimenopause and later: include 2 strength sessions per week, progressing toward heavy loads (sets of about 5 reps) where the rider has no contraindications and good technique, and add some impact work (jumps or brisk walking) for bone. Riders with known osteoporosis need clinician approval for heavy or impact work.
- For riders reporting hot flashes or night sweats, check sleep and recovery scores more closely, allow cooler, indoor, or earlier sessions, and avoid sudden big jumps in intensity.
- Use each rider's own test data and response to training. Do not apply sex based multipliers to zones, ramp rates, or recovery days.

## Where evidence is thin or disputed
- Nearly all training load, recovery, and ramp rate guidance was built on men and is extrapolated to women.
- Whether any woman benefits from phase based training. Studies rarely confirmed cycle phase with hormone tests, and endurance training studies are very few.
- Effects of contraceptives other than the combined pill (implants, hormonal coils, injections) on training and performance.
- Sex differences in recovery time between hard sessions.
- The right ferritin cutoff for athletes; studies range from 7 to 40 µg/L.
- The energy availability level at which harm starts; the 30 kcal per kg fat free mass figure is no longer treated as a clinical cutoff.
- Whether exercise reduces hot flashes (one RCT says no, a narrative review says it may help).
- How perimenopause and menopause change recovery, heat tolerance, and training response in trained cyclists. Very little direct research.
- Safe training loads for elite level athletes during pregnancy, and best timelines for postpartum return to hard cycling.
